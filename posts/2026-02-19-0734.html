Analytic Number Theory: The Music of the Primes

analytic-number-theory

Motivation

How are the prime numbers distributed among the integers? This question, which captivated mathematicians for millennia, gave birth to analytic number theory. By using tools from complex analysis—contour integrals, Dirichlet series, and the Riemann zeta function—mathematicians have uncovered deep connections between the discrete world of integers and the continuous world of analysis. The prime number theorem, proved independently by Hadamard and de la Vallée Poussin in 1896, showed that the number of primes up to \(x\) is asymptotically \(x/\log x\). The Riemann Hypothesis, perhaps the most famous unsolved problem in mathematics, predicts that all non-trivial zeros of the zeta function lie on the critical line \(\text{Re}(s) = 1/2\).

Definitions

  • Riemann Zeta Function: For \(\text{Re}(s) > 1\): \(\zeta(s) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n^s} = \prod_p \frac{1}{1-p^{-s}}\) (Euler product).
  • Prime Counting Function: \(\pi(x) = \#\{p \leq x : p \text{ prime}\}\).
  • Chebyshev Functions: \(\vartheta(x) = \sum_{p \leq x} \log p\) and \(\psi(x) = \sum_{n \leq x} \Lambda(n)\) where \(\Lambda(n)\) is the von Mangoldt function (\(\log p\) if \(n = p^k\), 0 otherwise).
  • Dirichlet Character: A completely multiplicative function \(\chi: \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{C}\) that is periodic with period \(q\) and satisfies \(\chi(n) = 0\) if \(\gcd(n,q) > 1\).
  • Dirichlet L-function: \(L(s, \chi) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{\chi(n)}{n^s}\) for \(\text{Re}(s) > 1\).

Theorem: The Prime Number Theorem

Theorem (Prime Number Theorem): As \(x \to \infty\):

\[\pi(x) \sim \frac{x}{\log x}\]

Equivalently, \(\pi(x) \sim \text{Li}(x)\) where \(\text{Li}(x) = \int_2^x \frac{dt}{\log t}\) is the logarithmic integral.

Error Term: Under the Riemann Hypothesis: \(\pi(x) = \text{Li}(x) + O(\sqrt{x} \log x)\).

Dirichlet's Theorem: If \(\gcd(a,q) = 1\), there are infinitely many primes \(p \equiv a \pmod{q}\).

Proof Sketch (Newman's Tauberian Approach)

Newman's 1980 proof uses contour integration and a Tauberian theorem. Define \(\Phi(s) = \sum_p \frac{\log p}{p^s}\). The key steps are:

  1. Show \(\zeta(s)\) has a simple pole at \(s = 1\) and no zeros on \(\text{Re}(s) = 1\).
  2. Prove \(-\frac{\zeta'(s)}{\zeta(s)} = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{\Lambda(n)}{n^s}\) has a simple pole at \(s = 1\) with residue 1.
  3. Apply Newman's Tauberian theorem: if \(f(t)\) is bounded and \(\int_0^{\infty} f(t)e^{-st}dt \to A\) as \(s \to 0^+\), then \(\int_0^T f(t)dt \sim AT\).
  4. Conclude \(\psi(x) \sim x\), which is equivalent to \(\pi(x) \sim x/\log x\).

Notation Guide

  • \(s = \sigma + it\): Complex variable
  • \(\zeta(s)\): Riemann zeta function
  • \(\pi(x)\): Prime counting function
  • \(\Lambda(n)\): von Mangoldt function
  • \(\psi(x)\): Second Chebyshev function
  • \(\text{Li}(x)\): Logarithmic integral
  • \(L(s, \chi)\): Dirichlet L-function
  • \(\varphi(q)\): Euler's totient function

Enumerative Geometry: Counting Curves

enumerative-geometry

Motivation

How many conics pass through 5 general points in the plane? How many lines lie on a cubic surface? These are questions of enumerative geometry, which seeks to count geometric objects satisfying given conditions. The field has roots in the 19th century work of Schubert, who developed "calculus of conditions" to solve such problems systematically. Modern enumerative geometry, revolutionized by string theory and mirror symmetry in the 1990s, connects to Gromov-Witten theory, moduli spaces, and integrable systems. The famous number 2875 counts lines on a general quintic threefold—this single number launched a thousand papers in mathematical physics.

Definitions

  • Chow Ring: For a smooth projective variety \(X\), \(A^*(X)\) is the ring of algebraic cycles modulo rational equivalence, graded by codimension.
  • Moduli Space of Curves: \(\overline{M}_{g,n}\) parametrizes stable curves of genus \(g\) with \(n\) marked points.
  • Gromov-Witten Invariants: \(\langle \tau_{a_1}(\gamma_1) \cdots \tau_{a_n}(\gamma_n) \rangle_{g,\beta}\) counts stable maps from genus \(g\) curves to \(X\) representing class \(\beta\), with incidence conditions.
  • Quantum Cohomology: A deformation of the cohomology ring where structure constants are Gromov-Witten invariants.
  • Schubert Calculus: The study of intersection theory on Grassmannians, using Schubert cycles as a basis.

Theorem: The Five Conic Problem

Theorem: There are exactly 3264 conics tangent to 5 given conics in general position in \(\mathbb{P}^2\).

Theorem (Cayley-Salmon): A smooth cubic surface in \(\mathbb{P}^3\) contains exactly 27 lines.

Theorem (Schubert): The number of twisted cubics meeting 12 lines in general position in \(\mathbb{P}^3\) is 5,819,539,783,680.

Theorem (Kontsevich's Formula): For \(\mathbb{P}^2\), let \(N_d\) be the number of degree \(d\) rational curves through \(3d-1\) points. Then:

\[N_d = \sum_{d_1 + d_2 = d} N_{d_1} N_{d_2} \left[ d_1^2 d_2^2 \binom{3d-4}{3d_1-2} - d_1^3 d_2 \binom{3d-4}{3d_1-1} \right]\]

Proof Sketch (27 Lines on Cubic Surface)

The proof uses the incidence correspondence. Let \(F\) be the space of cubic surfaces (19-dimensional projective space) and \(G(1,3)\) the Grassmannian of lines in \(\mathbb{P}^3\). Consider:

\[I = \{(S, L) \in F \times G(1,3) : L \subset S\}\]

The projection to \(G(1,3)\) has fibers of dimension 15 (cubics containing a fixed line). Thus \(\dim(I) = 19 = \dim(F)\). One shows the projection to \(F\) is generically finite of degree 27. The number 27 appears because the Weyl group of type \(E_6\) has order 51,840 = 27 × 1920, reflecting the deep connection to root systems.

Notation Guide

  • \(\mathbb{P}^n\): Complex projective n-space
  • \(G(k,n)\): Grassmannian of k-planes in n-space
  • \(\overline{M}_{g,n}(X, \beta)\): Moduli of stable maps
  • \(\sigma_\lambda\): Schubert cycle indexed by partition \(\lambda\)
  • \(N_d\): Gromov-Witten invariant for degree \(d\) curves in \(\mathbb{P}^2\)
  • \(\tau_a(\gamma)\): Descendant insertion
  • \(QH^*(X)\): Quantum cohomology ring

    """
    Compute N_d (number of degree d rational curves through 3d-1 points in P²)

        N[d] = total
    
    return N

Derived Algebraic Geometry: Beyond Schemes

derived-algebraic-geometry

Motivation

What happens when we allow functions to have "higher homotopical" information? Derived algebraic geometry, developed by Toën, Vezzosi, and Lurie in the 2000s, extends classical algebraic geometry by replacing commutative rings with simplicial commutative rings or \(\mathbb{E}_\infty\)-ring spectra. This seemingly technical generalization solves fundamental problems: it makes fiber products of schemes behave correctly, provides the right framework for deformation theory, and gives a rigorous foundation for the Batalin-Vilkovisky quantization in physics. The key insight is that derived structures capture "derived intersections" and higher-order information invisible to classical geometry.

Definitions

  • Simplicial Commutative Ring: A functor \(\Delta^{op} \to \text{CRing}\), or equivalently a commutative monoid in simplicial abelian groups.
  • Derived Affine Scheme: \(\text{Spec}(A)\) where \(A\) is a simplicial commutative ring. The underlying topological space is \(\text{Spec}(\pi_0(A))\), but the structure sheaf is a sheaf of simplicial rings.
  • Cotangent Complex: For a ring map \(R \to S\), \(\mathbb{L}_{S/R}\) is an object of \(D(S)\) (derived category) measuring the "derived" Kähler differentials.
  • Derived Fiber Product: For \(X \to Z \leftarrow Y\), the derived fiber product \(X \times_Z^h Y\) remembers Tor information.
  • \(\infty\)-Category: A category enriched in simplicial sets, where morphisms have higher homotopical structure.

Theorem: Derived Base Change and Deformation Theory

Theorem (Derived Base Change): In derived algebraic geometry, fiber products are well-behaved. For a derived Cartesian square:

\[\begin{array}{ccc} X' & \to & X \\ \downarrow & & \downarrow \\ Y' & \to & Y \end{array}\]

we have \(\mathbb{L}_{X'/Y'} \cong f^* \mathbb{L}_{X/Y}\) in the derived category.

Theorem (Lurie): The cotangent complex controls deformations. For a derived scheme \(X\), first-order deformations over \(\text{Spec}(k[\epsilon]/\epsilon^2)\) are classified by \(\text{Ext}^1(\mathbb{L}_{X}, \mathcal{O}_X)\).

Proof Sketch (Derived Intersections)

Consider two subvarieties \(Y, Z \subset X\). The classical intersection \(Y \cap Z\) may have the "wrong" dimension. The derived intersection is:

\[Y \times_X^h Z = \text{Spec}\left(\mathcal{O}_Y \otimes_{\mathcal{O}_X}^L \mathcal{O}_Z\right)\]

The structure sheaf is the derived tensor product, with Tor terms \(\text{Tor}_i^{\mathcal{O}_X}(\mathcal{O}_Y, \mathcal{O}_Z)\) giving higher structure. When \(Y\) and \(Z\) intersect transversally, the derived intersection equals the classical one. In general, the virtual fundamental class \([Y \cap Z]^{vir}\) is constructed from the derived structure.

Notation Guide

  • \(s\text{CRing}\): Category of simplicial commutative rings
  • \(d\text{Aff}\): Category of derived affine schemes
  • \(\mathbb{L}_{X/Y}\): Cotangent complex
  • \(\otimes^L\): Derived tensor product
  • \(D(X)\): Derived category of quasi-coherent sheaves
  • \(\pi_i(A)\): Homotopy groups of a simplicial ring
  • \(\mathbb{E}_\infty\): Highly structured commutative ring spectrum

Neural ODEs: Deep Learning Meets Differential Equations

neural-odes

Motivation

What if we viewed deep neural networks as discretizations of continuous dynamical systems? Neural Ordinary Differential Equations (Neural ODEs), introduced by Chen et al. in 2018, replace the discrete layers of residual networks with a continuous-time ODE governed by a neural network. This perspective offers several advantages: constant memory cost (using the adjoint method), adaptive computation time, and a natural framework for modeling continuous-time phenomena. Neural ODEs have found applications in time series modeling, generative modeling (normalizing flows), and physics-informed machine learning. They represent a beautiful convergence of deep learning and numerical analysis.

Definitions

  • Neural ODE: A model defined by \(\frac{dh(t)}{dt} = f(h(t), t, \theta)\) where \(f\) is a neural network with parameters \(\theta\).
  • Adjoint State: \(a(t) = \frac{\partial L}{\partial h(t)}\), the gradient of the loss with respect to the hidden state.
  • Adjoint Method: Computes gradients by solving a backward ODE: \(\frac{da(t)}{dt} = -a(t)^T \frac{\partial f}{\partial h}\).
  • Normalizing Flow: A generative model using invertible transformations where the log-likelihood is tractable via the change of variables formula.
  • Continuous Normalizing Flow (CNF): A normalizing flow defined by a Neural ODE with the instantaneous change of variables formula.

Theorem: Adjoint Sensitivity Method

Theorem: For a Neural ODE with loss \(L(h(t_1))\) depending on the terminal state, the gradient with respect to parameters can be computed by solving:

\[\frac{dh(t)}{dt} = f(h(t), t, \theta), \quad h(t_0) = h_0\]

\[\frac{da(t)}{dt} = -a(t)^T \frac{\partial f(h(t), t, \theta)}{\partial h}, \quad a(t_1) = \frac{\partial L}{\partial h(t_1)}\]

\[\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial \theta}\right) = -a(t)^T \frac{\partial f(h(t), t, \theta)}{\partial \theta}\]

Instantaneous Change of Variables: For CNFs, the log-probability evolves as:

\[\frac{\partial \log p(h(t))}{\partial t} = -\text{tr}\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial h}\right)\]

Proof Sketch (Adjoint Method)

The key insight is that we don't need to store all intermediate states. Define the adjoint \(a(t) = \partial L/\partial h(t)\). By the chain rule:

\[\frac{da(t)}{dt} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial h(t)} \cdot \frac{\partial h(t)}{\partial h(t+\epsilon)} \cdot \frac{\partial h(t+\epsilon)}{\partial t} \cdot \frac{dt}{d\epsilon}\]

Using \(\frac{\partial h(t+\epsilon)}{\partial h(t)} \approx I + \epsilon \frac{\partial f}{\partial h}\) and taking \(\epsilon \to 0\) yields the adjoint ODE. The gradient \(\partial L/\partial \theta\) is computed by augmenting the system with the parameter gradient dynamics.

Notation Guide

  • \(h(t)\): Hidden state at "time" \(t\)
  • \(f(h, t, \theta)\): Neural network dynamics
  • \(a(t)\): Adjoint (co-state)
  • \(\text{ODESolve}(f, h_0, t_0, t_1)\): Numerical ODE solver
  • \(\text{tr}(J)\): Trace of Jacobian for CNF
  • \(p_t(x)\): Probability density at time \(t\)
  • \(\phi_t\): Flow map from time 0 to \(t\)

import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import solve_ivp
from scipy.linalg import expm

class NeuralODE:
    """
    Simplified Neural ODE implementation.
    In practice, use torchdiffeq or jax-based libraries.
    """
    def __init__(self, hidden_dim):
        self.hidden_dim = hidden_dim
        # Simple dynamics: dh/dt = tanh(Wh + b)
        self.W = np.random.randn(hidden_dim, hidden_dim) * 0.1
        self.b = np.zeros(hidden_dim)
    
    def f(self, t, h):
        """Dynamics function."""
        return np.tanh(self.W @ h + self.b)
    
    def forward(self, h0, t_span=(0, 1)):
        """Forward pass: integrate from t=0 to t=1."""
        sol = solve_ivp(self.f, t_span, h0, dense_output=True)
        return sol.y[:, -1], sol
    
    def adjoint_sensitivity(self, h0, dL_dh1, t_span=(0, 1)):
        """
        Compute gradients using adjoint method.
        Returns dL/dW and dL/db.
        """
        # Forward pass
        h1, sol = self.forward(h0, t_span)
        
        # Backward: solve adjoint ODE
        def adjoint_dynamics(t, a):
            h = sol.sol(t)
            # da/dt = -a^T @ ∂f/∂h
            # ∂f/∂h = (1 - tanh²(Wh+b)) * W
            z = self.W @ h + self.b
            sech2 = 1 - np.tanh(z)**2
            df_dh = (sech2[:, None] * self.W).T
            return -df_dh @ a
        
        # Solve backward from t=1 to t=0
        a_sol = solve_ivp(adjoint_dynamics, [t_span[1], t_span[0]], dL_dh1)
        
        return a_sol.y[:, -1]

Causal Inference: From Correlation to Causation

causal-inference

Motivation

Does smoking cause lung cancer? Would a particular policy reduce unemployment? These causal questions cannot be answered by standard statistical methods that only reveal associations. Causal inference, formalized by Judea Pearl's do-calculus and Donald Rubin's potential outcomes framework, provides mathematical tools for reasoning about cause and effect from observational data. The field has revolutionized epidemiology, economics, and social science, enabling researchers to estimate causal effects even when randomized experiments are impossible. The key insight is that causality requires assumptions beyond data—represented through causal diagrams or structural equations.

Definitions

  • Causal DAG: A directed acyclic graph where nodes represent variables and edges represent direct causal effects.
  • do-Operator: \(\text{do}(X = x)\) represents an intervention that sets variable \(X\) to value \(x\), breaking incoming causal arrows.
  • Potential Outcomes: For treatment \(T\), \(Y(1)\) and \(Y(0)\) are the outcomes that would be observed under treatment and control, respectively.
  • Average Treatment Effect (ATE): \(\text{ATE} = \mathbb{E}[Y(1) - Y(0)]\).
  • Confounder: A variable that affects both treatment and outcome, creating spurious associations.
  • Back-Door Criterion: A set of variables \(Z\) satisfies the back-door criterion if it blocks all back-door paths from treatment to outcome without opening new paths.

Theorem: The Back-Door and Front-Door Criteria

Theorem (Back-Door Criterion): If a set of variables \(Z\) satisfies the back-door criterion relative to \((X, Y)\), then:

\[P(y | \text{do}(x)) = \sum_z P(y | x, z) P(z)\]

Theorem (Front-Door Criterion): If a set of variables \(Z\) satisfies:

  1. \(Z\) intercepts all directed paths from \(X\) to \(Y\)
  2. There is no back-door path from \(X\) to \(Z\)
  3. All back-door paths from \(Z\) to \(Y\) are blocked by \(X\)
then \(P(y | \text{do}(x))\) is identifiable.

Adjustment Formula: \[P(y | \text{do}(x)) = \sum_z P(z | x) \sum_{x'} P(y | x', z) P(x')\]

Proof Sketch (Back-Door Adjustment)

The key observation is that conditioning on \(Z\) blocks confounding paths. By the rules of do-calculus:

\[P(y | \text{do}(x)) = \sum_z P(y | \text{do}(x), z) P(z | \text{do}(x))\]

Since \(Z\) blocks back-door paths, \(Y \perp\!\!\!\perp X | Z\) in the mutilated graph, giving \(P(y | \text{do}(x), z) = P(y | x, z)\). Also, \(Z\) is not a descendant of \(X\), so \(P(z | \text{do}(x)) = P(z)\). Combining yields the adjustment formula.

Notation Guide

  • \(G\): Causal DAG
  • \(G_{\overline{X}}\): Graph with incoming edges to \(X\) removed
  • \(G_{\underline{X}}\): Graph with outgoing edges from \(X\) removed
  • \(Y(t)\): Potential outcome under treatment \(t\)
  • \(T \perp\!\!\!\perp Y(0), Y(1)\): Treatment ignorability/exogeneity
  • \(\text{ATE}\): Average treatment effect
  • \(\text{CATE}(x)\): Conditional average treatment effect

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

class CausalInference:
    """
    Demonstration of causal inference concepts.
    """
    
    @staticmethod
    def generate_confounded_data(n=10000, seed=42):
        """
        Generate data with confounding:
        Z → X, Z → Y (confounder)
        X → Y (causal effect)
        """
        np.random.seed(seed)
        Z = np.random.normal(0, 1, n)  # Confounder
        X = 0.5 * Z + np.random.normal(0, 0.5, n)  # Treatment
        Y = 2.0 * X + 1.5 * Z + np.random.normal(0, 0.3, n)  # Outcome
        # True ATE = 2.0
        return X, Y, Z
    
    @staticmethod
    def naive_estimate(X, Y):
        """Naive regression (confounded)."""
        slope, intercept, r, p, se = stats.linregress(X, Y)
        return slope
    
    @staticmethod
    def backdoor_adjustment(X, Y, Z):
        """
        Back-door adjustment: E[Y|do(X)] = Σ_z E[Y|X,z] P(z)
        Using linear regression for E[Y|X,Z].
        """
        # Fit Y ~ X + Z
        A = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(X)), X, Z])
        beta = np.linalg.lstsq(A, Y, rcond=None)[0]
        
        # E[Y|X=x,Z] = beta_0 + beta_1*x + beta_2*Z
        # E[Y|do(X=x)] = E_Z[E[Y|X=x,Z]] = beta_0 + beta_1*x + beta_2*E[Z]
        # Since E[Z] = 0, the ATE is just beta_1
        return beta[1]  # Coefficient of X
    
    @staticmethod
    def inverse_probability_weighting(X, Y, Z):
        """
        IPW estimator for ATE.
        """
        # Estimate propensity score e(Z) = P(X=1|Z)
        # Here X is continuous, so we use a simple model
        A = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(Z)), Z])
        beta_ps = np.linalg.lstsq(A, X, rcond=None)[0]
        e_Z = A @ beta_ps
        
        # IPW weights
        # For continuous treatment, we use a stabilized approach
        weights = 1.0 / (e_Z - e_Z.mean() + X.mean())
        weights = np.clip(weights, 0.1, 10)  # Stabilize
        
        # Weighted regression
        W = np.diag(weights)
        A = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(X)), X])
        beta = np.linalg.lstsq(A.T @ W @ A, A.T @ W @ Y, rcond=None)[0]
        return beta[1]

Mathematical Oncology: Modeling Cancer Dynamics

mathematical-oncology

Motivation

How do tumors grow, evolve, and respond to treatment? Mathematical oncology applies differential equations, stochastic processes, and evolutionary game theory to understand cancer as a complex dynamical system. Models range from simple exponential growth laws to sophisticated agent-based simulations of tumor microenvironments. These models help predict treatment outcomes, optimize therapy schedules, and understand mechanisms of resistance. The field exemplifies how abstract mathematics can directly impact human health, with models informing clinical trials and personalized treatment decisions. Key insights include the importance of tumor heterogeneity, the role of the immune system, and the evolutionary nature of therapeutic resistance.

Definitions

  • Tumor Growth Models: Exponential \((N(t) = N_0 e^{rt})\), logistic \((\frac{dN}{dt} = rN(1-\frac{N}{K}))\), Gompertz \((\frac{dN}{dt} = -rN \log(\frac{N}{K}))\).
  • Pharmacokinetics (PK): Modeling drug concentration over time, typically using compartment models.
  • Pharmacodynamics (PD): Modeling drug effect on tumor cells, often via the Emax model: \(E = E_{max} \frac{C^n}{EC_{50}^n + C^n}\).
  • Clonal Evolution: Tumors evolve through mutation and selection; resistant clones may pre-exist or emerge during treatment.
  • Tumor-Immune Interaction: Modeled by predator-prey dynamics: \(\frac{dT}{dt} = rT(1-\frac{T}{K}) - \frac{aTI}{1+sT}\), \(\frac{dI}{dt} = \frac{eTI}{1+sT} - dI + p\).

Theorem: Norton-Simon Hypothesis and Dose-Density

Theorem (Gompertzian Growth): Many tumors follow Gompertz growth where the growth rate decreases with size:

\[\frac{dN}{dt} = rN \log\left(\frac{K}{N}\right)\]

with solution \(N(t) = K \exp\left(-\log(K/N_0)e^{-rt}\right)\).

Norton-Simon Hypothesis: For Gompertzian tumors, dose-dense scheduling (more frequent, lower doses) is more effective than standard scheduling because:

  1. Small tumors grow faster (exponentially)
  2. Regrowth between cycles is faster when tumors are small
  3. Frequent dosing prevents rapid regrowth

Goldie-Coldman Model: The probability of resistance depends on mutation rate and tumor size. For a tumor with \(N\) cells and mutation rate \(\mu\) per cell per generation, the probability of at least one resistant cell is approximately \(1 - e^{-N\mu}\).

Proof Sketch (Gompertz Solution)

Let \(u = \log(N/K)\), so \(N = Ke^u\). Then:

\[\frac{dN}{dt} = Ke^u \frac{du}{dt} = rKe^u (-u)\]

Thus \(\frac{du}{dt} = -ru\), giving \(u(t) = u(0)e^{-rt}\). Substituting back:

\[N(t) = K \exp\left(\log(N_0/K)e^{-rt}\right) = K \left(\frac{N_0}{K}\right)^{e^{-rt}}\]

Notation Guide

  • \(N(t)\): Tumor cell count at time \(t\)
  • \(r\): Growth rate parameter
  • \(K\): Carrying capacity
  • \(C(t)\): Drug concentration
  • \(EC_{50}\): Concentration for 50% effect
  • \(\mu\): Mutation rate per cell per division
  • \(T\): Tumor cells, \(I\): Immune cells
  • \(R(t)\): Resistant cell fraction

class TumorModels:
    """Mathematical models of tumor growth and treatment."""
    
    @staticmethod
    def gompertz_growth(N, t, r, K):
        """dN/dt = r*N*log(K/N)"""
        return r * N * np.log(K / max(N, 1e-10))
    
    @staticmethod
    def logistic_growth(N, t, r, K):
        """dN/dt = r*N*(1 - N/K)"""
        return r * N * (1 - N / K)
    
    @staticmethod
    def treatment_model(state, t, r, K, a, d, C_max, dose_times):
        """
        Tumor with periodic chemotherapy.
        state = [N] (tumor cells)
        a = drug sensitivity
        d = drug decay rate
        """
        N = state[0]
        
        # Drug concentration (simplified PK)
        C = 0
        for dose_time in dose_times:
            if t >= dose_time:
                C += C_max * np.exp(-d * (t - dose_time))
        
        # Growth with treatment
        growth = r * N * np.log(K / max(N, 1e-10))
        kill = a * C * N
        
        return [growth - kill]
    
    @staticmethod
    def immune_tumor_model(state, t, r, K, a, e, d, s, p):
        """
        Tumor-immune predator-prey model.
        state = [T, I] (tumor cells, immune cells)
        """
        T, I = state
        
        dTdt = r * T * (1 - T/K) - a * T * I / (1 + s * T)
        dIdt = e * T * I / (1 + s * T) - d * I + p
        
        return [dTdt, dIdt]

Mirror Symmetry: When Physics Meets Geometry

mirror-symmetry

Historical Context

Mirror symmetry emerged from string theory in the late 1980s when physicists Candelas, de la Ossa, Green, and Parkes discovered a remarkable phenomenon: Calabi-Yau manifolds come in mirror pairs where complex geometry on one side corresponds to symplectic geometry on the other. Specifically, they predicted that the number of rational curves on a quintic threefold (a difficult enumerative geometry problem) could be computed via period integrals on the mirror manifold. This prediction was stunningly accurate—their formula gave numbers like 2,875, 609,250, and 317,206,375 for curves of degrees 1, 2, and 3, which mathematicians had painstakingly verified. This launched a revolution connecting algebraic geometry, symplectic geometry, and theoretical physics.

Definitions

  • Calabi-Yau Manifold: A compact Kähler manifold with trivial canonical bundle (\(c_1 = 0\)) and vanishing Ricci curvature. In complex dimension 3, these are the compactification spaces for string theory.
  • Hodge Numbers: \(h^{p,q} = \dim H^{p,q}(X)\). For a Calabi-Yau threefold, mirror symmetry predicts \(h^{1,1}(X) = h^{2,1}(X^\vee)\) and \(h^{2,1}(X) = h^{1,1}(X^\vee)\).
  • Yukawa Coupling: On the complex side: \(Y_{ijk} = \int_X \Omega \wedge \frac{\partial^3 \Omega}{\partial z_i \partial z_j \partial z_k}\). On the symplectic side: counts of holomorphic curves.
  • Mirror Map: A coordinate transformation relating the complex moduli of \(X\) to the Kähler moduli of \(X^\vee\).
  • Gromov-Witten Potential: \(F(q) = \sum_{d=0}^{\infty} N_d q^d\) where \(N_d\) counts degree \(d\) curves.

Theorem: Mirror Symmetry for the Quintic

Theorem (Candelas et al., 1991): Let \(X\) be a quintic hypersurface in \(\mathbb{P}^4\) and \(X^\vee\) its mirror. The genus-zero Gromov-Witten invariants \(N_d\) (counts of degree \(d\) rational curves on \(X\)) are determined by the periods of the holomorphic 3-form on \(X^\vee\).

Picard-Fuchs Equation: The periods satisfy:

\[\left(\psi^{-5} D^4 - 5\prod_{j=1}^{4}(5D + j)\right)\varpi = 0\]

where \(D = \psi \frac{d}{d\psi}\) and \(\psi\) is the complex structure parameter.

Mirror Formula: The Yukawa coupling on the quintic is:

\[K_{zzz} = 5 + \sum_{d=1}^{\infty} n_d d^3 \frac{q^d}{1-q^d}\]

where \(n_d\) are the genus-zero Gromov-Witten invariants.

Proof Sketch (Mirror Construction)

The mirror of the quintic is constructed via Greene-Plesser orbifolding. Start with the Fermat quintic:

\[X_\psi = \{x_0^5 + x_1^5 + x_2^5 + x_3^5 + x_4^5 - 5\psi x_0 x_1 x_2 x_3 x_4 = 0\} \subset \mathbb{P}^4\]

The group \((\mathbb{Z}/5)^3\) acts by multiplying coordinates by 5th roots of unity. The quotient \(X_\psi / (\mathbb{Z}/5)^3\) is the mirror. The Picard-Fuchs equation comes from studying how the period integrals \(\int_\gamma \Omega\) vary with \(\psi\). The mirror map relates \(\psi\) to the Kähler parameter \(q = e^{2\pi i t}\).

Notation Guide

  • \(X, X^\vee\): Mirror pair of Calabi-Yau manifolds
  • \(h^{p,q}\): Hodge numbers
  • \(\Omega\): Holomorphic volume form
  • \(\varpi\): Period integral
  • \(\psi\): Complex structure parameter
  • \(q = e^{2\pi i t}\): Kähler parameter
  • \(N_d, n_d\): Gromov-Witten invariants
  • \(K_{zzz}\): Yukawa coupling

Arithmetic Statistics: The Distribution of Number Fields

arithmetic-statistics

Historical Context

Arithmetic statistics is a young field that applies probabilistic methods to number theory. Rather than studying individual number fields or elliptic curves, it asks: what does a "typical" number field look like? How often does the class number equal 1? What is the average rank of elliptic curves? The field gained momentum in the 2000s through the work of Bhargava, who developed powerful geometric methods to count number fields. His spectacular achievements include proving that the average rank of elliptic curves over \(\mathbb{Q}\) is bounded (later shown to be at most 1), and that a positive proportion of elliptic curves have rank 0. These results resolved long-standing conjectures and earned Bhargava the 2014 Fields Medal.

Definitions

  • Number Field: A finite extension \(K/\mathbb{Q}\), of degree \(n = [K:\mathbb{Q}]\).
  • Discriminant: \(D_K\), an integer measuring ramification. The discriminant of a polynomial is the discriminant of its splitting field.
  • Class Number: \(h_K = |\text{Cl}(\mathcal{O}_K)|\), the size of the ideal class group, measuring the failure of unique factorization.
  • Elliptic Curve: A smooth cubic curve \(E: y^2 = x^3 + Ax + B\) with discriminant \(\Delta = -16(4A^3 + 27B^2) \neq 0\).
  • Rank: The rank of the Mordell-Weil group \(E(\mathbb{Q})\), measuring the number of independent rational points.
  • Height: A measure of arithmetic complexity. For an elliptic curve, the naive height is \(H(E) = \max(4|A|^3, 27B^2)\).

Theorem: Bhargava's Results on Elliptic Curves

Theorem (Bhargava-Shankar, 2010-2015): When ordered by height, the average rank of elliptic curves over \(\mathbb{Q}\) satisfies:

\[\lim_{X \to \infty} \frac{\sum_{H(E) \leq X} \text{rank}(E)}{\#\{E : H(E) \leq X\}} \leq 1.5\]

Later improved to average rank \(< 1\).

Theorem (Bhargava-Skinner-Zhang, 2014): At least 66% of elliptic curves over \(\mathbb{Q}\) satisfy the Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture (rank part).

Theorem (Bhargava, 2014): The average size of the 2-Selmer group of elliptic curves is 3.

Theorem (Davenport-Heilbronn, 1971; Bhargava, 2005): The average number of cubic fields with discriminant in \([-X, X]\) is \(\frac{1}{3\zeta(3)}X\).

Proof Sketch (Counting Elliptic Curves)

Bhargava's method uses the representation theory of prehomogeneous vector spaces. An elliptic curve corresponds to a binary quartic form (for 2-Selmer) or a pair of ternary quadratic forms (for 3-Selmer). The key steps are:

  1. Parametrize Selmer elements by integral orbits in a representation \(V\).
  2. Use geometry of numbers to count lattice points in fundamental domains.
  3. Apply a sieve to count locally soluble orbits (Selmer elements).
  4. Relate the average size of Selmer groups to the average rank via exact sequences.

Notation Guide

  • \(K\): Number field
  • \(D_K\): Discriminant of \(K\)
  • \(h_K\): Class number
  • \(\text{Cl}(K)\): Class group
  • \(E(\mathbb{Q})\): Rational points on elliptic curve
  • \(\text{rank}(E)\): Mordell-Weil rank
  • \(\text{Sel}_p(E)\): p-Selmer group
  • \(H(E)\): Height of elliptic curve
  • \(N_n(X)\): Number of degree \(n\) fields with \(|D_K| \leq X\)

import numpy as np
from scipy.special import zeta

class ArithmeticStatistics:
    """
    Computational exploration of arithmetic statistics.
    """
    
    @staticmethod
    def count_quadratic_fields(X):
        """
        Number of quadratic fields with |D| ≤ X.
        Fundamental discriminants: D ≡ 0,1 (mod 4), squarefree.
        """
        # Approximately: N₂(X) ~ X/ζ(2) = 6X/π²
        return int(X / zeta(2))
    
    @staticmethod
    def count_cubic_fields(X):
        """
        Davenport-Heilbronn: N₃(X) ~ X/(3ζ(3))
        """
        return int(X / (3 * zeta(3)))
    
    @staticmethod
    def elliptic_curve_height(A, B):
        """
        Naive height of elliptic curve y² = x³ + Ax + B.
        """
        return max(4 * abs(A)**3, 27 * B**2)
    
    @staticmethod
    def count_elliptic_curves(X):
        """
        Number of elliptic curves with H(E) ≤ X.
        Approximately: c * X^(5/6) for some constant c.
        """
        # Rough estimate
        return int(0.5 * X**(5/6))
    
    @staticmethod
    def average_rank_estimate(X):
        """
        Bhargava-Shankar bound on average rank.
        """
        # As X → ∞, average rank < 1
        return 0.8  # Empirical estimate
    
    @staticmethod
    def selmer_average(p=2):
        """
        Average size of p-Selmer group.
        """
        if p == 2:
            return 3  # Bhargava's theorem
        elif p == 3:

Inter-universal Teichmüller Theory: Anabelian Geometry at the Extremes

Historical Context

In 2012, Shinichi Mochizuki released a series of four papers totaling over 500 pages, claiming a proof of the abc conjecture—a fundamental problem in number theory with implications for Fermat's Last Theorem, the Mordell conjecture, and countless other results. The proof introduced "inter-universal Teichmüller theory" (IUT), a radically new framework combining anabelian geometry, Hodge-Arakelov theory, and Frobenioid theory. The complexity and novelty of IUT made verification extremely difficult. After years of intense scrutiny, a consensus emerged in 2020-2021 that a gap remained in the proof, specifically in Corollary 3.12 of IUT-3. While Mochizuki maintains the correctness of his work, the mathematical community has not accepted the proof as complete. IUT remains one of the most controversial and fascinating developments in modern mathematics.

Definitions

  • abc Conjecture: For \(\epsilon > 0\), there exists \(C_\epsilon\) such that for coprime integers \(a, b, c\) with \(a + b = c\): \[c < C_\epsilon \cdot \text{rad}(abc)^{1+\epsilon}\] where \(\text{rad}(n)\) is the product of distinct prime factors.
  • Anabelian Geometry: The study of how much information about a variety is encoded in its étale fundamental group. Grothendieck's anabelian conjecture suggests that for certain "anabelian" varieties, the geometry is determined by the algebraic fundamental group.
  • Frobenioid: A category-theoretic structure introduced by Mochizuki to encode arithmetic information about number fields, including divisor monoids and Galois actions.
  • Hodge-Arakelov Theory: Mochizuki's earlier work comparing the de Rham and étale cohomology of elliptic curves over number fields.
  • Inter-universal Link: A correspondence between different "universes" of Frobenioids, allowing comparison of volumes/degrees across distinct arithmetic settings.

Theorem: The abc Conjecture and Its Implications

Conjecture (abc Conjecture, Masser-Oesterlé, 1985): For every \(\epsilon > 0\), there exists \(C_\epsilon > 0\) such that for all coprime positive integers \(a, b, c\) with \(a + b = c\):

\[c < C_\epsilon \cdot \text{rad}(abc)^{1+\epsilon}\]

Implication (Fermat's Last Theorem): The abc conjecture implies that for sufficiently large \(n\), there are no nontrivial integer solutions to \(x^n + y^n = z^n\).

Implication (Mordell Conjecture): The abc conjecture implies the Mordell conjecture (Faltings' theorem) that curves of genus \(\geq 2\) have finitely many rational points.

Implication (Roth's Theorem): The abc conjecture implies Roth's theorem on Diophantine approximation.

Claimed Theorem (Mochizuki, 2012): Inter-universal Teichmüller theory proves the abc conjecture.

Proof Sketch (IUT Strategy)

The IUT approach involves:

  1. Initial Theta Data: Construct a collection of elliptic curves over number fields with specific properties.
  2. Hodge Theaters: Build two parallel "theaters" (Frobenioid-theoretic structures) encoding different aspects of the arithmetic.
  3. Log-links: Establish correspondences between theaters using logarithmic transformations.
  4. Theta-links: Create inter-universal links comparing volumes between theaters.
  5. Volume Computations: Use the links to compare degrees of line bundles, leading to inequalities involving heights.
  6. Conclusion: Derive the abc inequality from these volume comparisons.

The critical issue concerns whether certain volume comparisons are valid across the inter-universal link.

Notation Guide

  • \(\text{rad}(n) = \prod_{p|n} p\): Radical of \(n\)
  • \(K\): Number field
  • \(\mathcal{O}_K\): Ring of integers
  • \(\pi_1^{\text{ét}}(X)\): Étale fundamental group
  • \(\mathcal{F}\): Frobenioid
  • \(\mathcal{D}\): Divisor monoid
  • \(\Theta_{\text{gau}}\): Gaussian theta function
  • \(\text{log}(\mathcal{F})\): Logarithmic Frobenioid

Quantum Topology: Knots, 3-Manifolds, and Quantum Field Theory

quantum-topology

Historical Context

Quantum topology emerged in the 1980s when Vaughan Jones discovered his polynomial knot invariant using operator algebras—a breakthrough that earned him the 1990 Fields Medal. The discovery revealed deep connections between knot theory, statistical mechanics, and quantum field theory. Witten then showed that the Jones polynomial could be understood via Chern-Simons theory, a topological quantum field theory (TQFT). This sparked the development of quantum invariants of 3-manifolds, including the Reshetikhin-Turaev invariants and the Turaev-Viro invariants. More recently, the volume conjecture relates quantum invariants to hyperbolic geometry, suggesting that quantum topology encodes classical geometric information. The field continues to evolve with connections to categorification (Khovanov homology) and quantum computing.

Definitions

  • Knot: An embedding of \(S^1\) in \(S^3\). A link is a disjoint union of knots.
  • Kauffman Bracket: A state sum invariant \(\langle L \rangle \in \mathbb{Z}[A, A^{-1}]\) defined by skein relations: \(\langle \bigcirc \rangle = 1\), \(\langle L \cup \bigcirc \rangle = (-A^2 - A^{-2})\langle L \rangle\), and \(A\langle \rangle - A^{-1}\langle \rangle = (A^2 - A^{-2})\langle \rangle\).
  • Jones Polynomial: \(V_L(t) = ((-A)^{-3w(L)}\langle L \rangle)_{A = t^{-1/4}}\) where \(w(L)\) is the writhe.
  • Chern-Simons Functional: For a connection \(A\) on a 3-manifold \(M\): \(CS(A) = \frac{k}{4\pi}\int_M \text{tr}(A \wedge dA + \frac{2}{3}A \wedge A \wedge A)\).
  • TQFT: A functor from the cobordism category to vector spaces, satisfying Atiyah's axioms.
  • Volume Conjecture: For a hyperbolic knot \(K\): \(\lim_{N \to \infty} \frac{2\pi \log |J_N(K; e^{2\pi i/N})|}{N} = \text{Vol}(S^3 \setminus K)\).

Theorem: The Jones Polynomial and TQFT

Theorem (Jones, 1984): There exists a unique Laurent polynomial invariant \(V_L(t) \in \mathbb{Z}[t^{1/2}, t^{-1/2}]\) satisfying:

  1. \(V_{\text{unknot}}(t) = 1\)
  2. The skein relation: \(t^{-1}V_{L_+}(t) - tV_{L_-}(t) = (t^{1/2} - t^{-1/2})V_{L_0}(t)\)

Theorem (Witten, 1989): The Jones polynomial at level \(k\) is the partition function of SU(2) Chern-Simons theory:

\[V_K(e^{2\pi i/(k+2)}) = \int \mathcal{D}A \, \text{tr}_R(\text{Hol}_K(A)) \, e^{iCS(A)}\]

Theorem (Reshetikhin-Turaev): Quantum groups \(U_q(\mathfrak{g})\) give rise to 3-manifold invariants and TQFTs.

Proof Sketch (Kauffman Bracket)

The Kauffman bracket is defined recursively on link diagrams:

  1. \(\langle \bigcirc \rangle = 1\) (normalization)
  2. \(\langle D \cup \bigcirc \rangle = d \langle D \rangle\) where \(d = -A^2 - A^{-2}\)
  3. \(\langle \rangle = A\langle \rangle + A^{-1}\langle \rangle\) (skein relation)

Each crossing is resolved into two smoothings, leading to \(2^n\) states for \(n\) crossings. The bracket is invariant under Reidemeister moves II and III, and the Jones polynomial is obtained by normalizing to achieve invariance under Reidemeister I.

Notation Guide

  • \(L\): Link or knot
  • \(\langle L \rangle\): Kauffman bracket
  • \(V_L(t)\): Jones polynomial
  • \(J_N(K; q)\): N-colored Jones polynomial
  • \(w(L)\): Writhe of oriented link
  • \(CS(A)\): Chern-Simons action
  • \(Z(M)\): Quantum invariant of 3-manifold
  • \(\text{Vol}(M)\): Hyperbolic volume

The Birth of Modern Statistics: Karl Pearson and the Biometric School

The late 19th and early 20th centuries witnessed a transformation in how science understood data and variation. At the center of this revolution stood Karl Pearson (1857-1936), a man whose intellectual curiosity spanned philosophy, law, literature, physics, and eventually, the field he would help create: mathematical statistics.

Pearson's journey to statistics was circuitous. After graduating from Cambridge with honors in mathematics, he studied law in London, traveled to Germany to absorb continental philosophy and literature, and held chairs in applied mathematics and mechanics at University College London. His statistical awakening came through his friendship with Walter Weldon, a zoologist studying crab measurements. Weldon brought Pearson masses of biological data that exhibited puzzling patterns of variation—patterns that existing mathematical tools couldn't adequately describe.

Inspired by Francis Galton's work on heredity and correlation, Pearson developed the method of moments for fitting distributions, introduced the chi-squared test for goodness of fit (1900), and coined the term "standard deviation." His 1894 paper "Contributions to the Mathematical Theory of Evolution" laid foundations for what would become the discipline of mathematical statistics. Pearson's vision was ambitious: he sought to establish a "mathematical theory of evolution" that could describe and predict biological variation through rigorous quantitative methods.

In 1901, Pearson, Weldon, and Galton founded the journal Biometrika, which remains one of the most prestigious statistical journals today. The biometric school, as they came to be known, promoted the idea that biological phenomena could be understood through statistical laws. This brought them into conflict with the Mendelians, led by William Bateson, who emphasized discontinuous variation and the primacy of genetics. The bitter feud between biometricians and Mendelians lasted decades, with Pearson refusing to accept Mendelian genetics until late in his career.

Pearson's influence extended beyond methodology to institutional power. He established the first university statistics department at UCL in 1911 and trained a generation of statisticians who would shape the field. Yet his legacy is complex. His eugenic views, common among intellectuals of his era but taken to extremes in his writings, cast a shadow over his achievements. The same mathematical tools he developed to understand heredity were employed in support of policies now universally condemned.

The statistical revolution Pearson helped launch would be carried forward by his students and successors. R.A. Fisher, who would revolutionize experimental design and estimation theory, began as Pearson's student but their relationship ended in acrimony—a pattern repeated with other talented mathematicians who challenged Pearson's authority. Jerzy Neyman, Egon Pearson (Karl's son), and others would build upon and transform the foundations laid by the biometric school.

Today, Pearson's contributions remain woven into the fabric of statistical practice. The correlation coefficient, chi-squared tests, and the method of moments are standard tools. His vision of statistics as a mathematical discipline applicable across the sciences has been fully realized. Yet the field he helped create has evolved far beyond his imagination, incorporating Bayesian methods, computational techniques, and theoretical frameworks that would be barely recognizable to the man who once sought a mathematical theory of evolution.

Putnam Competition Problem

Problem: Let \(f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\) be a continuous function such that for all \(x\in\mathbb{R}\):

\[f(x) + f(x+1) + f(x+2) = 0\]

Prove that \(f\) is periodic with period 6.

Hint

Apply the given relation at \(x\), \(x+1\), and \(x+2\). Look for a pattern by expressing \(f(x+3)\) and \(f(x+6)\) in terms of \(f(x)\).

Solution

From the given condition:

\[f(x) + f(x+1) + f(x+2) = 0 \quad \text{...(1)}\]

Replacing \(x\) with \(x+1\):

\[f(x+1) + f(x+2) + f(x+3) = 0 \quad \text{...(2)}\]

Subtracting (1) from (2):

\[f(x+3) - f(x) = 0\]

Therefore \(f(x+3) = f(x)\) for all \(x\).

This shows \(f\) has period 3. But wait—we need to verify this is consistent. Actually, from \(f(x+3) = f(x)\), we immediately get period 3, which implies period 6.

However, let's verify by computing \(f(x+6)\) directly:

From \(f(x+3) = f(x)\), we have \(f(x+6) = f(x+3) = f(x)\).

Thus \(f\) is periodic with period 6. (In fact, the argument shows period 3, which is stronger.)
