1. Schemes: The Geometry of Rings

Motivation: Classical algebraic geometry studies varieties as sets of solutions to polynomial equations. But this approach fails to capture nilpotent elements, infinitesimal structure, or behave well under base change. Grothendieck's schemes provide the definitive solution by viewing geometry as encoded entirely in the algebraic structure of rings.

Definitions:

  • An affine scheme is a locally ringed space isomorphic to \( \text{Spec}(A) \) for some commutative ring \( A \), where the underlying topological space consists of prime ideals with the Zariski topology.
  • A scheme is a locally ringed space \( (X, \mathcal{O}_X) \) that admits an open cover by affine schemes.
  • The structure sheaf \( \mathcal{O}_X \) assigns to each open set \( U \) the ring of "regular functions" on \( U \).

Theorem (Equivalence of Categories):

The functor \( A \mapsto \text{Spec}(A) \) induces a contravariant equivalence between the category of commutative rings and the category of affine schemes.

Proof Sketch:

Given a ring homomorphism \( \phi: A \to B \), we obtain a continuous map \( f: \text{Spec}(B) \to \text{Spec}(A) \) by \( \mathfrak{p} \mapsto \phi^{-1}(\mathfrak{p}) \). The map on structure sheaves comes from the localization maps. Conversely, any morphism of affine schemes induces a ring homomorphism on global sections. These constructions are mutually inverse.

Notation Guide:

  • \( \text{Spec}(A) \): Prime spectrum of ring \( A \)
  • \( \mathcal{O}_X \): Structure sheaf of scheme \( X \)
  • \( \mathfrak{p} \): Prime ideal
  • \( A_{\mathfrak{p}} \): Localization at prime \( \mathfrak{p} \)

2. Algebraic K-Theory: Measuring Linear Structure

Motivation: Given a ring, how can we measure its "linear algebraic" properties? K-theory assigns to each ring a sequence of abelian groups \( K_n(R) \) that encode deep information about projective modules, invertible matrices, and higher symmetries. \( K_0 \) classifies projective modules, \( K_1 \) captures the stable linear group, and higher K-groups reveal subtle arithmetic and geometric invariants.

Definitions:

  • \( K_0(R) \): The Grothendieck group of finitely generated projective \( R \)-modules.
  • \( K_1(R) = GL(R)^{ab} = GL(R)/[GL(R), GL(R)] \), the abelianization of the infinite general linear group.
  • For a field \( F \), \( K_1(F) \cong F^\times \) (the multiplicative group).

Theorem (Bass-Serre):

For a Dedekind domain \( R \) with fraction field \( F \), there is an exact sequence:

\[ 0 \to K_0(R) \to K_0(F) \to \bigoplus_{\mathfrak{p}} K_0(R/\mathfrak{p}) \to 0 \]

Proof Sketch:

The map \( K_0(R) \to K_0(F) \) sends a projective module to its localization. The boundary map sends the class of \( F \) to the sum of residue field contributions at each prime. Exactness follows from the structure of projective modules over Dedekind domains: every projective is a direct sum of ideals.

Notation Guide:

  • \( K_n(R) \): n-th K-group of ring \( R \)
  • \( GL_n(R) \): General linear group of invertible \( n \times n \) matrices
  • \( GL(R) = \varinjlim GL_n(R) \): Infinite general linear group
  • \( P(R) \): Category of finitely generated projective modules

3. Étale Cohomology: Cohomology for Arithmetic Geometry

Motivation: For complex algebraic varieties, singular cohomology provides powerful topological invariants. But over finite fields or number fields, the Zariski topology is too coarse for meaningful cohomology. Grothendieck's étale topology provides a refinement where "open sets" are étale morphisms, enabling a Weil cohomology theory for arbitrary schemes.

Definitions:

  • An étale morphism \( f: X \to Y \) is flat, locally of finite presentation, and for all \( y \in Y \), the fiber \( X_y \) is a disjoint union of spectra of finite separable field extensions of \( k(y) \).
  • The étale site \( X_{\text{ét}} \) has objects that are étale morphisms to \( X \), with the étale topology.
  • Étale cohomology \( H^i_{\text{ét}}(X, \mathcal{F}) \) is the derived functor cohomology on the étale site.

Theorem (Comparison):

For a smooth scheme \( X \) over \( \mathbb{C} \) and a finite abelian group \( A \):

\[ H^i_{\text{ét}}(X, A) \cong H^i_{\text{sing}}(X(\mathbb{C}), A) \]

Proof Sketch:

The étale topology refines the classical topology in the analytic setting. The comparison theorem follows from showing that étale covers correspond to topological covers and that the étale sheaf cohomology computes the same derived functors as singular cohomology.

Notation Guide:

  • \( X_{\text{ét}} \): Étale site of \( X \)
  • \( H^i_{\text{ét}} \): Étale cohomology groups
  • \( \mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}(1) = \mu_n \): Sheaf of n-th roots of unity
  • \( \mathcal{F} \): Étale sheaf (lisse sheaf, constructible sheaf)

def etale_cohomology_point(field, n):
    """
    For Spec(F) where F is a field:
    H^0_et(Spec(F), Z/nZ) = Z/nZ
    H^1_et(Spec(F), Z/nZ) = Hom(Gal(F^sep/F), Z/nZ) = H^1(Gal, Z/nZ)
    """
    if field == "finite":
        # Gal(F_q^sep/F_q) ≅ Z-hat (profinite completion)
        # H^1 = Hom(Z-hat, Z/nZ) = Z/nZ
        return {"H^0": "Z/nZ", "H^1": "Z/nZ", "higher": "0"}
    elif field == "complex":
        # Trivial Galois group
        return {"H^0": "Z/nZ", "higher": "0"}
    return {}

def etale_cohomology_curve(genus, n):
    """
    For a smooth projective curve X of genus g over C:
    H^0_et(X, Z/nZ) = Z/nZ
    H^1_et(X, Z/nZ) = (Z/nZ)^{2g}
    H^2_et(X, Z/nZ) = Z/nZ (orientation)
    """
    return {
        "H^0": f"Z/{n}Z",
        "H^1": f"(Z/{n}Z)^{2*{genus}}",
        "H^2": f"Z/{n}Z"
    }

1. Post-Quantum Cryptography: Lattice-Based Systems

Real-World Motivation: Quantum computers threaten RSA and elliptic curve cryptography through Shor's algorithm. Lattice-based cryptography offers quantum-resistant alternatives based on the hardness of problems like Learning With Errors (LWE) and Shortest Vector Problem (SVP). NIST has already standardized lattice schemes for the post-quantum era.

Definitions:

  • A lattice \( L \subset \mathbb{R}^n \) is a discrete additive subgroup, equivalently \( L = \{ \sum_{i=1}^n z_i \mathbf{b}_i : z_i \in \mathbb{Z} \} \) for basis \( \{\mathbf{b}_i\} \).
  • The Shortest Vector Problem (SVP): Given a lattice basis, find the shortest non-zero vector in \( L \).
  • Learning With Errors (LWE): Given pairs \( (\mathbf{a}_i, b_i = \langle \mathbf{a}_i, \mathbf{s} \rangle + e_i \mod q) \), recover secret \( \mathbf{s} \).

Theorem (Regev):

If there exists an efficient algorithm for solving LWE with non-negligible advantage, then there exists an efficient quantum algorithm for approximating SVP to within \( \tilde{O}(n) \) in the worst case.

Proof Sketch:

The reduction uses a quantum algorithm that, given an LWE oracle, samples from the discrete Gaussian on the dual lattice. By iterating this process, one can solve the closest vector problem, which implies a solution to SVP. The quantum step is essential for the reduction.

Notation:

  • \( \Lambda \): Lattice
  • \( \lambda_1(\Lambda) \): Length of shortest non-zero vector
  • \( \mathbf{e} \): Error vector (small norm)
  • \( q \): Modulus (typically polynomial in \( n \))
# Python: Lattice-Based Cryptography (LWE)
import numpy as np
from numpy.random import randint, normal

def generate_lwe_instance(n, m, q, sigma):
    """
    Generate LWE instance: (A, b = As + e mod q)
    n: dimension of secret
    m: number of samples
    q: modulus
    sigma: error standard deviation
    """
    # Random matrix A
    A = randint(0, q, size=(m, n))
    
    # Secret vector s
    s = randint(0, q, size=n)
    
    # Error vector (discrete Gaussian approximation)
    e = np.round(normal(0, sigma, size=m)).astype(int) % q
    
    # Compute b = As + e mod q
    b = (A @ s + e) % q
    
    return A, b, s, e

2. Sparse Recovery: Compressed Sensing Foundations

Real-World Motivation: MRI scans traditionally require lengthy data acquisition. Compressed sensing revolutionized medical imaging by proving that sparse signals can be recovered from far fewer measurements than Nyquist-Shannon requires. This enables faster scans, reduced radiation exposure, and efficient signal acquisition.

Definitions:

  • A vector \( \mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n \) is k-sparse if at most \( k \) entries are non-zero.
  • A matrix \( A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n} \) satisfies the Restricted Isometry Property (RIP) of order \( k \) if for all k-sparse \( \mathbf{x} \): \[ (1-\delta_k)\|\mathbf{x}\|_2^2 \leq \|A\mathbf{x}\|_2^2 \leq (1+\delta_k)\|\mathbf{x}\|_2^2 \]
  • Basis Pursuit: \( \min \|\mathbf{x}\|_1 \) subject to \( A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b} \).

Theorem (Candès-Tao):

If \( A \) satisfies RIP with \( \delta_{2k} < \sqrt{2} - 1 \), then the solution to Basis Pursuit equals the sparsest solution.

Proof Sketch:

Let \( \mathbf{x}^* \) be the BP solution and \( \mathbf{x}_0 \) the true sparse solution. Their difference \( \mathbf{h} = \mathbf{x}^* - \mathbf{x}_0 \) lies in the null space of \( A \). The RIP condition ensures that any null space vector cannot have most of its \( \ell_1 \) mass concentrated on a small support. This forces \( \mathbf{h} = 0 \).

Notation:

  • \( \|\mathbf{x}\|_0 \): Number of non-zero entries (sparsity)
  • \( \|\mathbf{x}\|_1 = \sum |x_i| \): \( \ell_1 \) norm (convex relaxation)
  • \( \delta_k \): RIP constant of order \( k \)
  • \( \Sigma_k \): Set of k-sparse vectors
# Python: Compressed Sensing and Sparse Recovery
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import minimize, linprog

def generate_rip_matrix(m, n, k):
    """
    Generate a matrix with good RIP properties (Gaussian ensemble)
    m: number of measurements
    n: signal dimension
    k: sparsity level
    """
    # Gaussian matrix scaled appropriately
    A = np.random.randn(m, n) / np.sqrt(m)
    return A

def basis_pursuit(A, b):
    """
    Solve: min ||x||_1 subject to Ax = b
    Using linear programming
    """
    m, n = A.shape
    
    # Convert to standard LP: min c^T z subject to Mz = d, z >= 0
    # x = x_pos - x_neg where x_pos, x_neg >= 0
    # Objective: sum(x_pos) + sum(x_neg)
    
    c = np.ones(2 * n)  # Minimize sum of positive and negative parts
    
    # Equality constraints: A(x_pos - x_neg) = b
    A_eq = np.hstack([A, -A])
    b_eq = b
    
    # Bounds: z >= 0
    bounds = [(0, None) for _ in range(2 * n)]
    
    result = linprog(c, A_eq=A_eq, b_eq=b_eq, bounds=bounds, method='highs')
    
    if result.success:
        z = result.x
        x = z[:n] - z[n:]
        return x
    return None

def ista(A, b, lambda_reg, max_iter=1000, tol=1e-6):
    """
    Iterative Soft Thresholding Algorithm for LASSO
    min ||Ax - b||^2 + lambda*||x||_1
    """
    m, n = A.shape
    x = np.zeros(n)
    
    # Lipschitz constant
    L = np.linalg.norm(A.T @ A, 2)
    step = 1.0 / L
    
    for _ in range(max_iter):
        x_old = x.copy()
        # Gradient step
        grad = A.T @ (A @ x - b)
        y = x - step * grad
        # Soft thresholding
        x = np.sign(y) * np.maximum(np.abs(y) - step * lambda_reg, 0)
        
        if np.linalg.norm(x - x_old) < tol:
            break
    
    return x

# Example: Sparse signal recovery
n = 100  # Signal dimension
k = 5    # Sparsity
m = 30   # Measurements (m << n but m > O(k log(n/k)))

# Generate sparse signal
x_true = np.zeros(n)
support = np.random.choice(n, k, replace=False)
x_true[support] = np.random.randn(k) * 5

# Generate measurement matrix
A = generate_rip_matrix(m, n, k)

# Measurements
b = A @ x_true

# Recover using Basis Pursuit
x_bp = basis_pursuit(A, b)

print(f"Signal dimension: {n}, Sparsity: {k}, Measurements: {m}")
print(f"True support: {sorted(support)}")
if x_bp is not None:
    recovered_support = np.where(np.abs(x_bp) > 0.1)[0]
    print(f"Recovered support: {sorted(recovered_support)}")
    print(f"Recovery error: {np.linalg.norm(x_true - x_bp):.6f}")

# Also try ISTA
x_ista = ista(A, b, lambda_reg=0.1)
print(f"ISTA error: {np.linalg.norm(x_true - x_ista):.6f}")

3. Causal Inference: The Do-Calculus

Real-World Motivation: Correlation does not imply causation. Yet in medicine, economics, and policy, we need to know the causal effect of interventions: "Does smoking cause cancer?" "What is the effect of a minimum wage increase?" Judea Pearl's do-calculus provides a mathematical framework for answering such questions from observational data and causal graphs.

Definitions:

  • A causal Bayesian network is a directed acyclic graph where edges represent direct causal influences.
  • The do-operator \( P(Y | do(X=x)) \) represents the distribution of \( Y \) when \( X \) is intervened upon (forced to value \( x \)).
  • A set \( Z \) d-separates \( X \) from \( Y \) if it blocks all paths between them.

Theorem (Back-Door Criterion):

If a set of variables \( Z \) satisfies: (1) no node in \( Z \) is a descendant of \( X \), and (2) \( Z \) blocks every path between \( X \) and \( Y \) that contains an arrow into \( X \), then:

\[ P(Y | do(X=x)) = \sum_z P(Y | X=x, Z=z) P(Z=z) \]

Proof Sketch:

The back-door paths (those with arrows into \( X \)) represent confounding. Conditioning on \( Z \) blocks these confounding paths. Since \( Z \) contains no descendants of \( X \), we don't condition away any of the causal effect. The formula follows from the factorization of the joint distribution.

Notation:

  • \( do(X=x) \): Intervention setting \( X \) to \( x \)
  • \( P(Y | do(X=x)) \): Interventional distribution
  • \( G_{\overline{X}} \): Graph with incoming edges to \( X \) removed
  • \( G_{\underline{X}} \): Graph with outgoing edges from \( X \) removed

1. Tropical Geometry: The Geometry of the Min-Plus Semiring

Historical Context: Tropical geometry emerged in the 1980s from the work of Imre Simon on optimization and automata theory, later developed by Kapranov, Mikhalkin, and others. It studies algebraic varieties over the tropical semiring \( (\mathbb{R} \cup \{\infty\}, \min, +) \), providing a piecewise-linear shadow of classical algebraic geometry with applications to enumerative geometry, phylogenetics, and optimization.

Definitions:

  • The tropical semiring \( \mathbb{T} = (\mathbb{R} \cup \{\infty\}, \oplus, \odot) \) where \( a \oplus b = \min(a,b) \) and \( a \odot b = a + b \).
  • A tropical polynomial is a piecewise-linear function \( f(x) = \min_i (a_i + \langle b_i, x \rangle) \).
  • The tropical hypersurface of \( f \) is the set where the minimum is achieved at least twice.

Theorem (Kapranov's Theorem):

Let \( f = \sum c_\alpha x^\alpha \in \mathbb{C}[x_1, \ldots, x_n] \). The tropical hypersurface of the tropical polynomial \( \text{trop}(f) = \min_\alpha (\text{val}(c_\alpha) + \langle \alpha, x \rangle) \) equals the corner locus of the Amoeba of the variety \( V(f) \).

Proof Sketch:

The amoeba is the image of \( V(f) \) under the log-absolute map. As \( t \to \infty \), the amoeba degenerates to a polyhedral complex. The tropical variety captures this limiting behavior, with each cell corresponding to a term in the tropical polynomial achieving the minimum.

Notation:

  • \( \mathbb{T} \): Tropical semiring
  • \( a \oplus b = \min(a,b) \): Tropical addition
  • \( a \odot b = a + b \): Tropical multiplication
  • \( \text{trop}(f) \): Tropicalization of polynomial \( f \)
# Python: Tropical Geometry
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def tropical_add(a, b):
    """Tropical addition: min(a, b)"""
    return np.minimum(a, b)

def tropical_mult(a, b):
    """Tropical multiplication: a + b"""
    return a + b

def tropical_poly(coeffs, exponents, x):
    """
    Evaluate tropical polynomial
    coeffs: array of coefficients (in tropical sense: shifts)
    exponents: array of exponent vectors
    x: point at which to evaluate
    
    f(x) = min_i (coeffs[i] + dot(exponents[i], x))
    """
    values = []
    for c, e in zip(coeffs, exponents):
        val = c + np.dot(e, x)
        values.append(val)
    return np.min(values), values

def tropical_line(a, b, x_range):
    """
    Tropical line: min(ax, by, 0)
    This creates a piecewise linear curve
    """
    X, Y = np.meshgrid(x_range, x_range)
    Z = np.minimum(np.minimum(a * X, b * Y), 0)
    return X, Y, Z

2. Mirror Symmetry: Physics Meets Algebraic Geometry

Historical Context: Mirror symmetry originated in string theory in the late 1980s when physicists Candelas and others noticed that Calabi-Yau manifolds come in pairs with "mirrored" properties. The phenomenon was brought to mathematics when Candelas, de la Ossa, Green, and Parkes used mirror symmetry to predict curve counts on the quintic threefold, sparking a revolution in enumerative geometry and leading to deep connections with Hodge theory, derived categories, and the Strominger-Yau-Zaslow conjecture.

Definitions:

  • A Calabi-Yau manifold is a compact Kähler manifold with trivial canonical bundle and vanishing first Chern class.
  • A mirror pair consists of Calabi-Yau manifolds \( X \) and \( X^\vee \) with Hodge numbers swapped: \( h^{p,q}(X) = h^{n-p,q}(X^\vee) \).
  • The A-model (symplectic) counts holomorphic curves (Gromov-Witten invariants).
  • The B-model (complex) computes period integrals of holomorphic forms.

Theorem (Mirror Symmetry for the Quintic):

Let \( X \) be the quintic threefold in \( \mathbb{P}^4 \) and \( X^\vee \) its mirror. The Gromov-Witten potential of \( X \) equals the variation of Hodge structure prepotential of \( X^\vee \).

Proof Sketch:

The proof uses toric geometry to construct the mirror family explicitly. The Picard-Fuchs equations governing periods on the B-side are solved using the GKZ system. The mirror map relates complex moduli to Kähler moduli, and the Yukawa couplings match Gromov-Witten invariants through the mirror transformation.

Notation:

  • \( h^{p,q} \): Hodge numbers
  • \( \mathcal{M}_{\text{cs}} \): Complex structure moduli space
  • \( \mathcal{M}_{\text{Kähler}} \): Kähler moduli space
  • \( \Omega \): Holomorphic volume form
  • \( q = e^{2\pi i t} \): Mirror map coordinate

3. Derived Categories: Homological Algebra's Ultimate Tool

Historical Context: Derived categories were introduced by Verdier in his 1963 thesis (published in 1996) as a framework for Grothendieck's duality theory. They have since become fundamental in algebraic geometry, representation theory, and mathematical physics. Kontsevich's Homological Mirror Symmetry conjecture posits that mirror symmetry is an equivalence of derived categories.

Definitions:

  • The derived category \( D(\mathcal{A}) \) of an abelian category is obtained by formally inverting quasi-isomorphisms in the homotopy category of chain complexes.
  • An object in \( D^b(X) \) (bounded derived category of coherent sheaves) is a complex of sheaves with bounded cohomology.
  • The Fourier-Mukai transform is an equivalence of derived categories induced by a kernel object.

Theorem (Orlov):

Let \( X \) and \( Y \) be smooth projective varieties. Any fully faithful exact functor \( F: D^b(X) \to D^b(Y) \) is a Fourier-Mukai transform.

Proof Sketch:

The proof constructs a kernel object \( \mathcal{K} \in D^b(X \times Y) \) from the functor \( F \) by considering the image of the structure sheaf of the diagonal under a certain composition. The convolution with this kernel recovers the original functor.

Notation:

  • \( D^b(X) \): Bounded derived category of coherent sheaves
  • \( [n] \): Shift functor (\( \mathcal{F}[n]^i = \mathcal{F}^{i+n} \))
  • \( \Phi_{\mathcal{K}} \): Fourier-Mukai transform with kernel \( \mathcal{K} \)
  • \( \text{Hom}^i(A,B) = \text{Ext}^i(A,B) \)
# Python: Derived Category Concepts (Symbolic)
# Note: Actual derived category computations are highly abstract

class ChainComplex:
    """
    Simplified model of a chain complex
    """
    def __init__(self, terms, differentials):
        self.terms = terms  # List of objects (sheaves, modules, etc.)
        self.differentials = differentials  # Maps d_i: C_i -> C_{i-1}
    
    def cohomology(self, i):
        """Compute i-th cohomology: ker(d_i) / im(d_{i+1})"""
        if i < 0 or i >= len(self.terms):
            return 0
        # Simplified - in practice requires homological algebra
        return f"H^{i}(C)"
    
    def shift(self, n):
        """Shift complex by n: C[n]^i = C^{i+n}"""
        return ChainComplex(
            [None]*n + self.terms,
            [None]*n + self.differentials
        )

4. Noncommutative Geometry: Spaces from Algebras

Historical Context: Noncommutative geometry was founded by Alain Connes in the 1980s as a synthesis of operator algebras and differential geometry. It provides a framework where the commutative algebra of functions on a space is replaced by a noncommutative algebra, allowing geometric concepts to extend to quantum spaces, foliations, and number theory.

Definitions:

  • A spectral triple \( (\mathcal{A}, \mathcal{H}, D) \) consists of a C*-algebra \( \mathcal{A} \) acting on a Hilbert space \( \mathcal{H} \), and a self-adjoint operator \( D \) with compact resolvent.
  • The Connes distance between states \( \phi, \psi \) is \( d(\phi, \psi) = \sup\{ |\phi(a) - \psi(a)| : \|[D,a]\| \leq 1 \} \).
  • The noncommutative torus \( \mathbb{T}^2_\theta \) has C*-algebra generated by unitaries \( U, V \) with \( UV = e^{2\pi i \theta} VU \).

Theorem (Connes):

For a compact spin Riemannian manifold \( M \), the spectral triple \( (C^\infty(M), L^2(S), D) \) completely determines \( M \) up to isometry.

Proof Sketch:

The Dirac operator \( D \) encodes the metric through the commutator condition. The distance formula recovers the geodesic distance. The algebra structure plus the spectral data reconstruct the manifold's differentiable structure and metric.

Notation:

  • \( (\mathcal{A}, \mathcal{H}, D) \): Spectral triple
  • \( \Omega_D(\mathcal{A}) \): Differential forms in NCG
  • \( \oint a|D|^{-n} \): Dixmier trace (integration)
  • \( \mathbb{T}^n_\theta \): Noncommutative torus

📜 Math History: Alexander Grothendieck and the Revolution of Schemes

Alexander Grothendieck (1928–2014) transformed algebraic geometry more radically than any mathematician since Descartes unified geometry and algebra. Born in Berlin to anarchist parents, Grothendieck spent his childhood in refugee camps and later in France, where his mathematical genius emerged at the Institut des Hautes Études Scientifiques (IHÉS).

Before Grothendieck, algebraic geometers studied varieties—solution sets of polynomial equations—using geometric intuition and commutative algebra. This approach, developed by the Italian school and refined by Zariski and Weil, had achieved remarkable results but faced fundamental limitations. Weil's foundations, while rigorous, were technically cumbersome and didn't capture all the phenomena geometers needed.

Grothendieck's insight was to replace varieties with schemes. Where a variety captures only the geometric points of a polynomial system, a scheme encodes all its algebraic structure—nilpotents, infinitesimal behavior, and arithmetic information. The key was to view geometry as emerging from rings rather than sets of points. The prime spectrum Spec(A) of a ring A became the basic building block.

This perspective shift enabled Grothendieck and his collaborators to prove the Weil conjectures, settle fundamental questions in algebraic geometry, and create tools—sheaf cohomology, étale cohomology, topos theory—that would reshape mathematics. His Séminaire de Géométrie Algébrique (SGA) and Éléments de Géométrie Algébrique (EGA) remain foundational references.

Grothendieck's later life took unexpected turns. He abandoned mathematics in 1970, protesting military funding of science. He spent years as an environmental activist and hermit, writing philosophical and mystical texts. His autobiography Récoltes et Semailles (Harvests and Sowings) offers profound reflections on creativity, mathematics, and meaning.

Problem: AIME 2024

Let \( n \) be a positive integer. A sequence of integers \( a_1, a_2, \ldots, a_n \) is called good if:

  1. \( 1 \leq a_i \leq 100 \) for all \( i \)
  2. For all \( i < j \), if \( a_i = a_j \) then \( a_{i+1} = a_{j+1} \) (when defined)

Find the number of good sequences of length 4.

💡 Hint

Consider cases based on how many distinct values appear in the sequence. The constraint essentially says that equal elements must be followed by equal elements—this is related to De Bruijn sequences and finite automata.

✅ Solution

Let us analyze the constraint carefully. The condition says that if \( a_i = a_j \), then the next elements must also be equal. This means the sequence is determined by:

  • The set of distinct values that appear
  • The order in which "transitions" between values occur

For a sequence of length 4, let's count by the number of distinct values:

Case 1: Exactly 1 distinct value.
All entries are equal. There are 100 choices for this value.
Count: 100

Case 2: Exactly 2 distinct values.
The sequence has the form \( a, a, \ldots, a, b, b, \ldots, b \) where we switch from \( a \) to \( b \) exactly once.
Choose positions: the switch can happen after position 1, 2, or 3 (3 choices).
Choose values: \( a \neq b \), so \( 100 \times 99 \) choices.
Count: \( 3 \times 100 \times 99 = 29700 \)

Case 3: Exactly 3 distinct values.
The sequence has form \( a, \ldots, a, b, \ldots, b, c, \ldots, c \) with two switches.
Choose positions: choose 2 positions from 3 possible switch points: \( \binom{3}{2} = 3 \) ways.
Choose values: \( a, b, c \) all distinct: \( 100 \times 99 \times 98 \) ways.
Count: \( 3 \times 100 \times 99 \times 98 = 2910600 \)

Case 4: Exactly 4 distinct values.
All entries distinct: \( a, b, c, d \).
Count: \( 100 \times 99 \times 98 \times 97 = 94109400 \)

Total: \( 100 + 29700 + 2910600 + 94109400 = 97049800 \)

🎨 Visualization of the Day: The Hopf Fibration

The Hopf fibration is one of the most beautiful structures in mathematics—a way of filling 3-dimensional space with linked circles, arising from a map from the 3-sphere to the 2-sphere. Each point on \( S^2 \) corresponds to a circle (fiber) in \( S^3 \), and any two distinct fibers are linked.

# Python: Visualizing the Hopf Fibration
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from mpl_toolkits.mplot3d import Axes3D

def hopf_fibration(theta, phi, psi):
    """
    Parametrize S^3 via Hopf coordinates
    (theta, phi, psi) -> (z1, z2) in C^2 with |z1|^2 + |z2|^2 = 1
    """
    z1 = np.exp(1j * (psi + phi)/2) * np.cos(theta/2)
    z2 = np.exp(1j * (psi - phi)/2) * np.sin(theta/2)
    return z1, z2

def stereographic_projection(z1, z2):
    """
    Stereographic projection from S^3 to R^3
    (z1, z2) -> (x, y, z) in R^3
    """
    # Point on S^3: (Re(z1), Im(z1), Re(z2), Im(z2))
    x4 = np.real(z1)
    y4 = np.imag(z1)
    z4 = np.real(z2)
    w4 = np.imag(z2)
    
    # Stereographic projection from (0,0,0,1)
    denom = 1 - w4
    x = x4 / denom
    y = y4 / denom
    z = z4 / denom
    return x, y, z

def hopf_fiber(phi0, n_points=100):
    """
    Compute a single Hopf fiber (circle in S^3)
    Fixed phi = phi0, varying theta and psi with theta + psi = constant
    """
    t = np.linspace(0, 2*np.pi, n_points)
    
    # For a fixed phi, the fiber is parameterized by varying psi
    # theta is determined by the Hopf map: theta = constant for fixed base point
    theta = np.pi/2  # Equator of S^2
    
    # The fiber: phi = phi0, theta = pi/2, psi varies
    z1 = np.exp(1j * (t + phi0)/2) * np.cos(theta/2)
    z2 = np.exp(1j * (t - phi0)/2) * np.sin(theta/2)
    
    # Project to R^3
    x, y, z = stereographic_projection(z1, z2)
    return x, y, z
