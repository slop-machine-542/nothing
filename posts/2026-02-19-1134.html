Commutative Algebra: The Language of Geometry

commutative-algebra

Motivation

Commutative algebra studies commutative rings and their ideals, providing the algebraic foundation for algebraic geometry. The subject emerged from the work of David Hilbert and Emmy Noether in the early 20th century, who recognized that geometric properties of algebraic varieties could be translated into algebraic properties of coordinate rings. This correspondence—known as the algebra-geometry dictionary—allows us to study geometric objects through their algebraic descriptions, and vice versa. Modern commutative algebra has applications in number theory, combinatorics, and even computational biology.

Definitions

  • Commutative Ring: A ring \(R\) where multiplication is commutative: \(ab = ba\) for all \(a, b \in R\).
  • Prime Ideal: An ideal \(\mathfrak{p} \subsetneq R\) such that if \(ab \in \mathfrak{p}\), then \(a \in \mathfrak{p}\) or \(b \in \mathfrak{p}\). Equivalently, \(R/\mathfrak{p}\) is an integral domain.
  • Maximal Ideal: An ideal \(\mathfrak{m}\) that is maximal with respect to inclusion among proper ideals. Equivalently, \(R/\mathfrak{m}\) is a field.
  • Nilradical: The intersection of all prime ideals, denoted \(\mathfrak{N}(R)\), consisting of all nilpotent elements.
  • Localization: For a multiplicative set \(S \subseteq R\), the ring \(S^{-1}R\) consists of fractions \(r/s\) with \(r \in R\), \(s \in S\).
  • Noetherian Ring: A ring satisfying the ascending chain condition on ideals: every increasing chain stabilizes.

Theorem: Hilbert's Nullstellensatz

Theorem (Weak Nullstellensatz): Let \(k\) be an algebraically closed field and \(\mathfrak{m} \subseteq k[x_1, \ldots, x_n]\) a maximal ideal. Then there exist \(a_1, \ldots, a_n \in k\) such that:

\[\mathfrak{m} = (x_1 - a_1, \ldots, x_n - a_n)\]

Strong Nullstellensatz: For any ideal \(I \subseteq k[x_1, \ldots, x_n]\):

\[I(V(I)) = \sqrt{I}\]

where \(V(I) = \{x \in k^n : f(x) = 0 \text{ for all } f \in I\}\) and \(\sqrt{I}\) is the radical of \(I\).

Proof Sketch

The proof uses Noether normalization and Zariski's Lemma:

  1. Zariski's Lemma: If \(K\) is a field extension of \(k\) that is finitely generated as a \(k\)-algebra, then \(K\) is a finite extension of \(k\).
  2. For the weak form: \(k[x_1, \ldots, x_n]/\mathfrak{m}\) is a field finitely generated over \(k\), hence equals \(k\) (since \(k\) is algebraically closed).
  3. The images of \(x_i\) in the quotient give the coordinates \((a_1, \ldots, a_n)\).
  4. The strong form follows from the Rabinowitsch trick: introduce a new variable and apply the weak Nullstellensatz.

Notation Guide

  • \(R\) — commutative ring with unity
  • \(\mathfrak{p}, \mathfrak{q}\) — prime ideals (Gothic font convention)
  • \(\mathfrak{m}\) — maximal ideal
  • \(\text{Spec}(R)\) — spectrum of prime ideals of \(R\)
  • \(R_{\mathfrak{p}}\) — localization at prime \(\mathfrak{p}\)
  • \(R_f\) — localization at powers of \(f\)
  • \(k[x_1, \ldots, x_n]\) — polynomial ring over field \(k\)
  • \(\sqrt{I}\) — radical of ideal \(I\)

Functional Analysis: Infinite-Dimensional Linear Algebra

functional-analysis

Motivation

Functional analysis extends the methods of linear algebra and calculus to infinite-dimensional spaces. Born from the study of differential and integral equations in the early 20th century, it provides the rigorous framework for quantum mechanics, partial differential equations, and optimization theory. The key insight is that many analytical problems can be reformulated as questions about operators on infinite-dimensional vector spaces, often Banach or Hilbert spaces. This abstraction reveals deep connections between seemingly unrelated areas of mathematics.

Definitions

  • Banach Space: A complete normed vector space \((X, \|\cdot\|)\), where every Cauchy sequence converges.
  • Hilbert Space: A Banach space whose norm comes from an inner product: \(\|x\|^2 = \langle x, x \rangle\).
  • Bounded Linear Operator: A linear map \(T: X \to Y\) between normed spaces with finite operator norm: \(\|T\| = \sup_{\|x\| \leq 1} \|Tx\| < \infty\).
  • Dual Space: \(X^* = \{f: X \to \mathbb{K} : f \text{ is bounded and linear}\}\), the space of continuous linear functionals.
  • Compact Operator: An operator \(T\) that maps bounded sets to relatively compact sets (closures are compact).
  • Spectrum: For \(T \in \mathcal{L}(X)\), \(\sigma(T) = \{\lambda \in \mathbb{C} : T - \lambda I \text{ is not invertible}\}\).

Theorem: Spectral Theorem for Compact Self-Adjoint Operators

Theorem: Let \(H\) be a separable Hilbert space and \(T: H \to H\) a compact self-adjoint operator. Then:

  1. The spectrum \(\sigma(T)\) consists of at most countably many real eigenvalues \(\{\lambda_n\}\) with \(\lambda_n \to 0\).
  2. There exists an orthonormal basis \(\{e_n\}\) of eigenvectors.
  3. For any \(x \in H\): \(Tx = \sum_{n} \lambda_n \langle x, e_n \rangle e_n\).

Proof Sketch

  1. Self-adjointness implies real spectrum: If \(Tx = \lambda x\), then \(\lambda \langle x, x \rangle = \langle Tx, x \rangle = \langle x, Tx \rangle = \overline{\lambda} \langle x, x \rangle\).
  2. Extremal principle: Consider maximizing \(| \langle Tx, x \rangle|\) over \(\|x\| = 1\). Compactness ensures the maximum is attained.
  3. Inductive construction: Once an eigenvector \(e_1\) is found, restrict to its orthogonal complement and repeat.
  4. Convergence: The eigenvalues must tend to zero; otherwise, we'd have an infinite orthonormal set with no convergent subsequence, contradicting compactness.

Notation Guide

  • \(X, Y, Z\) — Banach or Hilbert spaces
  • \(\mathcal{L}(X, Y)\) — bounded linear operators from \(X\) to \(Y\)
  • \(X^*\) — dual space of \(X\)
  • \(\langle x, y \rangle\) — inner product
  • \(T^*\) — adjoint operator
  • \(\sigma(T)\) — spectrum of \(T\)
  • \(\sigma_p(T)\) — point spectrum (eigenvalues)
  • \(\mathcal{K}(X)\) — compact operators on \(X\)

Galois Theory: The Mathematics of Equations

galois-theory

Motivation

Galois theory, developed by Évariste Galois in the 1830s, provides a profound connection between field theory and group theory. It answers fundamental questions about polynomial equations: when can they be solved by radicals? The theory reveals that the solvability of a polynomial is encoded in the structure of its Galois group—a group of automorphisms of the splitting field. This insight resolved centuries-old problems, proving that general quintic equations cannot be solved by radicals and characterizing exactly which equations can be.

Definitions

  • Field Extension: A pair of fields \(F \subseteq E\). The degree \([E:F]\) is the dimension of \(E\) as an \(F\)-vector space.
  • Splitting Field: For a polynomial \(f \in F[x]\), the smallest extension \(E/F\) containing all roots of \(f\).
  • Galois Extension: A normal and separable field extension. For finite extensions, this is equivalent to being the splitting field of a separable polynomial.
  • Galois Group: \(\text{Gal}(E/F) = \{\sigma \in \text{Aut}(E) : \sigma|_F = \text{id}_F\}\), the group of automorphisms fixing \(F\).
  • Fixed Field: For \(H \subseteq \text{Gal}(E/F)\), \(E^H = \{x \in E : \sigma(x) = x \text{ for all } \sigma \in H\}\).
  • Solvable by Radicals: A polynomial is solvable by radicals if its roots can be expressed using field operations and \(n\)-th roots.

Theorem: The Fundamental Theorem of Galois Theory

Theorem: Let \(E/F\) be a finite Galois extension with Galois group \(G = \text{Gal}(E/F)\). There is an inclusion-reversing bijection:

\[\{\text{intermediate fields } K : F \subseteq K \subseteq E\} \longleftrightarrow \{\text{subgroups } H \subseteq G\}\]

given by \(K \mapsto \text{Gal}(E/K)\) and \(H \mapsto E^H\). Furthermore:

  • \([E:K] = |\text{Gal}(E/K)|\) and \([K:F] = [G : \text{Gal}(E/K)]\)
  • \(K/F\) is Galois if and only if \(\text{Gal}(E/K) \trianglelefteq G\)
  • If \(K/F\) is Galois, then \(\text{Gal}(K/F) \cong G / \text{Gal}(E/K)\)

Proof Sketch

  1. Artin's theorem: For any finite group \(H\) of automorphisms of \(E\), \([E:E^H] = |H|\).
  2. Correspondence: Show that \(E^{\text{Gal}(E/K)} = K\) and \(\text{Gal}(E/E^H) = H\).
  3. Normality criterion: \(K/F\) is normal iff \(\sigma(K) = K\) for all \(\sigma \in G\), which occurs precisely when \(\text{Gal}(E/K)\) is normal.
  4. Isomorphism: The restriction map \(G \to \text{Gal}(K/F)\) has kernel \(\text{Gal}(E/K)\).

Notation Guide

  • \(F, E, K\) — fields (typically \(F \subseteq K \subseteq E\))
  • \([E:F]\) — degree of field extension
  • \(\text{Gal}(E/F)\) — Galois group
  • \(E^H\) — fixed field of subgroup \(H\)
  • \(F(\alpha)\) — smallest field containing \(F\) and \(\alpha\)
  • \(\overline{F}\) — algebraic closure of \(F\)
  • \(\text{char}(F)\) — characteristic of field \(F\)

def galois_group_quadratic(a, b, c):
    """

Statistical Learning Theory: The Mathematics of Generalization

machine-learning-theory

Real-World Motivation

When a neural network classifies images or a spam filter identifies unwanted emails, how do we know it will work on new data? Statistical learning theory, founded by Vapnik and Chervonenkis in the 1960s-70s, provides the mathematical framework for understanding generalization—the ability of a model to perform well on unseen data. This theory has become essential for modern machine learning, guiding the design of algorithms that can learn from finite samples while controlling overfitting.

Definitions

  • Risk: \(R(h) = \mathbb{E}_{(x,y) \sim \mathcal{D}}[L(h(x), y)]\), the expected loss of hypothesis \(h\) under data distribution \(\mathcal{D}\).
  • Empirical Risk: \(\hat{R}_n(h) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n L(h(x_i), y_i)\), the average loss on training data.
  • Hypothesis Class: A set \(\mathcal{H}\) of candidate functions \(h: \mathcal{X} \to \mathcal{Y}\).
  • VC Dimension: The largest set that can be shattered by \(\mathcal{H}\). A set is shattered if \(\mathcal{H}\) can realize all possible labelings.
  • Rademacher Complexity: \(\mathfrak{R}_n(\mathcal{H}) = \mathbb{E}_{\sigma, S}[\sup_{h \in \mathcal{H}} \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \sigma_i h(x_i)]\), measuring the richness of \(\mathcal{H}\).

Theorem: Uniform Convergence Bound

Theorem: For a hypothesis class \(\mathcal{H}\) with VC dimension \(d\), with probability at least \(1-\delta\):

\[\sup_{h \in \mathcal{H}} |R(h) - \hat{R}_n(h)| \leq O\left(\sqrt{\frac{d \log(n/d) + \log(1/\delta)}{n}}\right)\]

Corollary (Generalization): If \(\hat{h}\) minimizes empirical risk, then with high probability:

\[R(\hat{h}) \leq R(h^*) + O\left(\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}\right)\]

where \(h^*\) is the optimal hypothesis in \(\mathcal{H}\).

Proof Sketch

  1. Sauer-Shelah Lemma: A class with VC dimension \(d\) can realize at most \(\sum_{i=0}^d \binom{n}{i} \leq (en/d)^d\) labelings on \(n\) points.
  2. Symmetrization: Bound the deviation using a ghost sample and Rademacher variables.
  3. Union bound: Over the effective number of hypotheses (polynomial in \(n\) for finite VC dimension).
  4. Concentration: Apply Hoeffding's inequality to each hypothesis.

Notation Guide

  • \(\mathcal{X}, \mathcal{Y}\) — input and output spaces
  • \(\mathcal{H}\) — hypothesis class
  • \(R(h)\) — true/population risk
  • \(\hat{R}_n(h)\) — empirical risk
  • \(\text{VC}(\mathcal{H})\) — VC dimension
  • \(\mathfrak{R}_n(\mathcal{H})\) — Rademacher complexity
  • \(L(y, \hat{y})\) — loss function
  • \(h^* = \arg\min_{h \in \mathcal{H}} R(h)\) — Bayes optimal in class

def vc_dimension_bound(n, d, delta=0.05):
    """
    Compute the VC generalization bound.
    
    With probability 1-delta:
    |R(h) - R_n(h)| ≤ sqrt((d*log(n/d) + log(1/delta))/n)
    """
    import numpy as np
    if n <= d:
        return np.inf
    bound = np.sqrt((d * np.log(n/d) + np.log(1/delta)) / n)
    return bound

def rademacher_complexity_empirical(X, hypothesis_class_samples=1000):
    """
    Empirical estimate of Rademacher complexity for a linear classifier.
    
    R_n(H) = E_sigma[sup_h (1/n) sum sigma_i h(x_i)]
    """
    n = len(X)
    max_correlations = []
    
    for _ in range(hypothesis_class_samples):
        # Random Rademacher variables
        sigma = np.random.choice([-1, 1], size=n)
        
        # For linear classifiers h(x) = sign(w·x), the supremum is achieved
        # by aligning w with sum sigma_i x_i
        correlation = np.abs(np.mean(sigma[:, None] * X, axis=0))
        max_correlations.append(np.linalg.norm(correlation))
    
    return np.mean(max_correlations)

Mathematical Finance: Modeling Uncertainty in Markets

financial-mathematics

Real-World Motivation

Financial mathematics provides the quantitative tools for pricing derivatives, managing risk, and optimizing investment portfolios. The field exploded after the Black-Scholes-Merton model (1973) showed how to price options using stochastic calculus. Today, mathematical finance underpins global markets, from mortgage-backed securities to cryptocurrency derivatives. The key challenge is modeling uncertainty—asset prices are random processes, and models must balance tractability with realism while respecting no-arbitrage principles.

Definitions

  • Stochastic Process: A collection of random variables \(\{X_t\}_{t \geq 0}\) indexed by time.
  • Wiener Process (Brownian Motion): A continuous-time stochastic process \(W_t\) with \(W_0 = 0\), independent increments, and \(W_t - W_s \sim \mathcal{N}(0, t-s)\).
  • Geometric Brownian Motion: \(dS_t = \mu S_t dt + \sigma S_t dW_t\), modeling stock prices with drift \(\mu\) and volatility \(\sigma\).
  • Martingale: A process where \(\mathbb{E}[X_t | \mathcal{F}_s] = X_s\) for \(s \leq t\) (fair game).
  • Equivalent Martingale Measure (EMM): A probability measure \(Q\) equivalent to \(P\) under which discounted asset prices are martingales.
  • Arbitrage: A trading strategy with zero initial cost, no risk of loss, and positive probability of profit.

Theorem: Fundamental Theorem of Asset Pricing

First Fundamental Theorem: A market model is arbitrage-free if and only if there exists at least one equivalent martingale measure.

Second Fundamental Theorem: An arbitrage-free market is complete (every contingent claim is replicable) if and only if the equivalent martingale measure is unique.

Black-Scholes Formula: For a European call option with strike \(K\), maturity \(T\), on stock with price \(S\), volatility \(\sigma\), and risk-free rate \(r\):

\[C = S\Phi(d_1) - Ke^{-rT}\Phi(d_2)\]

where \(d_1 = \frac{\ln(S/K) + (r + \sigma^2/2)T}{\sigma\sqrt{T}}\), \(d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{T}\), and \(\Phi\) is the standard normal CDF.

Proof Sketch (Risk-Neutral Pricing)

  1. Girsanov's Theorem: Change measure from \(P\) to \(Q\) such that the discounted stock \(\tilde{S}_t = e^{-rt}S_t\) is a martingale.
  2. Under \(Q\): \(dS_t = rS_t dt + \sigma S_t d\tilde{W}_t\), where \(\tilde{W}_t\) is \(Q\)-Brownian motion.
  3. Martingale representation: The option payoff \(H\) can be replicated by a self-financing portfolio.
  4. Price: \(V_t = e^{-r(T-t)}\mathbb{E}_Q[H | \mathcal{F}_t]\).
  5. For European call \(H = (S_T - K)^+\), evaluate the expectation to get the closed form.

Notation Guide

  • \(S_t\) — asset price at time \(t\)
  • \(W_t\) — Wiener process (Brownian motion)
  • \(\mu\) — drift (expected return)
  • \(\sigma\) — volatility
  • \(r\) — risk-free interest rate
  • \(Q\) — risk-neutral measure
  • \(\tilde{S}_t = e^{-rt}S_t\) — discounted price
  • \(\Phi(x)\) — standard normal CDF
  • \(dW_t\) — Itô differential

from scipy.stats import norm
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def black_scholes_call(S, K, T, r, sigma):
    """
    Black-Scholes formula for European call option.
    
    Parameters:
    S: current stock price
    K: strike price
    T: time to maturity (in years)
    r: risk-free interest rate
    sigma: volatility
    """
    d1 = (np.log(S / K) + (r + 0.5 * sigma**2) * T) / (sigma * np.sqrt(T))
    d2 = d1 - sigma * np.sqrt(T)
    
    call_price = S * norm.cdf(d1) - K * np.exp(-r * T) * norm.cdf(d2)
    
    # Greeks
    delta = norm.cdf(d1)
    gamma = norm.pdf(d1) / (S * sigma * np.sqrt(T))
    theta = -(S * norm.pdf(d1) * sigma) / (2 * np.sqrt(T)) - r * K * np.exp(-r * T) * norm.cdf(d2)
    vega = S * norm.pdf(d1) * np.sqrt(T)
    
    return {
        'price': call_price,
        'delta': delta,
        'gamma': gamma,
        'theta': theta,
        'vega': vega,
        'd1': d1,
        'd2': d2
    }

def simulate_gbm(S0, mu, sigma, T, n_paths=1000, n_steps=252):
    """
    Simulate Geometric Brownian Motion paths.
    
    dS_t = mu * S_t * dt + sigma * S_t * dW_t
    """
    dt = T / n_steps
    paths = np.zeros((n_paths, n_steps + 1))
    paths[:, 0] = S0
    
    for t in range(1, n_steps + 1):
        Z = np.random.standard_normal(n_paths)
        paths[:, t] = paths[:, t-1] * np.exp((mu - 0.5 * sigma**2) * dt + sigma * np.sqrt(dt) * Z)
    
    return paths

def monte_carlo_option_price(S0, K, T, r, sigma, n_sims=100000, option_type='call'):
    """
    Monte Carlo option pricing under risk-neutral measure.
    """
    # Under Q: drift = r
    ST = S0 * np.exp((r - 0.5 * sigma**2) * T + sigma * np.sqrt(T) * np.random.standard_normal(n_sims))
    
    if option_type == 'call':
        payoffs = np.maximum(ST - K, 0)
    else:
        payoffs = np.maximum(K - ST, 0)
    
    price = np.exp(-r * T) * np.mean(payoffs)
    stderr = np.exp(-r * T) * np.std(payoffs) / np.sqrt(n_sims)
    
    return price, stderr

Game Theory: Strategic Interaction and Equilibrium

game-theory

Real-World Motivation

Game theory studies strategic interactions where the outcome for each participant depends on the choices of all. From auction design and market competition to nuclear deterrence and evolutionary biology, game theory provides a mathematical language for analyzing rational decision-making. John von Neumann and Oskar Morgenstern laid the foundations in 1944, and John Nash's equilibrium concept (1950) revolutionized economics. Today, game theory informs policy decisions, algorithm design, and understanding of cooperation and conflict.

Definitions

  • Normal Form Game: A tuple \(G = (N, (A_i)_{i \in N}, (u_i)_{i \in N})\) where \(N\) is the set of players, \(A_i\) is player \(i\)'s action set, and \(u_i: A \to \mathbb{R}\) is the payoff function.
  • Strategy: A complete plan of action. Pure strategy selects one action; mixed strategy is a probability distribution over actions.
  • Nash Equilibrium: A strategy profile \(\sigma^* = (\sigma_1^*, \ldots, \sigma_n^*)\) where no player can benefit by unilaterally deviating: \(u_i(\sigma_i^*, \sigma_{-i}^*) \geq u_i(\sigma_i, \sigma_{-i}^*)\) for all \(\sigma_i\).
  • Dominant Strategy: A strategy that is optimal regardless of what others do.
  • Pareto Optimality: An outcome where no player can be made better off without making another worse off.
  • Extensive Form Game: A game represented as a tree, capturing sequential moves and information sets.

Theorem: Nash's Existence Theorem

Theorem: Every finite normal-form game has at least one Nash equilibrium in mixed strategies.

Proof Outline:

  1. Define the best-response correspondence \(BR_i(\sigma_{-i}) = \arg\max_{\sigma_i} u_i(\sigma_i, \sigma_{-i})\).
  2. The combined correspondence \(BR(\sigma) = (BR_1(\sigma_{-1}), \ldots, BR_n(\sigma_{-n}))\) maps the strategy simplex to itself.
  3. By Kakutani's fixed-point theorem (convex-valued, upper hemicontinuous correspondence on a compact convex set), \(BR\) has a fixed point.
  4. A fixed point of \(BR\) is a Nash equilibrium.

Notation Guide

  • \(N = \{1, \ldots, n\}\) — set of players
  • \(A_i\) — action set for player \(i\)
  • \(A = A_1 \times \cdots \times A_n\) — set of action profiles
  • \(\sigma_i\) — mixed strategy for player \(i\)
  • \(\sigma_{-i}\) — strategies of all players except \(i\)
  • \(\Delta(A_i)\) — set of probability distributions over \(A_i\)
  • \(u_i: A \to \mathbb{R}\) — payoff function
  • \(BR_i\) — best-response correspondence

def find_nash_equilibrium_2x2(payoff_matrix_A, payoff_matrix_B):
    """
    Find mixed strategy Nash equilibrium for 2x2 game.
    
    Player A plays row, Player B plays column.
    """
    # For 2x2 game, solve for indifference conditions
    # Player A mixes with probability p on action 0
    # Player B mixes with probability q on action 0
    
    a00, a01 = payoff_matrix_A[0]
    a10, a11 = payoff_matrix_A[1]
    b00, b01 = payoff_matrix_B[0, 0], payoff_matrix_B[1, 0]
    b10, b11 = payoff_matrix_B[0, 1], payoff_matrix_B[1, 1]
    
    # Player B's mix makes A indifferent
    # p*(q*a00 + (1-q)*a01) + (1-p)*(q*a10 + (1-q)*a11)
    # Solve: q*a00 + (1-q)*a01 = q*a10 + (1-q)*a11
    if a00 - a01 - a10 + a11 != 0:
        q = (a11 - a01) / (a00 - a01 - a10 + a11)
    else:
        q = 0.5  # Any q works
    
    # Player A's mix makes B indifferent
    if b00 - b10 - b01 + b11 != 0:
        p = (b11 - b01) / (b00 - b10 - b01 + b11)
    else:
        p = 0.5
    
    # Clip to valid probabilities
    p = np.clip(p, 0, 1)
    q = np.clip(q, 0, 1)
    
    return {'p': p, 'q': q, 'A_strategy': [p, 1-p], 'B_strategy': [q, 1-q]}

Algebraic Geometry: From Equations to Spaces

algebraic-geometry

Historical Context

Algebraic geometry has its roots in the study of geometric objects defined by polynomial equations, dating back to Descartes' coordinate geometry. The field underwent revolutionary transformations in the 20th century: Italian geometers developed intuitive foundations, Weil and Zariski introduced rigorous algebraic methods, Serre brought in sheaf theory, and Grothendieck's scheme theory unified number theory and geometry. Today, algebraic geometry interacts with physics (string theory), cryptography (elliptic curves), and computer science (computational algebraic geometry).

Definitions

  • Affine Variety: For a set of polynomials \(S \subseteq k[x_1, \ldots, x_n]\), \(V(S) = \{x \in \mathbb{A}^n : f(x) = 0 \text{ for all } f \in S\}\).
  • Coordinate Ring: For a variety \(V\), \(k[V] = k[x_1, \ldots, x_n]/I(V)\), where \(I(V)\) is the ideal of polynomials vanishing on \(V\).
  • Projective Space: \(\mathbb{P}^n = (\mathbb{A}^{n+1} \setminus \{0\})/\sim\) where \(x \sim \lambda x\) for \(\lambda \in k^*\).
  • Sheaf: A tool for tracking locally defined algebraic data on a topological space.
  • Scheme: A locally ringed space \((X, \mathcal{O}_X)\) that locally looks like \(\text{Spec}(R)\).
  • Birational Equivalence: Two varieties are birationally equivalent if they have isomorphic function fields.

Theorem: Bézout's Theorem

Theorem: Let \(C\) and \(D\) be projective plane curves of degrees \(m\) and \(n\) respectively, with no common component. Then counting with multiplicity:

\[|C \cap D| = m \cdot n\]

More generally, in \(\mathbb{P}^n\), if \(n\) hypersurfaces of degrees \(d_1, \ldots, d_n\) intersect transversally, they meet in exactly \(d_1 \cdots d_n\) points.

Proof Sketch

  1. Intersection multiplicity: Define \(i_P(C, D)\) using the length of the local ring \(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^2, P}/(f, g)\).
  2. Global count: Use the fact that the Hilbert polynomial of the coordinate ring of \(C \cap D\) has degree 0 and leading coefficient equal to the intersection number.
  3. Alternative (cohomological): Apply the Riemann-Roch theorem or use the fact that the cup product in cohomology counts intersections.

Notation Guide

  • \(\mathbb{A}^n\) — affine \(n\)-space
  • \(\mathbb{P}^n\) — projective \(n\)-space
  • \(V(I)\) — variety of ideal \(I\)
  • \(I(X)\) — ideal of variety \(X\)
  • \(k[X]\) — coordinate ring
  • \(k(X)\) — function field
  • \(\text{Spec}(R)\) — spectrum of prime ideals
  • \(\mathcal{O}_X\) — structure sheaf

Analytic Number Theory: The Distribution of Primes

number-theory

Historical Context

Analytic number theory applies methods from analysis—complex analysis, Fourier analysis, and asymptotic methods—to study properties of integers. The field began with Euler's product formula (1737) connecting primes to the zeta function. Dirichlet (1837) used L-functions to prove infinitely many primes in arithmetic progressions. Riemann's 1859 paper introduced the zeta function's complex-analytic properties and the famous hypothesis. The prime number theorem (proved independently by Hadamard and de la Vallée Poussin in 1896) stands as one of the great achievements of 19th-century mathematics.

Definitions

  • Riemann Zeta Function: \(\zeta(s) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n^s} = \prod_p \frac{1}{1-p^{-s}}\) for \(\text{Re}(s) > 1\), analytically continued to \(\mathbb{C} \setminus \{1\}\).
  • Prime Counting Function: \(\pi(x) = \#\{p \leq x : p \text{ prime}\}\).
  • Logarithmic Integral: \(\text{li}(x) = \int_2^x \frac{dt}{\ln t}\).
  • Dirichlet Character: A completely multiplicative function \(\chi: \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{C}\) that is periodic and vanishes on non-coprime integers.
  • Dirichlet L-function: \(L(s, \chi) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{\chi(n)}{n^s}\).
  • Möbius Function: \(\mu(n) = (-1)^k\) if \(n\) is a product of \(k\) distinct primes, 0 if \(n\) has a squared factor.

Theorem: The Prime Number Theorem

Theorem: \(\displaystyle\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{\pi(x)}{x/\ln x} = 1\)

Equivalently: \(\pi(x) \sim \text{li}(x) \sim \frac{x}{\ln x}\)

Error Term: Under the Riemann Hypothesis: \(|\pi(x) - \text{li}(x)| = O(\sqrt{x} \ln x)\)

Proof Sketch (Newman/Zagier's Short Proof)

  1. Contour integration: Consider the integral \(\int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty} \frac{x^s}{s(s+1)} \left(-\frac{\zeta'(s)}{\zeta(s)}\right) ds\).
  2. Explicit formula: Relate this to \(\psi(x) = \sum_{n \leq x} \Lambda(n)\) (Chebyshev function).
  3. Analytic continuation: Use that \(\zeta(s)\) has no zeros on \(\text{Re}(s) = 1\).
  4. Tauberian theorem: Show \(\psi(x) \sim x\) implies \(\pi(x) \sim x/\ln x\).

Notation Guide

  • \(\zeta(s)\) — Riemann zeta function
  • \(\pi(x)\) — prime counting function
  • \(\psi(x)\) — Chebyshev function \(\sum_{p^k \leq x} \ln p\)
  • \(\vartheta(x)\) — first Chebyshev function \(\sum_{p \leq x} \ln p\)
  • \(\Lambda(n)\) — von Mangoldt function (\(\ln p\) if \(n = p^k\), else 0)
  • \(\mu(n)\) — Möbius function
  • \(\varphi(n)\) — Euler's totient function
  • \(f(x) \sim g(x)\) — asymptotic equivalence: \(\lim f(x)/g(x) = 1\)

Harmonic Analysis: Decomposing Functions into Waves

harmonic-analysis

Historical Context

Harmonic analysis originated with Fourier's 1807 discovery that periodic functions can be decomposed into sums of sines and cosines. This insight revolutionized physics, engineering, and eventually mathematics itself. The field expanded to study representations of groups, abstract Fourier transforms on locally compact groups, and applications to partial differential equations. Modern harmonic analysis includes wavelets, time-frequency analysis, and deep connections to ergodic theory, number theory, and geometric measure theory.

Definitions

  • Fourier Transform: For \(f \in L^1(\mathbb{R}^n)\), \(\hat{f}(\xi) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^n} f(x) e^{-2\pi i x \cdot \xi} dx\).
  • Fourier Series: For periodic \(f\), \(f(x) = \sum_{n \in \mathbb{Z}} \hat{f}(n) e^{2\pi i n x}\) where \(\hat{f}(n) = \int_0^1 f(x) e^{-2\pi i n x} dx\).
  • Convolution: \((f * g)(x) = \int f(y) g(x-y) dy\).
  • Plancherel's Theorem: The Fourier transform extends to a unitary operator on \(L^2\): \(\|\hat{f}\|_2 = \|f\|_2\).
  • Uncertainty Principle: A function and its Fourier transform cannot both be sharply localized: \(\|xf\|_2 \|\xi\hat{f}\|_2 \geq \frac{1}{4\pi}\|f\|_2^2\).
  • Hardy Space: \(H^p(\mathbb{R}^n)\) consists of functions with controlled maximal functions or atomic decompositions.

Theorem: The Fourier Inversion Theorem

Theorem: If \(f \in L^1(\mathbb{R}^n)\) and \(\hat{f} \in L^1(\mathbb{R}^n)\), then:

\[f(x) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^n} \hat{f}(\xi) e^{2\pi i x \cdot \xi} d\xi\]

almost everywhere. In particular, \(f\) is continuous and the equality holds pointwise.

Plancherel's Theorem: The Fourier transform extends uniquely to a unitary isomorphism on \(L^2(\mathbb{R}^n)\).

Proof Sketch

  1. Approximate identity: Use a Gaussian approximate identity \(\phi_\epsilon(x) = \epsilon^{-n} e^{-\pi|x|^2/\epsilon^2}\).
  2. Key identity: \(\int f(x) \hat{g}(x) dx = \int \hat{f}(\xi) g(\xi) d\xi\) (proved by Fubini).
  3. Gaussian calculation: The Fourier transform of a Gaussian is a Gaussian.
  4. Limit: As \(\epsilon \to 0\), \(\phi_\epsilon * f \to f\) and the right side gives the inversion formula.

Notation Guide

  • \(\hat{f}(\xi)\) or \(\mathcal{F}[f](\xi)\) — Fourier transform
  • \(\check{f}(x)\) — inverse Fourier transform
  • \(f * g\) — convolution
  • \(\tau_y f(x) = f(x-y)\) — translation
  • \(M_\xi f(x) = e^{2\pi i x \cdot \xi} f(x)\) — modulation
  • \(D_\lambda f(x) = \lambda^{-n/2} f(x/\lambda)\) — dilation
  • \(\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^n)\) — Schwartz space (rapidly decreasing functions)
  • \(\mathcal{S}'(\mathbb{R}^n)\) — tempered distributions

Probability Theory: Measure-Theoretic Foundations

probability-theory

Historical Context

Modern probability theory emerged from Kolmogorov's 1933 axiomatization using measure theory. This unified the treatment of discrete and continuous random variables and provided rigorous foundations for stochastic processes. The theory built on earlier work by Borel, Lebesgue, and others in measure theory and integration. Key developments include the central limit theorem (de Moivre, Laplace, Lyapunov), martingale theory (Doob), and the study of Markov processes (Markov, Kolmogorov). Today, probability theory is essential in finance, physics, machine learning, and statistics.

Definitions

  • Probability Space: A triple \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)\) where \(\Omega\) is the sample space, \(\mathcal{F}\) is a \(\sigma\)-algebra of events, and \(P\) is a probability measure.
  • Random Variable: A measurable function \(X: \Omega \to \mathbb{R}\).
  • Expectation: \(\mathbb{E}[X] = \int_\Omega X(\omega) dP(\omega)\).
  • Conditional Expectation: \(\mathbb{E}[X | \mathcal{G}]\) is the unique \(\mathcal{G}\)-measurable random variable satisfying \(\int_G \mathbb{E}[X | \mathcal{G}] dP = \int_G X dP\) for all \(G \in \mathcal{G}\).
  • Martingale: A process \((X_n)\) adapted to filtration \((\mathcal{F}_n)\) with \(\mathbb{E}[|X_n|] < \infty\) and \(\mathbb{E}[X_{n+1} | \mathcal{F}_n] = X_n\).
  • Convergence in Distribution: \(X_n \xrightarrow{d} X\) if \(\mathbb{E}[f(X_n)] \to \mathbb{E}[f(X)]\) for all bounded continuous \(f\).

Theorem: The Central Limit Theorem

Theorem (Lindeberg-Lévy): Let \(X_1, X_2, \ldots\) be i.i.d. random variables with \(\mathbb{E}[X_i] = \mu\) and \(\text{Var}(X_i) = \sigma^2 < \infty\). Then:

\[\frac{\bar{X}_n - \mu}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n (X_i - \mu)}{\sigma\sqrt{n}} \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, 1)\]

as \(n \to \infty\), where \(\bar{X}_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i\).

Characteristic Function Proof: The characteristic function of the standardized sum converges to \(e^{-t^2/2}\).

Proof Sketch

  1. Standardize: Assume \(\mu = 0\), \(\sigma = 1\) without loss of generality.
  2. Characteristic function: \(\varphi_{S_n/\sqrt{n}}(t) = \varphi_X(t/\sqrt{n})^n\).
  3. Taylor expansion: \(\varphi_X(t) = 1 - t^2/2 + o(t^2)\) since \(\mathbb{E}[X] = 0\), \(\mathbb{E}[X^2] = 1\).
  4. Limit: \(\varphi_X(t/\sqrt{n})^n = (1 - t^2/(2n) + o(1/n))^n \to e^{-t^2/2}\).
  5. Lévy's continuity theorem: Convergence of characteristic functions implies convergence in distribution.

Notation Guide

  • \((\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)\) — probability space
  • \(\mathcal{F}_n\) — filtration (increasing sequence of \(\sigma\)-algebras)
  • \(X, Y, Z\) — random variables
  • \(\mathbb{E}[X]\) — expectation
  • \(\text{Var}(X)\) — variance
  • \(\mathbb{E}[X | \mathcal{G}]\) — conditional expectation
  • \(\xrightarrow{d}\) — convergence in distribution
  • \(\xrightarrow{p}\) — convergence in probability
  • \(\xrightarrow{a.s.}\) — almost sure convergence

📜 Math History: The Development of Abstract Algebra

The development of abstract algebra represents one of the most profound shifts in mathematical thinking, transforming the subject from the study of specific number systems to the investigation of general algebraic structures. This revolution unfolded across the 19th and early 20th centuries, driven by the need to solve long-standing problems and unify disparate areas of mathematics.

The story begins with the struggle to solve polynomial equations. Mathematicians had known since Babylonian times how to solve quadratic equations, and Italian mathematicians of the Renaissance—del Ferro, Tartaglia, Cardano, and Ferrari—discovered formulas for cubic and quartic equations. But the quintic equation resisted all attempts. In 1799, Paolo Ruffini made the first substantial claim that the general quintic might be unsolvable by radicals, though his proof had gaps. It was the young Norwegian mathematician Niels Henrik Abel who, in 1824, provided the first complete proof of the unsolvability of the general quintic.

Évariste Galois, a French revolutionary who died in a duel at age 20 in 1832, transformed Abel's negative result into a positive theory of extraordinary depth. Galois discovered that the solvability of a polynomial equation depends on the structure of a group associated with its roots—the Galois group. His work, published posthumously by Liouville in 1846, introduced the concept of a group and established the foundations of what would become group theory. Galois theory unified the theory of equations with the emerging theory of permutations, showing that abstract structural properties determine concrete solvability.

Meanwhile, other strands of algebra were developing. Carl Friedrich Gauss studied quadratic forms and modular arithmetic, introducing the ring of integers modulo n. In England, George Boole developed algebraic logic, while Arthur Cayley and James Joseph Sylvester pioneered the theory of matrices and invariant theory. The concept of an abstract group gradually emerged from these diverse sources: permutation groups (Cauchy, Galois), groups of geometric transformations (Klein), and groups of numbers under addition or multiplication.

The modern axiomatic approach crystallized in the work of Richard Dedekind, Heinrich Weber, and especially David Hilbert. Dedekind introduced the concepts of ideals and modules, while Weber's 1895 Lehrbuch der Algebra presented field theory in a form recognizable today. Emmy Noether, working in Göttingen in the 1920s, completed the transformation to abstract algebra. Her famous declaration "Es steht alles schon bei Dedekind" (It's all already in Dedekind) understated her own revolutionary contributions. Noether's abstract approach—studying rings, ideals, and modules in full generality, unencumbered by specific representations—set the pattern for twentieth-century algebra.

Emil Artin, Bartel van der Waerden, and others spread Noether's vision. Van der Waerden's 1930 Moderne Algebra, based on lectures by Noether and Artin, became the definitive textbook, organizing the subject around the now-standard structures: groups, rings, fields, and modules. The abstract approach proved extraordinarily fruitful, providing the language for algebraic geometry, algebraic number theory, and eventually much of modern mathematics. What began as a theory of solving equations became a theory of structure itself—a transformation that exemplifies the power of mathematical abstraction.

IMO Shortlist Problem (Algebra)

Problem: Let \(n\) be a positive integer. Find all functions \(f: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}\) such that for all real numbers \(x\) and \(y\):

\[f(x + f(y)) = f(x) + y^n\]

💡 Hint

First, show that \(f\) is injective. Then find \(f(0)\) by setting appropriate values. Consider what happens when you apply \(f\) twice to both sides. For even \(n\), be careful about the range of \(y^n\).

✅ Solution

Step 1: Show \(f\) is injective.

Suppose \(f(a) = f(b)\). Then for any \(x\):

\[f(x) + a^n = f(x + f(a)) = f(x + f(b)) = f(x) + b^n\]

Thus \(a^n = b^n\). For odd \(n\), this implies \(a = b\). For even \(n\), we have \(a = \pm b\), and need additional work.

Step 2: Find \(f(0)\).

Set \(x = 0\): \(f(f(y)) = f(0) + y^n\).

Set \(y = 0\): \(f(x + f(0)) = f(x)\).

By injectivity (for appropriate \(n\)), \(f(0) = 0\).

Step 3: Apply \(f\) to both sides of the original equation.

\[f(f(x + f(y))) = f(f(x) + y^n)\]

Using \(f(f(z)) = z^n\):

\[(x + f(y))^n = f(f(x) + y^n)\]

Step 4: For odd \(n\), try \(f(x) = cx\).

Substituting: \(c(x + cy) = cx + y^n\), so \(c^2 y = y^n\).

This requires \(n = 1\) and \(c^2 = 1\), so \(c = \pm 1\).

Answer:

  • For \(n = 1\): \(f(x) = x\) and \(f(x) = -x\) are solutions.
  • For odd \(n > 1\): No solutions exist.
  • For even \(n\): No solutions exist (since \(y^n \geq 0\) but the left side must achieve all values).

The Hopf Fibration: Mapping Spheres to Spheres

The Hopf fibration is a remarkable geometric construction discovered by Heinz Hopf in 1931. It describes a continuous mapping from the 3-sphere \(S^3\) to the 2-sphere \(S^2\) where each point on \(S^2\) has a fiber that is a circle \(S^1\). This was the first example of a non-trivial fiber bundle and showed that \(\pi_3(S^2) = \mathbb{Z}\), contradicting the intuition that higher homotopy groups of spheres might be trivial.

The visualization below shows stereographic projections of Hopf fibers. Each fiber is a circle in \(S^3\) that maps to a single point on \(S^2\). The fibers are linked in an intricate way—any two distinct fibers are linked exactly once, like two rings in a chain.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from mpl_toolkits.mplot3d import Axes3D
from matplotlib.colors import hsv_to_rgb

def hopf_fibration_visualization():
    """
    Visualize the Hopf fibration.
    
    The Hopf map h: S³ → S² is defined by:
    h(z₁, z₂) = (2Re(z₁*conj(z₂)), 2Im(z₁*conj(z₂)), |z₁|² - |z₂|²)
    
    where (z₁, z₂) ∈ C² with |z₁|² + |z₂|² = 1 (i.e., S³ ⊂ C²).
    """
    fig = plt.figure(figsize=(14, 6))
    
    # Create subplots
    ax1 = fig.add_subplot(121, projection='3d')
    ax2 = fig.add_subplot(122, projection='3d')
    
    # Parameters
    n_fibers = 8
    n_points = 200
    
    # Generate fibers for different points on S²
    theta_vals = np.linspace(0, np.pi, n_fibers)
    phi_vals = np.linspace(0, 2*np.pi, n_fibers, endpoint=False)
    
    colors = plt.cm.hsv(np.linspace(0, 1, n_fibers * n_fibers))
    color_idx = 0
    
    for theta in theta_vals[::2]:  # Sample subset for clarity
        for phi in phi_vals[::2]:
            # Target point on S²
            x = np.sin(theta) * np.cos(phi)
            y = np.sin(theta) * np.sin(phi)
            z = np.cos(theta)
            
            # Generate the fiber (circle in S³)
            t = np.linspace(0, 2*np.pi, n_points)
            
            # Parametrize the fiber
            # For point (x,y,z) on S², the fiber consists of (z₁, z₂) such that h(z₁,z₂) = (x,y,z)
            sqrt_factor = np.sqrt((1 + z) / 2)
            
            if sqrt_factor > 1e-10:
                z1 = sqrt_factor * np.exp(1j * (t + phi))
                z2 = ((x + 1j*y) / (2*sqrt_factor)) * np.exp(1j*t)
            else:
                # South pole special case
                z1 = np.zeros_like(t)
                z2 = np.exp(1j*t)
            
            # Stereographic projection from S³ to R³
            # Project from (0,0,0,1) onto the hyperplane w=0
            w = np.real(z1)
            x_s3 = np.imag(z1)
            y_s3 = np.real(z2)
            z_s3 = np.imag(z2)
            
            # Stereographic projection
            denom = 1 - w
            x_proj = x_s3 / denom
            y_proj = y_s3 / denom
            z_proj = z_s3 / denom
            
            # Plot the fiber
            ax1.plot(x_proj, y_proj, z_proj, 
                    color=colors[color_idx % len(colors)], alpha=0.7, linewidth=1.5)
            color_idx += 1
    
    ax1.set_title('Hopf Fibers (Stereographic Projection)', fontsize=12)
    ax1.set_xlabel('X')
    ax1.set_ylabel('Y')
    ax1.set_zlabel('Z')
    
    # Second visualization: Show the base S² with fiber colors
    u = np.linspace(0, 2*np.pi, 50)
    v = np.linspace(0, np.pi, 50)
    u, v = np.meshgrid(u, v)
    
    x_sphere = np.sin(v) * np.cos(u)
    y_sphere = np.sin(v) * np.sin(u)
    z_sphere = np.cos(v)
    
    ax2.plot_surface(x_sphere, y_sphere, z_sphere, alpha=0.3, cmap='coolwarm')
    
    # Mark some base points
    for theta in theta_vals[::2]:
        for phi in phi_vals[::2]:
            x = np.sin(theta) * np.cos(phi)
            y = np.sin(theta) * np.sin(phi)
            z = np.cos(theta)
            ax2.scatter([x], [y], [z], color='red', s=30)
    
    ax2.set_title('Base Space S² (Fiber Base Points)', fontsize=12)
    ax2.set_xlabel('X')
    ax2.set_ylabel('Y')
    ax2.set_zlabel('Z')
    
    plt.tight_layout()
    plt.savefig('hopf_fibration.png', dpi=150, bbox_inches='tight')
    plt.show()
    
    print("Hopf fibration visualization saved!")
    print(f"\nKey properties:")
    print(f"  - Each fiber is a circle S¹")
    print(f"  - Fibers are linked: any two distinct fibers link once")
    print(f"  - π₃(S²) = Z, generated by the Hopf map")

def torus_knot_fibers():
    """
    Alternative visualization: Hopf fibers as torus knots.
    
    Each fiber corresponds to a (1,1) curve on a torus.
    """
    fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(10, 10), subplot_kw=dict(projection='3d'))
    
    n_fibers = 5
    n_points = 500
    
    for k in range(n_fibers):
        t = np.linspace(0, 2*np.pi, n_points)
        
        # Torus parameters
        R = 3  # Major radius
        r = 1  # Minor radius
        
        # (p,q) torus knot parameters for Hopf fiber
        p, q = 1, 1
        phase = 2 * np.pi * k / n_fibers
        
        # Torus knot equations
        x = (R + r * np.cos(q * t + phase)) * np.cos(p * t)
        y = (R + r * np.cos(q * t + phase)) * np.sin(p * t)
        z = r * np.sin(q * t + phase)
        
        color = plt.cm.rainbow(k / n_fibers)
        ax.plot(x, y, z, color=color, linewidth=2, alpha=0.8)
    
    ax.set_title('Hopf Fibers as Torus Knots', fontsize=14)
    ax.set_xlabel('X')
    ax.set_ylabel('Y')
    ax.set_zlabel('Z')
    
    plt.tight_layout()
    plt.savefig('hopf_torus.png', dpi=150, bbox_inches='tight')
    plt.show()

# Run visualizations
hopf_fibration_visualization()
torus_knot_fibers()
