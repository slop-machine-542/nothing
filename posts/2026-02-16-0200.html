1. Knot Theory: The Jones Polynomial

Introduction

Knot theory studies embeddings of circles in 3-dimensional space. Two knots are equivalent if one can be continuously deformed into the other without cutting. The Jones Polynomial, discovered by Vaughan Jones in 1984, revolutionized knot theory by providing a powerful invariant that distinguishes many knots.

Notation Guide

  • \\(K\\) — a knot (embedding of \\(S^1\\) into \\(S^3\\) or \\(\\mathbb{R}^3\\))
  • \\(L\\) — a link (disjoint union of knots)
  • \\(n(L)\\) — number of components in link \\(L\\)
  • \\([L]\\) — Kauffman bracket (state sum invariant)
  • \\(w(L)\\) — writhe of an oriented link diagram
  • \\(V_L(t)\\) — Jones polynomial of link \\(L\\)
  • \\(A\\) — skein variable (related to \\(t = A^{-4}\\))
The Kauffman Bracket

The Kauffman bracket \\([D]\\) is a Laurent polynomial in variable \\(A\\) defined by:

\\[[ \\bigcirc ] = 1\\]

\\[[ D \\sqcup \\bigcirc ] = (-A^2 - A^{-2})[D]\\]

\\[\\left[ \\text{[diagram]} \\right] = A\\left[ \\text{[diagram]} \\right] + A^{-1}\\left[ \\text{[diagram]} \\right]\\]

The Jones Polynomial

For an oriented link \\(L\\) with diagram \\(D\\), the Jones polynomial is:

\\[V_L(t) = ((-A)^{-3w(D)}[D])_{A = t^{-1/4}}\\]

where \\(w(D)\\) is the writhe of the diagram.

Skein Relation for Jones Polynomial

\\[t^{-1}V_{L_+}(t) - tV_{L_-}(t) = (t^{1/2} - t^{-1/2})V_{L_0}(t)\\]

where \\(L_+, L_-, L_0\\) are identical except at one crossing.

Proof Sketch: Invariance Under Reidemeister Moves

Step 1: The Kauffman bracket is invariant under Reidemeister moves II and III.

Step 2: For Reidemeister I, \\([ \\text{[diagram]} ] = (-A^3)^{\\pm 1}[ \\text{[diagram]} ]\\).

Step 3: The writhe \\(w(D)\\) changes by \\(\\pm 1\\) under Reidemeister I, so \\((-A)^{-3w(D)}[D]\\) is invariant.

Step 4: Therefore \\(V_L(t)\\) is a knot invariant. ∎

2. Representation Theory: Schur's Lemma and Character Theory

Introduction

Representation theory studies abstract algebraic structures by representing their elements as linear transformations of vector spaces. It connects abstract algebra to linear algebra, making group theory more concrete and computable.

Notation Guide

  • \\(G\\) — a group
  • \\(V, W\\) — vector spaces (representation spaces)
  • \\(\\rho: G \\to GL(V)\\) — a representation of \\(G\\)
  • \\(GL(V)\\) — general linear group (invertible linear maps on \\(V\\))
  • \\(\\chi_\\rho(g) = \\text{tr}(\\rho(g))\\) — character of representation
  • \\(\\text{Hom}_G(V, W)\\) — intertwining operators (\\(G\\)-equivariant maps)
  • \\(\\mathbb{C}[G]\\) — group algebra of \\(G\\)
Schur's Lemma

Let \\(V\\) and \\(W\\) be irreducible representations of \\(G\\) over \\(\\mathbb{C}\\), and let \\(T: V \\to W\\) be a \\(G\\)-equivariant linear map (intertwining operator).

(a) If \\(V \\not\\cong W\\), then \\(T = 0\\).

(b) If \\(V = W\\), then \\(T = \\lambda I\\) for some \\(\\lambda \\in \\mathbb{C}\\).

Proof of Schur's Lemma

Step 1: Show \\(\\ker(T)\\) is a \\(G\\)-invariant subspace of \\(V\\).

For \\(v \\in \\ker(T)\\) and \\(g \\in G\\): \\(T(\\rho_V(g)v) = \\rho_W(g)T(v) = 0\\), so \\(\\rho_V(g)v \\in \\ker(T)\\).

Step 2: Show \\(\\text{im}(T)\\) is a \\(G\\)-invariant subspace of \\(W\\).

For \\(w = T(v) \\in \\text{im}(T)\\): \\(\\rho_W(g)w = \\rho_W(g)T(v) = T(\\rho_V(g)v) \\in \\text{im}(T)\\).

Step 3: Since \\(V\\) is irreducible, \\(\\ker(T) = \\{0\\}\\) or \\(\\ker(T) = V\\).

Step 4: If \\(\\ker(T) = V\\), then \\(T = 0\\).

Step 5: If \\(\\ker(T) = \\{0\\}\\), then \\(T\\) is injective, so \\(\\text{im}(T) \\cong V\\).

Step 6: Since \\(W\\) is irreducible, \\(\\text{im}(T) = \\{0\\}\\) or \\(\\text{im}(T) = W\\).

Step 7: If \\(\\text{im}(T) = W\\), then \\(T\\) is an isomorphism, so \\(V \\cong W\\).

Step 8: For part (b), if \\(V = W\\), let \\(\\lambda\\) be an eigenvalue of \\(T\\). Then \\(T - \\lambda I\\) has non-trivial kernel, so by irreducibility, \\(T - \\lambda I = 0\\). ∎

Orthogonality Relations for Characters

For irreducible representations \\(\\rho_i, \\rho_j\\) of a finite group \\(G\\):

\\[\\frac{1}{|G|}\\sum_{g \\in G} \\chi_{\\rho_i}(g)\\overline{\\chi_{\\rho_j}(g)} = \\delta_{ij}\\]

where \\(\\delta_{ij}\\) is the Kronecker delta.

3. Ergodic Theory: Birkhoff's Ergodic Theorem

Introduction

Ergodic theory studies dynamical systems with an invariant measure. It originated in statistical mechanics but has applications throughout mathematics. Birkhoff's Ergodic Theorem is the foundational result, relating time averages to space averages.

Notation Guide

  • \\((X, \\mathcal{B}, \\mu)\\) — probability measure space
  • \\(T: X \\to X\\) — measure-preserving transformation
  • \\(\\mu(T^{-1}(A)) = \\mu(A)\\) — measure preservation
  • \\(L^1(X, \\mu)\\) — integrable functions on \\(X\\)
  • \\(A_n f(x) = \\frac{1}{n}\\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} f(T^k x)\\) — time average
  • \\(\\bar{f} = \\int_X f \\, d\\mu\\) — space average
  • \\(\\mathcal{I} = \\{A \\in \\mathcal{B} : T^{-1}(A) = A\\}\\) — invariant \\(\\sigma\\)-algebra
Birkhoff's Ergodic Theorem (1931)

Let \\(T\\) be a measure-preserving transformation on \\((X, \\mathcal{B}, \\mu)\\) and \\(f \\in L^1(X, \\mu)\\). Then:

\\[\\lim_{n \\to \\infty} \\frac{1}{n}\\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} f(T^k x) = \\mathbb{E}[f | \\mathcal{I}](x)\\]

for \\(\\mu\\)-almost every \\(x \\in X\\), where \\(\\mathbb{E}[f | \\mathcal{I}]\\) is the conditional expectation of \\(f\\) given the invariant \\(\\sigma\\)-algebra.

Ergodic Case

If \\(T\\) is ergodic (the only invariant sets have measure 0 or 1), then:

\\[\\lim_{n \\to \\infty} \\frac{1}{n}\\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} f(T^k x) = \\int_X f \\, d\\mu\\]

for \\(\\mu\\)-almost every \\(x\\).

Proof Sketch (Maximal Ergodic Theorem Approach)

Step 1: Define \\(f^* = \\limsup_{n \\to \\infty} A_n f\\) and \\(f_* = \\liminf_{n \\to \\infty} A_n f\\).

Step 2: Prove the Maximal Ergodic Theorem: For \\(f \\in L^1\\), let \\(E_\\alpha = \\{x : \\sup_{n \\geq 1} A_n f(x) > \\alpha\\}\\). Then:

\\[\\int_{E_\\alpha} f \\, d\\mu \\geq \\alpha \\mu(E_\\alpha)\\]

Step 3: Show \\(f^* = f_*\\) almost everywhere using the maximal inequality.

Step 4: Prove the limit is invariant: \\(f^* \\circ T = f^*\\) a.e.

Step 5: For ergodic \\(T\\), invariant functions are constant a.e., so \\(f^* = \\int f \\, d\\mu\\). ∎

Python Implementation

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def birkhoff_ergodic_circle_rotation():
    """
    Demonstrate Birkhoff's Ergodic Theorem with circle rotation.
    
    System: X = [0,1), T(x) = x + α mod 1 (irrational rotation)
    - T is ergodic when α is irrational
    - Time averages converge to space averages
    
    Notation:
    - T^k(x) = x + kα mod 1 (k-th iterate)
    - A_n f(x) = (1/n) Σ(k=0 to n-1) f(T^k x) (time average)
    - ∫ f dμ (space average, μ = Lebesgue measure)
    """
    print("Birkhoff's Ergodic Theorem: Circle Rotation")
    print("=" * 60)
    
    # Irrational rotation
    alpha = (np.sqrt(5) - 1) / 2  # golden ratio conjugate (irrational)
    x0 = 0.3  # Initial point
    
    # Observable: f(x) = indicator of [0, 0.5)
    def f(x):
        return (x < 0.5).astype(float)
    
    # Compute time averages
    max_n = 10000
    time_avg = np.zeros(max_n)
    partial_sum = 0
    x = x0
    
    for n in range(max_n):
        partial_sum += f(x)
        time_avg[n] = partial_sum / (n + 1)
        x = (x + alpha) % 1.0
    
    # Space average (integral)
    space_avg = 0.5  # μ([0, 0.5)) = 0.5
    
    print(f"Rotation angle α = (√5 - 1)/2 ≈ {alpha:.6f}")
    print(f"Observable: f(x) = 1_[0,0.5)(x)")
    print(f"Space average: ∫f dμ = {space_avg}")
    print(f"Time average at n={max_n}: {time_avg[-1]:.6f}")
    print(f"Difference: {abs(time_avg[-1] - space_avg):.6f}")
    
    return time_avg, space_avg

1. Control Theory: The Kalman Filter

Introduction

The Kalman filter is an optimal recursive algorithm for estimating the state of a linear dynamic system from noisy measurements. Developed by Rudolf Kalman in 1960, it is ubiquitous in navigation, signal processing, and control systems.

Notation Guide

  • \\(x_k \\in \\mathbb{R}^n\\) — state vector at time \\(k\\)
  • \\(z_k \\in \\mathbb{R}^m\\) — measurement vector at time \\(k\\)
  • \\(u_k \\in \\mathbb{R}^p\\) — control input at time \\(k\\)
  • \\(F_k\\) — state transition matrix
  • \\(B_k\\) — control input matrix
  • \\(H_k\\) — observation matrix
  • \\(Q_k\\) — process noise covariance
  • \\(R_k\\) — measurement noise covariance
  • \\(P_{k|k}\\) — error covariance matrix
  • \\(\\hat{x}_{k|k}\\) — state estimate
Kalman Filter Algorithm

Prediction Step:

\\[\\hat{x}_{k|k-1} = F_k \\hat{x}_{k-1|k-1} + B_k u_k\\]

\\[P_{k|k-1} = F_k P_{k-1|k-1} F_k^T + Q_k\\]

Update Step:

\\[\\tilde{y}_k = z_k - H_k \\hat{x}_{k|k-1}\\]

\\[S_k = H_k P_{k|k-1} H_k^T + R_k\\]

\\[K_k = P_{k|k-1} H_k^T S_k^{-1}\\]

\\[\\hat{x}_{k|k} = \\hat{x}_{k|k-1} + K_k \\tilde{y}_k\\]

\\[P_{k|k} = (I - K_k H_k) P_{k|k-1}\\]

Optimality of Kalman Filter

The Kalman filter provides the minimum mean-square error (MMSE) estimate among all linear estimators. If the noise is Gaussian, it is optimal among all estimators.

Python Implementation

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

class KalmanFilter:
    """
    Kalman Filter for linear dynamical systems.
    
    System model:
      x_k = F * x_{k-1} + B * u_k + w_k  (process)
      z_k = H * x_k + v_k                (measurement)
    
    where w_k ~ N(0, Q), v_k ~ N(0, R)
    
    Notation:
    - x: state vector
    - P: error covariance
    - F: state transition matrix
    - B: control matrix
    - H: observation matrix
    - Q: process noise covariance
    - R: measurement noise covariance
    - K: Kalman gain
    """
    
    def __init__(self, F, B, H, Q, R, x0, P0):
        self.F = F  # State transition
        self.B = B  # Control input
        self.H = H  # Observation
        self.Q = Q  # Process noise
        self.R = R  # Measurement noise
        self.x = x0  # Initial state estimate
        self.P = P0  # Initial error covariance
    
    def predict(self, u=None):
        """Prediction step (time update)."""
        # State prediction
        if u is not None:
            self.x = self.F @ self.x + self.B @ u
        else:
            self.x = self.F @ self.x
        
        # Covariance prediction
        self.P = self.F @ self.P @ self.F.T + self.Q
        
        return self.x
    
    def update(self, z):
        """Update step (measurement update)."""
        # Innovation (measurement residual)
        y_tilde = z - self.H @ self.x
        
        # Innovation covariance
        S = self.H @ self.P @ self.H.T + self.R
        
        # Kalman gain
        K = self.P @ self.H.T @ np.linalg.inv(S)
        
        # State update
        self.x = self.x + K @ y_tilde
        
        # Covariance update (Joseph form for numerical stability)
        I = np.eye(self.P.shape[0])
        self.P = (I - K @ self.H) @ self.P
        
        return self.x

def kalman_filter_1d_tracking():
    """
    1D position tracking with velocity.
    
    State: x = [position, velocity]^T
    Measurement: z = position (noisy)
    """
    print("Kalman Filter: 1D Position Tracking")
    print("=" * 60)
    
    dt = 0.1  # Time step
    
    # State transition (constant velocity model)
    F = np.array([[1, dt],
                  [0, 1]])
    
    # Control input (acceleration)
    B = np.array([[0.5 * dt**2],
                  [dt]])
    
    # Observation (measure position only)
    H = np.array([[1, 0]])
    
    # Process noise (small)
    Q = np.array([[0.01, 0],
                  [0, 0.001]])
    
    # Measurement noise
    R = np.array([[1.0]])
    
    # Initial state and covariance
    x0 = np.array([[0], [1]])  # Start at 0, velocity 1
    P0 = np.eye(2)
    
    kf = KalmanFilter(F, B, H, Q, R, x0, P0)
    
    # Simulate true trajectory and noisy measurements
    t = np.arange(0, 10, dt)
    true_pos = t + 0.1 * t**2  # True position with acceleration
    measurements = true_pos + np.random.normal(0, 1, len(t))  # Noisy
    
    # Run Kalman filter
    estimates = []
    for i, z in enumerate(measurements):
        u = np.array([[0.2]])  # Constant acceleration
        kf.predict(u)
        est = kf.update(np.array([[z]]))
        estimates.append(est[0, 0])
    
    # Calculate errors
    measurement_error = np.mean((measurements - true_pos)**2)
    estimate_error = np.mean((np.array(estimates) - true_pos)**2)
    
    print(f"Mean squared error (measurements): {measurement_error:.4f}")
    print(f"Mean squared error (Kalman filter): {estimate_error:.4f}")
    print(f"Improvement: {measurement_error/estimate_error:.2f}x better")

2. Epidemiology: The SIR Model

Introduction

The SIR model is the foundational compartmental model in epidemiology. It divides a population into three groups: Susceptible, Infectious, and Recovered, and models disease transmission through differential equations.

Notation Guide

  • \\(S(t)\\) — fraction of population susceptible
  • \\(I(t)\\) — fraction of population infectious
  • \\(R(t)\\) — fraction of population recovered/removed
  • \\(N = S + I + R\\) — total population (constant)
  • \\(\\beta\\) — transmission rate (contact rate × infection probability)
  • \\(\\gamma\\) — recovery rate (\\(1/\\gamma\\) = average infectious period)
  • \\(\\mathcal{R}_0 = \\beta/\\gamma\\) — basic reproduction number
The SIR Model Equations

\\[\\frac{dS}{dt} = -\\beta SI\\]

\\[\\frac{dI}{dt} = \\beta SI - \\gamma I\\]

\\[\\frac{dR}{dt} = \\gamma I\\]

Basic Reproduction Number \\(\\mathcal{R}_0\\)

\\[\\mathcal{R}_0 = \\frac{\\beta}{\\gamma}\\]

The epidemic threshold:

  • If \\(\\mathcal{R}_0 < 1\\): Infection dies out
  • If \\(\\mathcal{R}_0 > 1\\): Epidemic occurs
Final Size Relation

Let \\(S_\\infty = \\lim_{t \\to \\infty} S(t)\\) and \\(R_0 = S(0)\\). Then:

\\[\\ln\\left(\\frac{S_0}{S_\\infty}\\right) = \\mathcal{R}_0\\left(1 - \\frac{S_\\infty}{N}\\right)\\]

This transcendental equation determines the final epidemic size.

Derivation of Final Size Relation

Step 1: From the SIR equations:

\\[\\frac{dI}{dS} = \\frac{\\beta SI - \\gamma I}{-\\beta SI} = -1 + \\frac{\\gamma}{\\beta S} = -1 + \\frac{1}{\\mathcal{R}_0 S}\\]

Step 2: Integrate from \\(t = 0\\) to \\(t = \\infty\\):

\\[\\int_{I_0}^{I_\\infty} dI = \\int_{S_0}^{S_\\infty} \\left(-1 + \\frac{1}{\\mathcal{R}_0 S}\\right) dS\\]

Step 3: Since \\(I_\\infty = 0\\) (epidemic ends):

\\[-I_0 = -(S_\\infty - S_0) + \\frac{1}{\\mathcal{R}_0}\\ln\\frac{S_\\infty}{S_0}\\]

Step 4: With \\(I_0 + S_0 \\approx N\\) (few initially recovered):

\\[\\ln\\frac{S_0}{S_\\infty} = \\mathcal{R}_0\\left(1 - \\frac{S_\\infty}{N}\\right)\\] ∎

def sir_model(y, t, beta, gamma):
    """
    SIR model differential equations.
    
    dS/dt = -βSI
    dI/dt = βSI - γI
    dR/dt = γI
    
    Notation:
    - S: susceptible fraction
    - I: infectious fraction
    - R: recovered fraction
    - β: transmission rate
    - γ: recovery rate

    dSdt = -beta * S * I
    dIdt = beta * S * I - gamma * I
    dRdt = gamma * I
    
    return [dSdt, dIdt, dRdt]

3. Queueing Theory: The M/M/1 Queue

Introduction

Queueing theory models waiting lines and service systems. The M/M/1 queue is the simplest non-trivial model: Poisson arrivals, exponential service times, and a single server. It provides fundamental insights into congestion and delay.

Notation Guide

  • \\(\\lambda\\) — arrival rate (customers per unit time)
  • \\(\\mu\\) — service rate (customers per unit time)
  • \\(\\rho = \\lambda/\\mu\\) — traffic intensity (utilization)
  • \\(P_n\\) — steady-state probability of \\(n\\) customers in system
  • \\(L = \\mathbb{E}[N]\\) — expected number in system
  • \\(L_q\\) — expected number in queue
  • \\(W\\) — expected time in system
  • \\(W_q\\) — expected waiting time in queue
Steady-State Distribution (M/M/1)

For \\(\\rho < 1\\) (stability condition):

\\[P_n = (1 - \\rho)\\rho^n, \\quad n = 0, 1, 2, \\ldots\\]

This is a geometric distribution with parameter \\(1 - \\rho\\).

Little's Law

For any stable queueing system in steady state:

\\[L = \\lambda W\\]

\\[L_q = \\lambda W_q\\]

Relating average number in system to average time in system.

M/M/1 Performance Measures

\\[L = \\frac{\\rho}{1 - \\rho} = \\frac{\\lambda}{\\mu - \\lambda}\\]

\\[L_q = \\frac{\\rho^2}{1 - \\rho}\\]

\\[W = \\frac{1}{\\mu - \\lambda}\\]

\\[W_q = \\frac{\\lambda}{\\mu(\\mu - \\lambda)}\\]

Derivation of Steady-State Probabilities

Step 1: Balance equations (rate in = rate out for each state):

\\[\\lambda P_0 = \\mu P_1\\]

\\[(\\lambda + \\mu)P_n = \\lambda P_{n-1} + \\mu P_{n+1}, \\quad n \\geq 1\\]

Step 2: From first equation: \\(P_1 = (\\lambda/\\mu)P_0 = \\rho P_0\\)

Step 3: By induction, assume \\(P_n = \\rho^n P_0\\). Then:

\\[P_{n+1} = \\frac{(\\lambda + \\mu)P_n - \\lambda P_{n-1}}{\\mu} = \\frac{(\\lambda + \\mu)\\rho^n - \\lambda \\rho^{n-1}}{\\mu}P_0 = \\rho^{n+1}P_0\\]

Step 4: Normalization: \\(\\sum_{n=0}^\\infty P_n = 1\\)

\\[P_0 \\sum_{n=0}^\\infty \\rho^n = P_0 \\frac{1}{1-\\rho} = 1\\]

\\[P_0 = 1 - \\rho\\]

Step 5: Therefore \\(P_n = (1-\\rho)\\rho^n\\). ∎

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

class MM1Queue:
    """
    M/M/1 Queue model.
    
    Notation:
    - λ (lambda): arrival rate
    - μ (mu): service rate
    - ρ = λ/μ: traffic intensity (must be < 1 for stability)
    - P_n = (1-ρ)ρ^n: steady-state probability of n customers
    """
    
    def __init__(self, lam, mu):
        if lam >= mu:
            raise ValueError("System unstable: λ must be < μ")
        self.lam = lam
        self.mu = mu
        self.rho = lam / mu
    
    def Pn(self, n):
        """Steady-state probability of n customers in system."""
        return (1 - self.rho) * (self.rho ** n)
    
    def expected_in_system(self):
        """L = expected number of customers in system."""
        return self.rho / (1 - self.rho)
    
    def expected_in_queue(self):
        """L_q = expected number of customers in queue."""
        return self.rho**2 / (1 - self.rho)
    
    def expected_time_in_system(self):
        """W = expected time in system (Little's Law: L = λW)."""
        return 1 / (self.mu - self.lam)
    
    def expected_wait_time(self):
        """W_q = expected waiting time in queue."""
        return self.lam / (self.mu * (self.mu - self.lam))
    
    def prob_wait(self, t):
        """Probability that waiting time in queue exceeds t."""
        return self.rho * np.exp(-self.mu * (1 - self.rho) * t)

    t = 0

    next_departure = float('inf')
    queue = 0
    total_customers = 0
    total_wait_time = 0
    
    # Statistics
    arrivals = []
    departures = []
    
    while t < T:
        if next_arrival <= next_departure:
            # Arrival event
            t = next_arrival
            arrivals.append(t)
            
            if queue == 0:
                # Start service immediately
                next_departure = t + np.random.exponential(1/mu)
            
            queue += 1
            next_arrival = t + np.random.exponential(1/lam)
            
        else:
            # Departure event
            t = next_departure
            departures.append(t)
            queue -= 1
            total_customers += 1
            
            if queue > 0:
                next_departure = t + np.random.exponential(1/mu)
            else:
                next_departure = float('inf')
    
    # Calculate average wait time
    wait_times = [d - a for a, d in zip(arrivals[:len(departures)], departures)]
    avg_wait = np.mean(wait_times) if wait_times else 0
    
    return avg_wait

1. Ramsey Theory: The Party Problem

Introduction

Ramsey theory states that complete disorder is impossible. In any sufficiently large system, some structure must emerge. The classic "party problem" asks: how many people must be at a party to guarantee that either \\(n\\) mutual acquaintances or \\(n\\) mutual strangers exist?

Notation Guide

  • \\(R(s, t)\\) — Ramsey number: minimum \\(n\\) such that any 2-coloring of \\(K_n\\) edges contains a red \\(K_s\\) or blue \\(K_t\\)
  • \\(K_n\\) — complete graph on \\(n\\) vertices
  • \\(\\binom{n}{k}\\) — binomial coefficient
  • \\(r(G, H)\\) — Ramsey number for graphs \\(G\\) and \\(H\\)
Ramsey's Theorem (1930)

For any positive integers \\(s\\) and \\(t\\), there exists a least positive integer \\(R(s, t)\\) such that every edge-coloring of the complete graph \\(K_{R(s,t)}\\) with red and blue contains either:

  • A red complete subgraph \\(K_s\\), or
  • A blue complete subgraph \\(K_t\\)
Upper Bound (Erdős–Szekeres)

\\[R(s, t) \\leq \\binom{s+t-2}{s-1}\\]

Special case: \\(R(s, s) \\leq \\binom{2s-2}{s-1} \\sim \\frac{4^{s-1}}{\\sqrt{\\pi(s-1)}}\\)

Known Ramsey Numbers
  • \\(R(3, 3) = 6\\) (the party problem)
  • \\(R(4, 4) = 18\\)
  • \\(R(5, 5)\\) is unknown (bounds: \\(43 \\leq R(5,5) \\leq 48\\))
Proof that \\(R(3, 3) = 6\\)

Upper bound (\\(R(3,3) \\leq 6\\)):

Step 1: Consider any vertex \\(v\\) in \\(K_6\\). It has 5 edges.

Step 2: By pigeonhole principle, at least \\(\\lceil 5/2 \\rceil = 3\\) edges have the same color (say red).

Step 3: Let these connect to vertices \\(a, b, c\\).

Step 4: If any edge among \\(\\{a, b, c\\}\\) is red, we have a red triangle with \\(v\\).

Step 5: If all edges among \\(\\{a, b, c\\}\\) are blue, they form a blue triangle.

Lower bound (\\(R(3,3) > 5\\)):

Step 6: The 5-cycle \\(C_5\\) with edges colored alternately has no monochromatic triangle.

Step 7: Therefore \\(R(3, 3) = 6\\). ∎

import itertools
import numpy as np

def check_ramsey(graph, s, t):
    """
    Check if a 2-colored complete graph contains red K_s or blue K_t.
    
    graph[i][j] = 1 for red edge, 0 for blue edge
    """
    n = len(graph)
    
    # Check for red K_s
    for vertices in itertools.combinations(range(n), s):
        if all(graph[i][j] == 1 for i, j in itertools.combinations(vertices, 2)):
            return True, 'red', vertices
    
    # Check for blue K_t
    for vertices in itertools.combinations(range(n), t):
        if all(graph[i][j] == 0 for i, j in itertools.combinations(vertices, 2)):
            return True, 'blue', vertices
    
    return False, None, None

2. Surreal Numbers: Conway's Number System

Introduction

Surreal numbers, invented by John Conway, form a proper class of numbers that includes all real numbers, all ordinals, and infinitesimals. They are constructed recursively from nothing, with each number defined as a pair of sets of previously constructed numbers.

Notation Guide

  • \\(\\{L | R\\}\\) — surreal number with left set \\(L\\) and right set \\(R\\)
  • \\(x \\leq y\\) — \\(x\\) is less than or equal to \\(y\\)
  • \\(x < y\\) — \\(x\\) is strictly less than \\(y\\)
  • \\(\\mathbf{On}\\) — the class of ordinal numbers
  • \\(\\omega\\) — the first infinite ordinal
  • \\(\\varepsilon\\) — infinitesimal numbers
  • \\(\\mathbf{No}\\) — the class of surreal numbers
Construction of Surreal Numbers

If \\(L\\) and \\(R\\) are sets of surreal numbers with no element of \\(L\\) \\(\\geq\\) any element of \\(R\\), then \\(\\{L | R\\}\\) is a surreal number.

All surreal numbers are constructed in this way, starting with \\(\\{\\emptyset | \\emptyset\\} = 0\\).

Comparison Rules

For surreal numbers \\(x = \\{X_L | X_R\\}\\) and \\(y = \\{Y_L | Y_R\\}\\):

\\[x \\leq y \\iff \\text{no } x_L \\geq y \\text{ and no } y_R \\leq x\\]

\\[x < y \\iff x \\leq y \\text{ and not } y \\leq x\\]

Birthdays and Simplicity

Each surreal number has a birthday (the ordinal at which it is created). The simplest number \\(\\{L | R\\}\\) is the unique surreal with:

  • All elements of \\(L\\) strictly less than all elements of \\(R\\)
  • Earliest possible birthday
Construction of Integers

Day 0: \\(\\{\\emptyset | \\emptyset\\} = 0\\)

Day 1:

\\(\\{\\emptyset | 0\\} = -1\\) (simplest number less than 0)

\\(\\{0 | \\emptyset\\} = 1\\) (simplest number greater than 0)

Day 2:

\\(\\{\\emptyset | -1\\} = -2\\)

\\(\\{-1 | 0\\} = -\\frac{1}{2}\\)

\\(\\{0 | 1\\} = \\frac{1}{2}\\)

\\(\\{1 | \\emptyset\\} = 2\\)

Induction: All dyadic rationals appear by finite days. All reals appear by day \\(\\omega\\). ∎

    def __hash__(self):
        return id(self)
    
    def __eq__(self, other):
        return self is other

3. The Hodge Conjecture

Introduction

The Hodge Conjecture is one of the seven Millennium Prize Problems. It connects algebraic topology (cohomology) to algebraic geometry (algebraic cycles) on complex projective varieties. It asks which cohomology classes can be represented by algebraic cycles.

Notation Guide

  • \\(X\\) — a smooth complex projective variety
  • \\(H^{p,q}(X)\\) — Dolbeault cohomology (\\((p,q)\\)-forms)
  • \\(H^{2k}(X, \\mathbb{Q})\\) — rational cohomology in degree \\(2k\\)
  • \\(H^{k,k}(X) = H^{2k}(X, \\mathbb{C}) \\cap H^{k,k}(X)\\) — Hodge classes
  • \\(Z^k(X)\\) — algebraic cycles of codimension \\(k\\)
  • \\([Z]\\) — cohomology class of algebraic cycle \\(Z\\)
Hodge Decomposition

For a compact Kähler manifold \\(X\\):

\\[H^n(X, \\mathbb{C}) = \\bigoplus_{p+q=n} H^{p,q}(X)\\]

where \\(H^{p,q}(X)\\) consists of cohomology classes representable by \\((p,q)\\)-forms.

The Hodge Conjecture

Let \\(X\\) be a smooth complex projective variety. Every rational Hodge class \\(\\alpha \\in H^{k,k}(X) \\cap H^{2k}(X, \\mathbb{Q})\\) is a \\(\\mathbb{Q}\\)-linear combination of cohomology classes of algebraic cycles:

\\[\\alpha = \\sum_i q_i [Z_i]\\]

where \\(Z_i\\) are algebraic subvarieties of codimension \\(k\\) and \\(q_i \\in \\mathbb{Q}\\).

Known Cases
  • \\(k = 0\\): Trivially true
  • \\(k = 1\\): True (Lefschetz theorem on \\((1,1)\\)-classes)
  • \\(k = \\dim(X) - 1\\): True (dual to \\(k = 1\\))
  • Abelian varieties: True (Poincaré)
  • \\(k = 2, \\dim(X) = 4\\): Open (Mumford-Tate conjecture related)
Proof of Lefschetz \\((1,1)\\) Theorem

Theorem: On a smooth projective variety, every \\((1,1)\\)-class in \\(H^2(X, \\mathbb{Q})\\) is algebraic.

Step 1: Consider the exponential exact sequence:

\\[0 \\to \\mathbb{Z} \\to \\mathcal{O}_X \\xrightarrow{\\exp} \\mathcal{O}_X^* \\to 1\\]

Step 2: Take cohomology, getting:

\\[H^1(X, \\mathcal{O}_X^*) \\xrightarrow{c_1} H^2(X, \\mathbb{Z}) \\to H^2(X, \\mathcal{O}_X)\\]

Step 3: \\(H^1(X, \\mathcal{O}_X^*) = \\text{Pic}(X)\\) is the Picard group (line bundles).

Step 4: The map \\(c_1\\) sends a line bundle to its first Chern class.

Step 5: The kernel of \\(H^2(X, \\mathbb{Z}) \\to H^2(X, \\mathcal{O}_X)\\) is exactly \\(H^{1,1}(X) \\cap H^2(X, \\mathbb{Z})\\).

Step 6: By Hodge theory, every \\((1,1)\\)-class is in this kernel, hence comes from a line bundle, hence is algebraic. ∎

4. Elliptic Curves and the Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer Conjecture

Introduction

An elliptic curve is a smooth projective curve of genus 1 with a specified base point. They are central to modern number theory, cryptography, and the proof of Fermat's Last Theorem. The Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer (BSD) Conjecture relates the arithmetic of elliptic curves to their analytic properties.

Notation Guide

  • \\(E\\) — elliptic curve
  • \\(E(\\mathbb{Q})\\) — group of rational points on \\(E\\)
  • \\(r = \\text{rank}(E(\\mathbb{Q}))\\) — algebraic rank
  • \\(L(E, s)\\) — L-function of \\(E\\)
  • \\(\\text{ord}_{s=1} L(E, s)\\) — analytic rank (order of zero at \\(s = 1\\))
  • \\(\\text{Sha}(E)\\) — Tate-Shafarevich group (measures failure of local-to-global)
  • \\(\\Omega_E\\) — real period
  • \\(\\text{Reg}_E\\) — regulator
  • \\(c_p\\) — Tamagawa numbers
Weierstrass Equation

An elliptic curve over \\(\\mathbb{Q}\\) can be written:

\\[E: y^2 = x^3 + ax + b\\]

with \\(a, b \\in \\mathbb{Q}\\) and discriminant \\(\\Delta = -16(4a^3 + 27b^2) \\neq 0\\).

Mordell's Theorem

The group of rational points \\(E(\\mathbb{Q})\\) is finitely generated:

\\[E(\\mathbb{Q}) \\cong E(\\mathbb{Q})_{\\text{tors}} \\times \\mathbb{Z}^r\\]

where \\(E(\\mathbb{Q})_{\\text{tors}}\\) is the finite torsion subgroup and \\(r\\) is the rank.

The Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer Conjecture

Weak BSD: The algebraic rank equals the analytic rank:

\\[r = \\text{ord}_{s=1} L(E, s)\\]

Strong BSD: The leading coefficient:

\\[\\lim_{s \\to 1} \\frac{L(E, s)}{(s-1)^r} = \\frac{\\Omega_E \\cdot \\text{Reg}_E \\cdot |\\text{Sha}(E)| \\cdot \\prod_p c_p}{|E(\\mathbb{Q})_{\\text{tors}}|^2}\\]

Sketch: Why BSD Might Be True

Step 1: The L-function encodes information about \\(E(\\mathbb{F}_p)\\) for all primes \\(p\\).

Step 2: By the Taylor expansion at \\(s = 1\\):

\\[L(E, s) = c(s-1)^r + O((s-1)^{r+1})\\]

Step 3: The class number formula for number fields suggests a similar formula for elliptic curves.

Step 4: Coates-Wiles (1977): If \\(E\\) has complex multiplication and \\(L(E, 1) \\neq 0\\), then \\(r = 0\\).

Step 5: Gross-Zagier (1986), Kolyvagin (1988): If \\(\\text{ord}_{s=1} L(E, s) \\leq 1\\), then weak BSD holds. ∎
