Lie Algebras: The Infinitesimal Structure of Symmetry

lie-algebras

Motivation

How do we study continuous symmetries algebraically? Lie algebras provide the linearized, infinitesimal description of Lie groups—continuous groups of symmetries that appear throughout mathematics and physics. Named after Sophus Lie, who developed the theory in the late 19th century to study differential equations, Lie algebras have become fundamental tools in representation theory, particle physics, and differential geometry. The classification of simple Lie algebras is one of the most beautiful achievements in mathematics.

Definitions

  • Lie Algebra: A vector space \( \mathfrak{g} \) over a field \( \mathbb{F} \) equipped with a bilinear operation \( [\cdot, \cdot]: \mathfrak{g} \times \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{g} \) called the Lie bracket satisfying:
    • Antisymmetry: \( [X, Y] = -[Y, X] \)
    • Jacobi identity: \( [X, [Y, Z]] + [Y, [Z, X]] + [Z, [X, Y]] = 0 \)
  • Adjoint Representation: For \( X \in \mathfrak{g} \), define \( \text{ad}_X: \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{g} \) by \( \text{ad}_X(Y) = [X, Y] \).
  • Killing Form: The symmetric bilinear form \( \kappa(X, Y) = \text{tr}(\text{ad}_X \circ \text{ad}_Y) \).
  • Semisimple: A Lie algebra with no non-zero abelian ideals (equivalently, with non-degenerate Killing form).
  • Cartan Subalgebra: A nilpotent subalgebra \( \mathfrak{h} \subseteq \mathfrak{g} \) that is self-normalizing.

Theorem: The Cartan Classification of Simple Lie Algebras

Theorem (Cartan, 1894): Every finite-dimensional simple Lie algebra over \( \mathbb{C} \) is isomorphic to exactly one of:

Classical Series:

  • \( A_n = \mathfrak{sl}_{n+1}(\mathbb{C}) \) for \( n \geq 1 \) (special linear)
  • \( B_n = \mathfrak{so}_{2n+1}(\mathbb{C}) \) for \( n \geq 2 \) (odd orthogonal)
  • \( C_n = \mathfrak{sp}_{2n}(\mathbb{C}) \) for \( n \geq 3 \) (symplectic)
  • \( D_n = \mathfrak{so}_{2n}(\mathbb{C}) \) for \( n \geq 4 \) (even orthogonal)

Exceptional Algebras:

  • \( E_6, E_7, E_8, F_4, G_2 \)

Proof Sketch: The proof uses the root space decomposition. For a semisimple Lie algebra \( \mathfrak{g} \) with Cartan subalgebra \( \mathfrak{h} \), we have:

\[ \mathfrak{g} = \mathfrak{h} \oplus \bigoplus_{\alpha \in \Phi} \mathfrak{g}_\alpha \]

where \( \Phi \subseteq \mathfrak{h}^* \) is the root system. The geometry of \( \Phi \) (encoded in the Cartan matrix or Dynkin diagram) completely determines \( \mathfrak{g} \). Classification of irreducible root systems yields the list above. ∎

Notation Guide

  • \( \mathfrak{g}, \mathfrak{h} \): Lie algebras (fraktur font)
  • \( [X, Y] \): Lie bracket
  • \( \text{ad}_X \): Adjoint action
  • \( \kappa(X, Y) \): Killing form
  • \( \Phi \): Root system
  • \( \mathfrak{g}_\alpha \): Root space for root \( \alpha \)
  • \( \mathfrak{sl}_n \): Special linear Lie algebra (trace zero matrices)
  • \( \mathfrak{so}_n \): Special orthogonal Lie algebra (skew-symmetric matrices)

from itertools import combinations

def lie_bracket(X, Y):
    """Compute the Lie bracket [X, Y] = XY - YX for matrices."""
    return X @ Y - Y @ X

def killing_form(X, Y, basis):
    """
    Compute the Killing form κ(X, Y) = tr(ad_X ∘ ad_Y).
    For matrix Lie algebras, κ(X, Y) = 2n tr(XY) for sl(n).
    """
    # For sl(n), use the simplified formula
    n = X.shape[0]
    return 2 * n * np.trace(X @ Y)

def verify_jacobi_identity(X, Y, Z):
    """Verify the Jacobi identity: [X,[Y,Z]] + [Y,[Z,X]] + [Z,[X,Y]] = 0"""
    term1 = lie_bracket(X, lie_bracket(Y, Z))
    term2 = lie_bracket(Y, lie_bracket(Z, X))
    term3 = lie_bracket(Z, lie_bracket(X, Y))
    return np.allclose(term1 + term2 + term3, 0)

Category Theory: The Mathematics of Structure

category-theory

Motivation

What do groups, topological spaces, and vector spaces have in common? Category theory, introduced by Samuel Eilenberg and Saunders Mac Lane in the 1940s, provides a unified framework for studying mathematical structures and the mappings between them. Rather than focusing on what objects are made of, category theory asks how they relate to each other. This shift in perspective has revealed deep connections between seemingly unrelated areas of mathematics and has become essential in algebraic geometry, logic, and theoretical computer science.

Definitions

  • Category \( \mathcal{C} \): Consists of:
    • A class of objects \( \text{Ob}(\mathcal{C}) \)
    • For each pair of objects \( X, Y \), a set of morphisms \( \text{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(X, Y) \)
    • Composition: \( \circ: \text{Hom}(Y, Z) \times \text{Hom}(X, Y) \to \text{Hom}(X, Z) \)
    • Identity morphisms \( \text{id}_X \) for each object
    satisfying associativity and identity laws.
  • Functor: A mapping \( F: \mathcal{C} \to \mathcal{D} \) preserving composition and identities.
  • Natural Transformation: A family of morphisms \( \eta_X: F(X) \to G(X) \) such that for all \( f: X \to Y \):

    \[ \eta_Y \circ F(f) = G(f) \circ \eta_X \]

  • Universal Property: A characterization of an object by its relationship to all other objects.
  • Adjunction: Functors \( F: \mathcal{C} \to \mathcal{D} \) and \( G: \mathcal{D} \to \mathcal{C} \) with a natural isomorphism:

    \[ \text{Hom}_{\mathcal{D}}(F(X), Y) \cong \text{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(X, G(Y)) \]

Theorem: The Yoneda Lemma

Theorem (Yoneda Lemma): Let \( \mathcal{C} \) be a locally small category, \( F: \mathcal{C}^{\text{op}} \to \text{Set} \) a functor, and \( X \in \mathcal{C} \). Then:

\[ \text{Nat}(\text{Hom}_{\mathcal{C}}(-, X), F) \cong F(X) \]

where \( \text{Nat} \) denotes natural transformations. Moreover, this isomorphism is natural in both \( X \) and \( F \).

Proof Sketch: Given \( \eta: \text{Hom}(-, X) \Rightarrow F \), evaluate at \( X \) and apply to \( \text{id}_X \) to get \( \eta_X(\text{id}_X) \in F(X) \). Conversely, given \( a \in F(X) \), define \( \eta_Y(f) = F(f)(a) \) for \( f: Y \to X \). One verifies these are inverse and natural. ∎

Corollary (Yoneda Embedding): The functor \( y: \mathcal{C} \to [\mathcal{C}^{\text{op}}, \text{Set}] \) sending \( X \mapsto \text{Hom}(-, X) \) is fully faithful.

Notation Guide

  • \( \mathcal{C}, \mathcal{D} \): Categories (calligraphic font)
  • \( \text{Ob}(\mathcal{C}) \): Objects of category \( \mathcal{C} \)
  • \( \text{Hom}(X, Y) \) or \( X \to Y \): Morphisms from \( X \) to \( Y \)
  • \( \circ \): Composition of morphisms
  • \( F: \mathcal{C} \to \mathcal{D} \): Functor between categories
  • \( \eta: F \Rightarrow G \): Natural transformation
  • \( \mathcal{C}^{\text{op}} \): Opposite category (arrows reversed)
  • \( [\mathcal{C}, \mathcal{D}] \): Functor category

from typing import Callable, TypeVar, Generic
from dataclasses import dataclass
from functools import reduce

# A simple demonstration of categorical concepts in Python

T = TypeVar('T')
U = TypeVar('U')
V = TypeVar('V')

class Functor(Generic[T]):
    """Abstract base for functors."""
    
    def map(self, f: Callable[[T], U]) -> 'Functor[U]':
        raise NotImplementedError
    
    def fmap(self, f: Callable[[T], U]) -> 'Functor[U]':
        """Alias for map."""
        return self.map(f)

@dataclass
class ListFunctor(Functor[T]):
    """List is a functor."""
    value: list[T]
    
    def map(self, f: Callable[[T], U]) -> 'ListFunctor[U]':
        return ListFunctor([f(x) for x in self.value])
    
    def __repr__(self):
        return f"ListFunctor({self.value})"

@dataclass  
class Maybe(Functor[T]):
    """Maybe functor (optional value)."""
    value: T | None
    
    def map(self, f: Callable[[T], U]) -> 'Maybe[U]':
        if self.value is None:
            return Maybe(None)
        return Maybe(f(self.value))
    
    def __repr__(self):
        return f"Maybe({self.value})"

# Natural transformation: Maybe -> List
def maybe_to_list(m: Maybe[T]) -> ListFunctor[T]:
    """A natural transformation from Maybe to List."""
    if m.value is None:
        return ListFunctor([])
    return ListFunctor([m.value])

# Verify naturality square commutes
# For f: T -> U, we need: maybe_to_list(Maybe(f(x))) == ListFunctor(f)(maybe_to_list(x))
def verify_naturality(f: Callable[[T], U], x: Maybe[T]) -> bool:
    """Verify the naturality square commutes."""
    left = maybe_to_list(x.map(f))
    right = maybe_to_list(x).map(f)
    return left == right

Algebraic Number Theory: Arithmetic Beyond the Rationals

algebraic-number-theory

Motivation

How do we solve Diophantine equations like \( x^2 + y^2 = z^2 \) or \( x^3 + y^3 = z^3 \)? Algebraic number theory extends the tools of number theory beyond the integers to algebraic number fields—finite extensions of \( \mathbb{Q} \). By studying factorization in rings of integers, ideal class groups, and Galois representations, we gain powerful methods for attacking problems that are intractable over \( \mathbb{Z} \) alone. This field, pioneered by Kummer, Dedekind, and Hilbert, remains at the forefront of mathematical research with connections to the Langlands program and Fermat's Last Theorem.

Definitions

  • Number Field: A finite field extension \( K/\mathbb{Q} \), denoted \( [K:\mathbb{Q}] = n \).
  • Ring of Integers: \( \mathcal{O}_K = \{ \alpha \in K : \alpha \text{ is integral over } \mathbb{Z} \} \).
  • Discriminant: For a basis \( \{\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_n\} \) of \( K/\mathbb{Q} \):

    \[ \Delta_K = \det(\text{Tr}_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha_i \alpha_j)) \]

  • Ideal: A non-zero subset \( \mathfrak{a} \subseteq \mathcal{O}_K \) closed under addition and multiplication by \( \mathcal{O}_K \).
  • Prime Ideal: An ideal \( \mathfrak{p} \) such that \( \mathfrak{ab} \subseteq \mathfrak{p} \) implies \( \mathfrak{a} \subseteq \mathfrak{p} \) or \( \mathfrak{b} \subseteq \mathfrak{p} \).
  • Class Group: \( \text{Cl}(K) = \text{Frac}(\mathcal{O}_K) / \text{Prin}(\mathcal{O}_K) \), measuring failure of unique factorization.

Theorem: Unique Factorization of Ideals

Theorem (Dedekind): Let \( K \) be a number field with ring of integers \( \mathcal{O}_K \). Then every non-zero ideal \( \mathfrak{a} \subseteq \mathcal{O}_K \) factors uniquely as a product of prime ideals:

\[ \mathfrak{a} = \mathfrak{p}_1^{e_1} \cdots \mathfrak{p}_r^{e_r} \]

where \( \mathfrak{p}_i \) are distinct prime ideals and \( e_i \geq 1 \).

Proof Sketch: The proof uses three key facts: (1) \( \mathcal{O}_K \) is a Dedekind domain (Noetherian, integrally closed, dimension 1), (2) In a Dedekind domain, every non-zero ideal is invertible in the ideal group, and (3) The ideal group is freely generated by prime ideals. The uniqueness follows from the fact that prime ideals are maximal in a Dedekind domain. ∎

Notation Guide

  • \( K, L \): Number fields
  • \( \mathcal{O}_K \): Ring of integers of \( K \)
  • \( \mathfrak{a}, \mathfrak{b}, \mathfrak{p} \): Ideals (fraktur font)
  • \( \text{Nm}(\mathfrak{a}) = |\mathcal{O}_K / \mathfrak{a}| \): Ideal norm
  • \( \text{Tr}_{K/\mathbb{Q}} \): Field trace
  • \( \Delta_K \): Discriminant of \( K \)
  • \( \text{Cl}(K) \): Ideal class group
  • \( h_K = |\text{Cl}(K)| \): Class number

def minimal_polynomial_quadratic(d):
    """
    For Q(√d), return the minimal polynomial and discriminant.
    Returns (a, b, c) for ax² + bx + c = 0
    """
    d = d if d % 4 == 1 else 4 * d
    return (1, 0, -d), d  # x² - d = 0

def ring_of_integers_basis(d):
    """
    Return integral basis for Q(√d).
    If d ≡ 1 (mod 4): {1, (1+√d)/2}
    Otherwise: {1, √d}
    """
    if d % 4 == 1:
        return [1, (1 + np.sqrt(d)) / 2]
    else:
        return [1, np.sqrt(d)]

def discriminant_quadratic(d):
    """Compute discriminant of Q(√d)."""
    if d % 4 == 1:
        return d
    else:
        return 4 * d

def prime_decomposition_quadratic(p, d):
    """
    Determine how a rational prime p decomposes in Q(√d).
    Returns: 'inert', 'splits', or 'ramifies'
    """
    disc = discriminant_quadratic(d)
    
    # Legendre symbol (disc/p)
    def legendre(a, p):
        if p == 2:
            return 0 if a % 2 == 0 else 1
        result = pow(a % p, (p - 1) // 2, p)
        return -1 if result == p - 1 else result
    
    leg = legendre(disc, p)
    
    if leg == -1:
        return 'inert'  # p remains prime
    elif leg == 1:
        return 'splits'  # p = pp̄
    else:
        return 'ramifies'  # p = p²

Graph Theory: Networks and Connectivity

graph-theory

Motivation

How do social networks form? What is the shortest route between two cities? Graph theory, founded by Euler in 1736 with his solution to the Königsberg bridge problem, provides the mathematical language for studying networks. From the internet to protein interactions, from power grids to neural connections, graphs model relationships between discrete entities. Modern graph theory combines combinatorics, probability, and linear algebra to analyze complex systems and design efficient algorithms.

Definitions

  • Graph: A pair \( G = (V, E) \) where \( V \) is a set of vertices and \( E \subseteq \binom{V}{2} \) is a set of edges.
  • Degree: \( \deg(v) = |\{e \in E : v \in e\}| \), the number of edges incident to \( v \).
  • Path: A sequence of vertices \( v_0, v_1, \ldots, v_k \) where \( \{v_i, v_{i+1}\} \in E \).
  • Connected: A graph where every pair of vertices is joined by a path.
  • Tree: A connected graph with no cycles; \( |E| = |V| - 1 \).
  • Adjacency Matrix: \( A_{ij} = 1 \) if \( \{i, j\} \in E \), else 0.
  • Laplacian: \( L = D - A \) where \( D \) is the diagonal degree matrix.

Theorem: The Matrix-Tree Theorem

Theorem (Kirchhoff): Let \( G \) be a connected graph with Laplacian matrix \( L \). Let \( L' \) be any \( (n-1) \times (n-1) \) principal minor of \( L \). Then:

\[ \tau(G) = \det(L') \]

where \( \tau(G) \) is the number of spanning trees of \( G \).

Proof Sketch: The proof uses the Binet-Cauchy formula. Express \( L = BB^T \) where \( B \) is the incidence matrix. Then \( \det(L') = \sum_{S} \det(B_S)^2 \) where the sum is over sets of \( n-1 \) edges. One shows \( |\det(B_S)| = 1 \) if \( S \) forms a spanning tree, and 0 otherwise. ∎

Notation Guide

  • \( G = (V, E) \): Graph with vertices \( V \) and edges \( E \)
  • \( n = |V| \): Number of vertices
  • \( m = |E| \): Number of edges
  • \( \deg(v) \): Degree of vertex \( v \)
  • \( \delta(G) \): Minimum degree
  • \( \Delta(G) \): Maximum degree
  • \( A(G) \): Adjacency matrix
  • \( L(G) \): Laplacian matrix
  • \( \tau(G) \): Number of spanning trees
  • \( \chi(G) \): Chromatic number

import numpy as np
from collections import deque

class Graph:
    """Simple graph implementation with adjacency list."""
    
    def __init__(self, n):
        self.n = n
        self.adj = [[] for _ in range(n)]
    
    def add_edge(self, u, v):
        """Add undirected edge."""
        self.adj[u].append(v)
        self.adj[v].append(u)
    
    def adjacency_matrix(self):
        """Return adjacency matrix."""
        A = np.zeros((self.n, self.n), dtype=int)
        for u in range(self.n):
            for v in self.adj[u]:
                A[u, v] = 1
        return A
    
    def laplacian(self):
        """Return Laplacian matrix L = D - A."""
        A = self.adjacency_matrix()
        D = np.diag([len(self.adj[i]) for i in range(self.n)])
        return D - A
    
    def bfs(self, start):
        """Breadth-first search from start vertex."""
        dist = [-1] * self.n
        dist[start] = 0
        queue = deque([start])
        
        while queue:
            u = queue.popleft()
            for v in self.adj[u]:
                if dist[v] == -1:
                    dist[v] = dist[u] + 1
                    queue.append(v)
        return dist
    
    def count_spanning_trees(self):
        """Kirchhoff's Matrix-Tree Theorem."""
        if self.n <= 1:
            return 1
        L = self.laplacian()
        # Remove last row and column
        L_minor = L[:-1, :-1]
        return int(round(np.linalg.det(L_minor)))

def create_complete_graph(n):
    """Create complete graph K_n."""
    g = Graph(n)
    for i in range(n):
        for j in range(i + 1, n):
            g.add_edge(i, j)
    return g

def create_path_graph(n):
    """Create path graph P_n."""
    g = Graph(n)
    for i in range(n - 1):
        g.add_edge(i, i + 1)
    return g

def create_cycle_graph(n):
    """Create cycle graph C_n."""
    g = Graph(n)
    for i in range(n):
        g.add_edge(i, (i + 1) % n)
    return g

Fluid Dynamics: The Mathematics of Flow

fluid-dynamics

Motivation

How do fluids move? From weather patterns to blood flow, from ocean currents to jet engines, fluid dynamics describes the motion of liquids and gases. The mathematical description of fluids dates back to Euler and the Bernoullis in the 18th century, with the modern theory established by Navier and Stokes in the 19th century. The Navier-Stokes equations, which govern viscous fluid flow, are among the most important—and challenging—equations in applied mathematics. Their study connects to turbulence, chaos, and one of the Millennium Prize Problems.

Definitions

  • Velocity Field: \( \mathbf{u}(\mathbf{x}, t) \in \mathbb{R}^3 \), the velocity of fluid at position \( \mathbf{x} \) and time \( t \).
  • Pressure: \( p(\mathbf{x}, t) \in \mathbb{R} \), the force per unit area.
  • Vorticity: \( \boldsymbol{\omega} = \nabla \times \mathbf{u} \), measuring local rotation.
  • Material Derivative: \( \frac{D}{Dt} = \frac{\partial}{\partial t} + \mathbf{u} \cdot \nabla \).
  • Reynolds Number: \( \text{Re} = \frac{\rho U L}{\mu} \), ratio of inertial to viscous forces.
  • Incompressible Flow: \( \nabla \cdot \mathbf{u} = 0 \), volume-preserving motion.

Theorem: Kelvin's Circulation Theorem

Theorem (Kelvin, 1869): For an inviscid, barotropic fluid with conservative body forces, the circulation around a closed material curve \( C(t) \) is constant:

\[ \frac{d}{dt} \oint_{C(t)} \mathbf{u} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = 0 \]

Proof Sketch: Using the material derivative and Stokes' theorem:

\[ \frac{d}{dt} \oint_{C(t)} \mathbf{u} \cdot d\mathbf{r} = \oint_{C(t)} \frac{D\mathbf{u}}{Dt} \cdot d\mathbf{r} \]

For inviscid flow, Euler's equation gives \( \frac{D\mathbf{u}}{Dt} = -\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla p - \nabla \Phi \). For barotropic flow (\( p = p(\rho) \)), this is a gradient, and the integral of a gradient around a closed loop vanishes. ∎

Corollary (Helmholtz): Vortex lines move with the fluid in inviscid flow.

def burgers_vortex(x, y, t, Re=100, Gamma=1):
    """
    Burgers vortex: exact solution to Navier-Stokes.
    A vortex stretched by an axisymmetric strain flow.
    """
    r = np.sqrt(x**2 + y**2)
    # Azimuthal velocity
    u_theta = (Gamma / (2 * np.pi * r)) * (1 - np.exp(-r**2 * Re / (4 * t)))
    # Convert to Cartesian
    u = -u_theta * y / r
    v = u_theta * x / r
    # Handle r=0
    u = np.where(r < 1e-10, 0, u)
    v = np.where(r < 1e-10, 0, v)
    return u, v

Operations Research: Optimization in Practice

operations-research

Motivation

How do airlines schedule flights? How should delivery routes be planned? Operations research (OR) applies mathematical methods to help organizations make better decisions. Born during World War II to optimize military logistics, OR has grown into a vast field encompassing linear programming, network flows, scheduling, inventory theory, and simulation. The simplex method, developed by George Dantzig in 1947, revolutionized the field by providing an efficient way to solve linear programs. Today, OR techniques power supply chains, financial portfolios, healthcare systems, and transportation networks worldwide.

Definitions

  • Linear Program (LP): Optimize \( \mathbf{c}^T \mathbf{x} \) subject to \( A\mathbf{x} \leq \mathbf{b} \) and \( \mathbf{x} \geq 0 \).
  • Feasible Region: The set of all points satisfying the constraints.
  • Basic Feasible Solution: A corner point of the feasible polytope.
  • Dual Problem: For primal LP, the dual is: minimize \( \mathbf{b}^T \mathbf{y} \) subject to \( A^T \mathbf{y} \geq \mathbf{c} \), \( \mathbf{y} \geq 0 \).
  • Simplex Method: An algorithm that moves from vertex to vertex along edges of the feasible region, improving the objective at each step.
  • Integer Program: An LP with the additional constraint that variables must be integers.

Theorem: Strong Duality

Theorem (Strong Duality): Consider the primal LP:

\[ \max \mathbf{c}^T \mathbf{x} \quad \text{s.t.} \quad A\mathbf{x} \leq \mathbf{b}, \, \mathbf{x} \geq 0 \]

and its dual:

\[ \min \mathbf{b}^T \mathbf{y} \quad \text{s.t.} \quad A^T \mathbf{y} \geq \mathbf{c}, \, \mathbf{y} \geq 0 \]

If either problem has a finite optimal solution, then so does the other, and:

\[ \max \mathbf{c}^T \mathbf{x} = \min \mathbf{b}^T \mathbf{y} \]

Proof Sketch: Weak duality (\( \mathbf{c}^T \mathbf{x} \leq \mathbf{b}^T \mathbf{y} \)) follows from:

\[ \mathbf{c}^T \mathbf{x} \leq (A^T \mathbf{y})^T \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{y}^T A \mathbf{x} \leq \mathbf{y}^T \mathbf{b} \]

Strong duality follows from the separating hyperplane theorem or Farkas' lemma. If the primal is bounded and feasible, there exists an optimal basic feasible solution, and complementary slackness yields the dual optimal solution. ∎

Notation Guide

  • \( \mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y} \): Decision variable vectors
  • \( \mathbf{c} \): Objective function coefficients
  • \( A \): Constraint matrix
  • \( \mathbf{b} \): Right-hand side vector
  • \( \mathbf{x} \geq 0 \): Component-wise non-negativity
  • \( z^* \): Optimal objective value
  • \( \mathbf{x}^* \): Optimal solution
  • \( \mathbf{s} \): Slack variables
  • LP: Linear Program
  • IP: Integer Program

import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import linprog

def solve_lp_simple(c, A_ub=None, b_ub=None, A_eq=None, b_eq=None, bounds=None):
    """
    Wrapper for scipy's linprog with cleaner interface.
    Solves: min c^T x subject to constraints.
    """
    result = linprog(c, A_ub=A_ub, b_ub=b_ub, A_eq=A_eq, b_eq=b_eq, 
                     bounds=bounds, method='highs')
    return result

def transportation_problem(supply, demand, costs):
    """
    Solve transportation problem using linear programming.
    
    Parameters:
    - supply: array of supply amounts at each source
    - demand: array of demand amounts at each destination
    - costs: matrix of transportation costs
    
    Returns optimal flows and total cost.
    """
    m = len(supply)  # number of sources
    n = len(demand)  # number of destinations
    
    # Flatten decision variables: x[i,j] = flow from source i to dest j
    c = costs.flatten()
    
    # Supply constraints: sum_j x[i,j] <= supply[i]
    A_supply = np.zeros((m, m * n))
    for i in range(m):
        for j in range(n):
            A_supply[i, i * n + j] = 1
    
    # Demand constraints: sum_i x[i,j] >= demand[j]  =>  -sum_i x[i,j] <= -demand[j]
    A_demand = np.zeros((n, m * n))
    for j in range(n):
        for i in range(m):
            A_demand[j, i * n + j] = -1
    
    A_ub = np.vstack([A_supply, A_demand])
    b_ub = np.concatenate([supply, -demand])
    
    bounds = [(0, None) for _ in range(m * n)]
    
    result = solve_lp_simple(c, A_ub=A_ub, b_ub=b_ub, bounds=bounds)
    
    if result.success:
        flows = result.x.reshape(m, n)
        return flows, result.fun
    else:
        return None, None

def diet_problem(foods, nutrients, requirements, costs):
    """
    Classic diet problem: minimize cost while meeting nutritional requirements.
    
    Parameters:
    - foods: list of food names
    - nutrients: matrix where nutrients[i,j] = amount of nutrient i in food j
    - requirements: minimum required amount of each nutrient
    - costs: cost per unit of each food
    """
    n_foods = len(foods)
    n_nutrients = len(requirements)
    
    # Objective: minimize cost
    c = costs
    
    # Constraints: nutrients @ x >= requirements
    A_ub = -nutrients.T  # negate for <= form
    b_ub = -requirements
    
    bounds = [(0, None) for _ in range(n_foods)]
    
    result = solve_lp_simple(c, A_ub=A_ub, b_ub=b_ub, bounds=bounds)
    return result

Tropical Geometry: Geometry Over the Min-Plus Semiring

tropical-geometry

Historical Context

Tropical geometry emerged in the late 20th century as a combinatorial shadow of algebraic geometry. The name "tropical" was coined by French mathematicians in honor of their Brazilian colleague Imre Simon, who pioneered the study of min-plus algebra. Tropical geometry replaces classical algebraic varieties with piecewise-linear objects, transforming difficult algebraic problems into tractable combinatorial ones. It has found remarkable applications in enumerative geometry, mirror symmetry, and computational biology.

Definitions

  • Tropical Semiring: \( \mathbb{T} = \mathbb{R} \cup \{\infty\} \) with operations:
    • Tropical addition: \( a \oplus b = \min(a, b) \)
    • Tropical multiplication: \( a \odot b = a + b \)
  • Tropical Polynomial: \( p(x) = \bigoplus_{i} (a_i \odot x^{\odot i}) = \min_i(a_i + i \cdot x) \).
  • Tropical Hypersurface: The set where the minimum is achieved at least twice.
  • Newton Polygon: The convex hull of points \( (i, a_i) \) for a tropical polynomial.

Theorem: The Fundamental Theorem of Tropical Geometry

Theorem: Let \( K \) be an algebraically closed field with non-archimedean valuation \( \text{val}: K^* \to \mathbb{R} \). For any variety \( X \subseteq (K^*)^n \), the tropicalization is:

\[ \text{trop}(X) = \overline{\text{val}(X)} = \bigcap_{f \in I(X)} \text{trop}(V(f)) \]

where the intersection is over all polynomials vanishing on \( X \).

Proof Sketch: The key insight is that the valuation map transforms multiplicative structure into additive structure. For a polynomial \( f = \sum c_\alpha x^\alpha \), the valuation of \( f(x) \) at a point is at least \( \min_\alpha(\text{val}(c_\alpha) + \alpha \cdot \text{val}(x)) \). The equality case corresponds to the tropical hypersurface. The theorem follows by showing this holds for all defining equations. ∎

Notation Guide

  • \( \mathbb{T} \): Tropical semiring
  • \( \oplus \): Tropical addition (minimum)
  • \( \odot \): Tropical multiplication (addition)
  • \( x^{\odot n} = n \cdot x \): Tropical power
  • \( \text{trop}(X) \): Tropicalization of variety \( X \)
  • \( \text{val} \): Non-archimedean valuation

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy.spatial import ConvexHull

def tropical_add(a, b):
    """Tropical addition: min(a, b)"""
    return np.minimum(a, b)

def tropical_mult(a, b):
    """Tropical multiplication: a + b"""
    return a + b

def tropical_polyval(coeffs, x):
    """
    Evaluate tropical polynomial.
    coeffs[i] is coefficient of x^i
    p(x) = min_i(coeffs[i] + i*x)
    """
    terms = [coeffs[i] + i * x for i in range(len(coeffs))]
    return np.minimum.reduce(terms)

def tropical_hypersurface(coeffs, x_range):
    """
    Find points where tropical polynomial achieves minimum twice.
    Returns the tropical variety (piecewise linear curve).
    """
    x_vals = np.linspace(x_range[0], x_range[1], 1000)
    y_vals = tropical_polyval(coeffs, x_vals)
    
    # Find breakpoints where two terms are equal
    breakpoints = []
    for i in range(len(coeffs) - 1):
        for j in range(i + 1, len(coeffs)):
            # coeffs[i] + i*x = coeffs[j] + j*x
            if i != j:
                x_bp = (coeffs[j] - coeffs[i]) / (i - j)
                if x_range[0] <= x_bp <= x_range[1]:
                    breakpoints.append(x_bp)
    
    return sorted(set(breakpoints))

def newton_polygon(coeffs):
    """
    Compute vertices of Newton polygon.
    coeffs[i] corresponds to point (i, coeffs[i])
    """
    points = np.array([(i, coeffs[i]) for i in range(len(coeffs))])
    hull = ConvexHull(points)
    return points[hull.vertices]

Arithmetic Geometry: Solutions in Numbers

arithmetic-geometry

Historical Context

Arithmetic geometry, the modern synthesis of number theory and algebraic geometry, emerged from attempts to solve Diophantine equations—polynomial equations seeking integer or rational solutions. Fermat's Last Theorem, which states that \( x^n + y^n = z^n \) has no non-trivial integer solutions for \( n > 2 \), stood as an unsolved problem for 358 years until Andrew Wiles proved it in 1994 using techniques from elliptic curves and modular forms. The field has since grown to encompass the study of rational points on varieties, Galois representations, and the deep conjectures of the Langlands program.

Definitions

  • Algebraic Variety: The set of common zeros of a collection of polynomials.
  • Rational Point: A point on a variety with coordinates in \( \mathbb{Q} \) (or a specified field).
  • Elliptic Curve: A smooth projective curve of genus 1 with a specified rational point, given by \( y^2 = x^3 + ax + b \).
  • Hasse Principle: A variety has a rational point if and only if it has points over \( \mathbb{R} \) and all \( \mathbb{Q}_p \).
  • Height: A measure of the complexity of a rational point, used to count points of bounded size.

Theorem: The Mordell-Weil Theorem

Theorem (Mordell-Weil): Let \( E \) be an elliptic curve over a number field \( K \). Then the group of \( K \)-rational points \( E(K) \) is a finitely generated abelian group:

\[ E(K) \cong E(K)_{\text{tors}} \oplus \mathbb{Z}^r \]

where \( E(K)_{\text{tors}} \) is the finite torsion subgroup and \( r \geq 0 \) is the rank.

Proof Sketch: The proof has two main parts: (1) The "weak Mordell-Weil theorem" shows \( E(K)/2E(K) \) is finite using Galois cohomology and Kummer theory. (2) Height arguments (descent) show that from a finite generating set for \( E(K)/2E(K) \), one can construct a finite generating set for \( E(K) \). The height function provides a measure of complexity that decreases under certain operations, allowing an inductive argument. ∎

Notation Guide

  • \( E \): Elliptic curve
  • \( E(K) \): K-rational points on E
  • \( \mathcal{O} \): Point at infinity (identity)
  • \( E[n] \): n-torsion points
  • \( r \): Rank of elliptic curve
  • \( h(P) \): Height of point P
  • \( \hat{h}(P) \): Canonical height
  • \( L(E, s) \): L-function of elliptic curve

import numpy as np
from fractions import Fraction

def elliptic_curve_discriminant(a, b):
    """Compute discriminant Δ = -16(4a³ + 27b²) of y² = x³ + ax + b."""
    return -16 * (4 * a**3 + 27 * b**2)

def is_singular(a, b):
    """Check if elliptic curve is singular."""
    return elliptic_curve_discriminant(a, b) == 0

def elliptic_add(P, Q, a):
    """
    Add two points on elliptic curve y² = x³ + ax + b.
    P, Q are tuples (x, y) or None for point at infinity.
    """
    # Point at infinity is identity
    if P is None:
        return Q
    if Q is None:
        return P
    
    x1, y1 = P
    x2, y2 = Q
    
    # Convert to Fractions for exact arithmetic
    x1, y1, x2, y2 = Fraction(x1), Fraction(y1), Fraction(x2), Fraction(y2)
    a = Fraction(a)
    
    if x1 == x2:
        if y1 == -y2:
            return None  # Point at infinity
        # Point doubling: λ = (3x₁² + a) / (2y₁)

Mirror Symmetry: Duality in String Theory

mirror-symmetry

Historical Context

Mirror symmetry is a profound duality discovered by physicists in the late 1980s while studying string theory compactifications on Calabi-Yau manifolds. Strikingly, two geometrically distinct Calabi-Yau threefolds \( X \) and \( X^\vee \) can give rise to identical physics, with complex structure deformations on one side corresponding to Kähler structure deformations on the other. This led to spectacular mathematical predictions, including the famous Candelas-de la Ossa-Green-Parkes formula for counting rational curves on the quintic threefold. Mirror symmetry has since developed into a rich field connecting algebraic geometry, symplectic geometry, and theoretical physics.

Definitions

  • Calabi-Yau Manifold: A compact Kähler manifold with trivial canonical bundle \( K_X \cong \mathcal{O}_X \) and \( H^i(X, \mathcal{O}_X) = 0 \) for \( 0 < i < \dim(X) \).
  • Hodge Numbers: \( h^{p,q} = \dim H^q(X, \Omega^p) \). For Calabi-Yau threefolds, mirror symmetry predicts \( h^{p,q}(X) = h^{3-p,q}(X^\vee) \).
  • Gromov-Witten Invariants: Counts of curves meeting specified conditions, defined via virtual fundamental classes.
  • Periods: Integrals of holomorphic forms over homology cycles, satisfying Picard-Fuchs equations.
  • Mirror Map: A coordinate transformation relating complex moduli on \( X \) to Kähler moduli on \( X^\vee \).

Theorem: Mirror Symmetry for the Quintic Threefold

Theorem (Candelas-de la Ossa-Green-Parkes, 1991): Let \( X \) be a smooth quintic hypersurface in \( \mathbb{P}^4 \). Its mirror \( X^\vee \) is a resolution of the quotient of a one-parameter family of quintics by a finite group. The genus-zero Gromov-Witten invariants \( n_d \) (counts of degree-\( d \) rational curves) are determined by the periods of the holomorphic 3-form on \( X^\vee \) via the mirror map.

Specifically, the prepotential \( F_0 \) satisfies:

\[ F_0 = \frac{5}{6} t^3 + \sum_{d=1}^\infty n_d q^d, \quad q = e^{2\pi i t} \]

where the instanton numbers \( n_d \) are extracted from solutions to the Picard-Fuchs equation:

\[ \left( z \frac{d}{dz} \right)^4 \Phi - 5z \prod_{k=1}^4 \left( 5z \frac{d}{dz} + k \right) \Phi = 0 \]

Proof Sketch: The proof uses variation of Hodge structures. The Picard-Fuchs equation governs periods of the holomorphic 3-form. Near the large complex structure limit, the solutions have logarithmic behavior. The mirror map \( t(z) \) is a ratio of periods. The Yukawa coupling, computed from the third derivative of the prepotential, encodes the Gromov-Witten invariants. The factorization into a classical cubic term plus instanton corrections follows from the monodromy properties. ∎

Notation Guide

  • \( X, X^\vee \): Mirror pair of Calabi-Yau manifolds
  • \( h^{p,q} \): Hodge numbers
  • \( \chi(X) \): Euler characteristic
  • \( n_d \): Genus-0 Gromov-Witten invariant (curve count)
  • \( F_g \): Genus-\( g \) prepotential
  • \( \Phi \): Period integral
  • \( t \): Flat coordinate (mirror map)
  • \( q = e^{2\pi i t} \): Complexified Kähler parameter

Noncommutative Geometry: Space Beyond Points

noncommutative-geometry

Historical Context

Noncommutative geometry, developed by Alain Connes in the 1980s, extends the duality between spaces and algebras to situations where the algebra of functions is noncommutative. In classical algebraic geometry, the Gelfand-Naimark theorem establishes that compact Hausdorff spaces are equivalent to commutative C*-algebras. Connes realized that by dropping commutativity, one could define "spaces" that have no classical point-set description. This framework has found applications in quantum physics, where observables don't commute, and in index theory, where it provides new proofs of the Atiyah-Singer index theorem.

Definitions

  • C*-Algebra: A Banach algebra \( A \) with involution \( * \) satisfying \( \|a^*a\| = \|a\|^2 \).
  • Spectral Triple: A triple \( (\mathcal{A}, \mathcal{H}, D) \) where:
    • \( \mathcal{A} \) is an algebra represented on Hilbert space \( \mathcal{H} \)
    • \( D \) is a self-adjoint operator (Dirac operator)
    • \( [D, a] \) is bounded for all \( a \in \mathcal{A} \)
  • Noncommutative Torus: The algebra generated by unitaries \( U, V \) with \( UV = e^{2\pi i \theta} VU \).
  • Cyclic Cohomology: A noncommutative generalization of de Rham cohomology.

Theorem: The Connes Index Theorem

Theorem (Connes): Let \( (\mathcal{A}, \mathcal{H}, D) \) be a spectral triple with dimension \( n \). For any projection \( e \in M_N(\mathcal{A}) \), the index of the twisted Dirac operator is given by:

\[ \text{Ind}(eDe) = \langle \text{ch}(e), \text{ch}(D) \rangle \]

where \( \text{ch}(e) \) is the Chern character in cyclic homology and \( \text{ch}(D) \) is the character of the cycle defined by \( D \).

For the classical case of a compact spin manifold, this reduces to the Atiyah-Singer index theorem.

Proof Sketch: The proof uses the local index formula in noncommutative geometry. The key insight is that the Dixmier trace of certain operators recovers the Wodzicki residue, allowing integration over noncommutative spaces. The pairing between K-theory and cyclic cohomology generalizes the classical Chern character. ∎

Notation Guide

  • \( \mathcal{A} \): C*-algebra
  • \( \mathcal{H} \): Hilbert space
  • \( D \): Dirac operator
  • \( [D, a] \): Commutator (measuring noncommutativity)
  • \( A_\theta \): Noncommutative torus with parameter \( \theta \)
  • \( \text{ch} \): Chern character
  • \( HC^* \): Cyclic cohomology
  • \( K_*(\mathcal{A}) \): K-theory groups

import numpy as np
from numpy.linalg import matrix_power

def noncommutative_torus_element(theta, coeffs_u, coeffs_v):
    """
    Represent an element of the noncommutative torus A_θ.
    
    The NCT is generated by unitaries U, V with UV = e^(2πiθ) VU.
    An element is a finite sum Σ a_{m,n} U^m V^n.
    
    For computation, we truncate to a finite-dimensional approximation.
    """
    # Use a finite-dimensional matrix representation
    N = max(len(coeffs_u), len(coeffs_v))
    
    # Clock and shift matrices
    omega = np.exp(2j * np.pi * theta)
    
    # U = clock matrix (diagonal)
    U = np.diag([omega**k for k in range(N)])
    
    # V = shift matrix (cyclic)
    V = np.roll(np.eye(N), 1, axis=0)
    
    # Construct element
    result = np.zeros((N, N), dtype=complex)
    for m, a_m in enumerate(coeffs_u):
        for n, b_n in enumerate(coeffs_v):
            # U^m V^n = ω^{mn} V^n U^m, but we use ordered form
            term = a_m * b_n * matrix_power(U, m) @ matrix_power(V, n)
            result += term
    
    return result

def weyl_algebra_element(n, coeffs):
    """
    Weyl algebra: [x, p] = iℏ.
    Represented on L²(R) via finite difference approximation.
    """
    # Position operator (diagonal)
    x = np.diag(np.linspace(-5, 5, n))
    
    # Momentum operator (derivative, finite difference)
    dx = 10 / (n - 1)
    p = -1j * (np.diag(np.ones(n-1), 1) - np.diag(np.ones(n-1), -1)) / (2 * dx)
    
    # Verify canonical commutation relation
    commutator = x @ p - p @ x
    
    return x, p, commutator

def fuzzy_sphere_element(j, coeffs):
    """
    Fuzzy sphere: noncommutative approximation of S².
    Functions are approximated by (2j+1) × (2j+1) matrices.

    N = 2 * j + 1
    
    # Angular momentum operators (spin-j representation)
    m_vals = np.arange(j, -j-1, -1)
    
    # J_z
    Jz = np.diag(m_vals)
    
    # J_+ and J_-
    J_plus = np.zeros((N, N), dtype=complex)
    for i, m in enumerate(m_vals[:-1]):
        J_plus[i, i+1] = np.sqrt(j*(j+1) - m*(m-1))

📜 Math History: Emmy Noether and the Algebraization of Physics

Amalie Emmy Noether (1882–1935) stands as one of the most influential mathematicians of the 20th century, despite facing extraordinary obstacles as a Jewish woman in early 20th-century Germany. Born in Erlangen to a family of mathematicians—her father Max Noether was a respected algebraic geometer—Emmy initially trained to teach French and English to girls. It was only after passing the examination to teach these languages that she sought permission to audit classes at the University of Erlangen, where women were not formally admitted as students.

Noether's mathematical genius quickly became apparent. She completed her doctoral dissertation in 1907 under Paul Gordan, working on invariant theory. For seven years after her doctorate, she worked without pay at the Mathematical Institute in Erlangen, researching and occasionally substituting for her father. Her breakthrough came in 1915 when David Hilbert and Felix Klein invited her to join the University of Göttingen, the world's leading center for mathematics. Despite initial resistance from faculty members who objected to a woman teaching, Noether's lectures were eventually advertised under Hilbert's name, with Noether delivering them.

At Göttingen, Noether made two revolutionary contributions that transformed mathematics and physics. In mathematics, she fundamentally restructured abstract algebra, shifting the focus from computation to conceptual understanding. Her 1921 paper "Idealtheorie in Ringbereichen" (Ideal Theory in Ring Domains) established the foundation of modern commutative ring theory, introducing what we now call Noetherian rings—rings satisfying the ascending chain condition on ideals. This condition, seemingly technical, captures the essence of finiteness that makes many algebraic structures tractable.

In physics, Noether's 1918 theorem established a profound connection between symmetry and conservation laws. She proved that every continuous symmetry of a physical system corresponds to a conserved quantity: time translation symmetry yields energy conservation, spatial translation symmetry yields momentum conservation, and rotational symmetry yields angular momentum conservation. This theorem became essential for the development of quantum field theory and the Standard Model of particle physics. Einstein wrote to Hilbert that Noether's work impressed him enormously, calling it "penetrating mathematical thinking."

When the Nazis came to power in 1933, Noether was dismissed from her position due to her Jewish ancestry. She accepted a position at Bryn Mawr College in Pennsylvania, where she worked until her sudden death in 1935 from complications following surgery. Her legacy extends through her students and the generations of mathematicians influenced by her work. Today, the term "Noetherian" appears throughout mathematics—Noetherian rings, Noetherian modules, Noetherian spaces—and her theorem remains a cornerstone of theoretical physics. In the words of mathematician Hermann Weyl, "Her significance for algebra cannot be read entirely from her own papers; she had great stimulating power, and many of her suggestions took shape only in the works of her pupils and co-workers."

Show that the spectrum of \( a \) is a Cantor set when \( \theta \) is irrational, and compute the spectral radius.

Hint

Consider the representation of \( A_\theta \) using the irrational rotation algebra on \( L^2(S^1) \). The operator \( a \) acts as a discrete Schrödinger operator. Use the fact that for irrational \( \theta \), the rotation by \( 2\pi\theta \) is ergodic. The spectral radius can be found using the Weyl criterion or by analyzing the almost Mathieu operator.

Solution

Step 1: Representation

Represent \( A_\theta \) on \( \mathcal{H} = L^2(S^1) \) by:

\[ (U\psi)(x) = e^{2\pi i x} \psi(x), \quad (V\psi)(x) = \psi(x + \theta) \]

One verifies \( UV = e^{2\pi i \theta} VU \) directly.

Step 2: The Operator

In this representation:

\[ (a\psi)(x) = 2\cos(2\pi x)\psi(x) + \psi(x + \theta) + \psi(x - \theta) \]

This is a discrete Schrödinger operator with quasi-periodic potential.

Step 3: Spectrum is a Cantor Set

For irrational \( \theta \), the rotation \( R_\theta: x \mapsto x + \theta \) is minimal and uniquely ergodic. By results of Bellissard and others, the spectrum of such almost Mathieu operators is a Cantor set (has no isolated points and is totally disconnected) when \( \theta \) satisfies certain Diophantine conditions. This was conjectured by Azbel and eventually proven for all irrational \( \theta \).

Step 4: Spectral Radius

The spectral radius is given by:

\[ r(a) = \lim_{n \to \infty} \|a^n\|^{1/n} \]

For the noncommutative torus, the C*-norm satisfies \( \|U\| = \|V\| = 1 \). By the triangle inequality:

\[ \|a\| \leq \|U\| + \|U^{-1}\| + \|V\| + \|V^{-1}\| = 4 \]

Equality holds because the representation is faithful and the numerical range of \( a \) contains points arbitrarily close to 4. Thus \( r(a) = \|a\| = 4 \).

Conclusion: The spectrum of \( a \) in \( A_\theta \) is a Cantor set of Lebesgue measure zero, contained in \( [-4, 4] \), with spectral radius 4. ∎

The Tropical Cubic Curve

Tropical geometry transforms algebraic curves into piecewise-linear objects. A tropical cubic curve in the plane is the locus where a tropical polynomial of degree 3 achieves its minimum at least twice. These curves are dual to triangulations of the Newton polygon and provide combinatorial insights into classical algebraic geometry.

The visualization below generates a random tropical cubic curve and displays its structure:

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from matplotlib.patches import Polygon
from scipy.spatial import Delaunay

def tropical_cubic(coeffs, x_range, y_range, resolution=500):
    """
    Evaluate tropical cubic polynomial and find curve.
    
    A tropical cubic has the form:
    p(x,y) = min_{i+j≤3} (c_{i,j} + i*x + j*y)
    
    The tropical curve is where the minimum is achieved twice.
    """
    x = np.linspace(x_range[0], x_range[1], resolution)
    y = np.linspace(y_range[0], y_range[1], resolution)
    X, Y = np.meshgrid(x, y)
    
    # Evaluate all terms
    terms = []
    for i in range(4):  # degree in x
        for j in range(4-i):  # degree in y, i+j ≤ 3
            idx = i * 4 + j - i * (i - 1) // 2
            if idx < len(coeffs):
                term = coeffs[idx] + i * X + j * Y
                terms.append(term)
    
    terms = np.array(terms)
    
    # Find where minimum is achieved (approximately)
    min_val = np.min(terms, axis=0)
    min_count = np.sum(np.abs(terms - min_val) < 0.1, axis=0)
    
    # Curve is where at least two terms achieve minimum
    curve_mask = min_count >= 2
    
    return X, Y, curve_mask, min_val
