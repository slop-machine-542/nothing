Representation Theory: Symmetry Through Linear Algebra

Tags: representation-theory group-theory character-theory

Motivation

Representation theory is the art of studying abstract algebraic structures by representing their elements as linear transformations of vector spaces. This approach transforms questions about groups into questions about matrices, where powerful tools from linear algebra can be applied. The theory has profound applications in quantum mechanics (where it describes symmetries of physical systems), harmonic analysis, number theory, and combinatorics.

Definitions

A representation of a group \( G \) on a vector space \( V \) over a field \( F \) is a group homomorphism \( \rho: G \to GL(V) \), where \( GL(V) \) is the group of invertible linear transformations of \( V \).

The character of a representation \( \rho \) is the function \( \chi_\rho: G \to F \) defined by \( \chi_\rho(g) = \text{tr}(\rho(g)) \), the trace of the linear transformation \( \rho(g) \).

A representation is irreducible if it has no nontrivial proper subspaces invariant under all \( \rho(g) \).

Theorem and Proof

Theorem (Schur's Lemma): Let \( \rho_1: G \to GL(V_1) \) and \( \rho_2: G \to GL(V_2) \) be irreducible representations of a group \( G \) over an algebraically closed field \( F \).

  1. If \( \rho_1 \) and \( \rho_2 \) are not isomorphic, then every \( G \)-equivariant linear map \( T: V_1 \to V_2 \) is zero.
  2. If \( \rho_1 = \rho_2 \), then every \( G \)-equivariant linear map is a scalar multiple of the identity.

Proof: Let \( T: V_1 \to V_2 \) be \( G \)-equivariant, meaning \( T \circ \rho_1(g) = \rho_2(g) \circ T \) for all \( g \in G \).

Step 1: Consider \( \ker(T) \subseteq V_1 \). For \( v \in \ker(T) \), we have \( T(\rho_1(g)v) = \rho_2(g)T(v) = 0 \), so \( \rho_1(g)v \in \ker(T) \). Thus \( \ker(T) \) is \( G \)-invariant. Since \( \rho_1 \) is irreducible, either \( \ker(T) = 0 \) or \( \ker(T) = V_1 \).

Step 2: Similarly, \( \text{im}(T) \subseteq V_2 \) is \( G \)-invariant. Since \( \rho_2 \) is irreducible, either \( \text{im}(T) = 0 \) or \( \text{im}(T) = V_2 \).

Case 1: If \( \ker(T) = V_1 \) or \( \text{im}(T) = 0 \), then \( T = 0 \).

Case 2: If \( \ker(T) = 0 \) and \( \text{im}(T) = V_2 \), then \( T \) is an isomorphism of representations. This proves part (1).

Part (2): If \( \rho_1 = \rho_2 \), let \( \lambda \) be an eigenvalue of \( T \) (exists since \( F \) is algebraically closed). Then \( T - \lambda I \) is \( G \)-equivariant with nontrivial kernel. By irreducibility, \( T - \lambda I = 0 \), so \( T = \lambda I \). ∎

Notation Guide

  • \( \rho: G \to GL(V) \) — representation homomorphism
  • \( \chi_\rho(g) = \text{tr}(\rho(g)) \) — character of the representation
  • \( \hat{G} \) — set of irreducible representations of \( G \)
  • \( \langle \chi_1, \chi_2 \rangle = \frac{1}{|G|} \sum_{g \in G} \chi_1(g) \overline{\chi_2(g)} \) — inner product of characters
  • \( \mathbb{C}[G] \) — group algebra

Harmonic Analysis: Decomposing Functions into Waves

Tags: harmonic-analysis fourier-analysis functional-analysis

Motivation

Harmonic analysis is the study of functions by decomposing them into basic waves—sines, cosines, or more general characters. Originating with Fourier's study of heat conduction, the subject has grown into a vast field connecting analysis, number theory, representation theory, and partial differential equations. The fundamental insight is that complex phenomena can be understood by studying their frequency components.

Definitions

The Fourier transform of a function \( f \in L^1(\mathbb{R}^n) \) is: \[\hat{f}(\xi) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^n} f(x) e^{-2\pi i x \cdot \xi} dx\]

A function \( f \) is in the Schwartz space \( \mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^n) \) if it is smooth and all derivatives decay faster than any polynomial.

The convolution of \( f \) and \( g \) is \( (f * g)(x) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^n} f(y)g(x-y)dy \).

Theorem and Proof

Theorem (Plancherel): The Fourier transform extends to a unitary operator on \( L^2(\mathbb{R}^n) \). Specifically, for \( f, g \in L^2(\mathbb{R}^n) \): \[\int_{\mathbb{R}^n} f(x) \overline{g(x)} dx = \int_{\mathbb{R}^n} \hat{f}(\xi) \overline{\hat{g}(\xi)} d\xi\] In particular, \( \|f\|_{L^2} = \|\hat{f}\|_{L^2} \).

Proof Sketch: First prove for \( f, g \in \mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^n) \).

Step 1: Define \( h = \overline{\hat{g}} \). Then \( \hat{h}(x) = \overline{g(x)} \) (by properties of Fourier transform).

Step 2: By the multiplication-convolution duality: \[\int f(x) \overline{g(x)} dx = \int f(x) \hat{h}(x) dx = \int \hat{f}(\xi) h(\xi) d\xi = \int \hat{f}(\xi) \overline{\hat{g}(\xi)} d\xi\]

Step 3: The Schwartz space is dense in \( L^2 \), and the Fourier transform is an isometry on \( \mathcal{S} \). By standard density arguments, the Fourier transform extends uniquely to a unitary operator on \( L^2 \). ∎

Notation Guide

  • \( \hat{f}(\xi) \) — Fourier transform of \( f \)
  • \( \mathcal{F} \) — Fourier transform operator
  • \( f * g \) — convolution
  • \( \mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^n) \) — Schwartz space
  • \( \|f\|_{L^p} = \left(\int |f|^p\right)^{1/p} \) — \( L^p \) norm
  • \( \check{f}(x) = \hat{f}(-x) \) — inverse Fourier transform

def dft_matrix(n):
    """Discrete Fourier Transform matrix."""
    omega = np.exp(-2j * np.pi / n)
    return np.array([[omega**(j*k) for k in range(n)] for j in range(n)])

def fft(x):
    """Cooley-Tukey FFT algorithm."""
    n = len(x)
    if n <= 1:
        return x
    elif n % 2 != 0:
        # Fall back to DFT for odd lengths
        return np.fft.fft(x)
    
    even = fft(x[0::2])
    odd = fft(x[1::2])
    
    T = [np.exp(-2j * np.pi * k / n) * odd[k] for k in range(n // 2)]
    return [even[k] + T[k] for k in range(n // 2)] + \
           [even[k] - T[k] for k in range(n // 2)]

The Hahn-Banach Theorem: Extending Linear Functionals

Tags: functional-analysis banach-spaces linear-functionals

Motivation

The Hahn-Banach theorem is one of the cornerstones of functional analysis. It answers a fundamental question: given a linear functional defined on a subspace, when can it be extended to the whole space while preserving certain properties? This seemingly technical result has profound consequences, including the existence of enough continuous linear functionals to separate points in normed spaces, the duality between \( L^p \) spaces, and geometric versions concerning separation of convex sets.

Definitions

A normed vector space is a vector space \( V \) over \( \mathbb{R} \) or \( \mathbb{C} \) equipped with a norm \( \|\cdot\|: V \to [0, \infty) \) satisfying: (1) \( \|v\| = 0 \Leftrightarrow v = 0 \), (2) \( \|\alpha v\| = |\alpha|\|v\| \), (3) \( \|v + w\| \leq \|v\| + \|w\| \).

A linear functional on \( V \) is a linear map \( \phi: V \to \mathbb{F} \) (where \( \mathbb{F} \) is the scalar field).

The dual space \( V^* \) is the space of all continuous linear functionals on \( V \).

Theorem and Proof

Theorem (Hahn-Banach): Let \( V \) be a vector space over \( \mathbb{R} \), \( p: V \to \mathbb{R} \) a sublinear functional (satisfying \( p(x+y) \leq p(x) + p(y) \) and \( p(tx) = tp(x) \) for \( t \geq 0 \)), \( W \subseteq V \) a subspace, and \( \phi: W \to \mathbb{R} \) a linear functional with \( \phi(w) \leq p(w) \) for all \( w \in W \). Then there exists a linear extension \( \tilde{\phi}: V \to \mathbb{R} \) such that \( \tilde{\phi}|_W = \phi \) and \( \tilde{\phi}(v) \leq p(v) \) for all \( v \in V \).

Proof Sketch:

Step 1 (One-dimensional extension): Let \( v_0 \in V \setminus W \). We want to extend \( \phi \) to \( W \oplus \mathbb{R}v_0 \). Any extension must have the form \( \phi_1(w + tv_0) = \phi(w) + tc \) for some constant \( c \). The constraint \( \phi_1 \leq p \) requires: \[\sup_{w \in W, t > 0} \frac{\phi(w) - p(w - tv_0)}{t} \leq c \leq \inf_{w \in W, t > 0} \frac{p(w + tv_0) - \phi(w)}{t}\]

One shows the sup ≤ inf using sublinearity of \( p \).

Step 2 (Zorn's Lemma): Consider the set of all extensions of \( \phi \) dominated by \( p \), ordered by extension. Every chain has an upper bound (the union). By Zorn's Lemma, a maximal extension exists.

Step 3: The maximal extension must be defined on all of \( V \), otherwise we could extend further using Step 1, contradicting maximality. ∎

Notation Guide

  • \( V^* \) — dual space of continuous linear functionals
  • \( \|\phi\|_{V^*} = \sup_{\|v\| \leq 1} |\phi(v)| \) — operator norm
  • \( p(x) \) — sublinear functional (often \( p(x) = \|x\| \))
  • \( W^\perp = \{\phi \in V^* : \phi(w) = 0 \text{ for all } w \in W\} \) — annihilator

Control Theory: Stabilizing Dynamical Systems

Tags: control-theory dynamical-systems stability

Real-World Motivation

Control theory is the mathematics of making systems behave as desired. From autopilot systems in aircraft to temperature regulation in buildings, from robotic arms to insulin pumps, control systems are everywhere. The fundamental question is: given a system that evolves according to certain dynamics, how can we design feedback mechanisms to ensure stability, track reference signals, and reject disturbances?

Definitions

A linear time-invariant (LTI) system has the state-space form: \[\dot{x}(t) = Ax(t) + Bu(t)\] \[y(t) = Cx(t) + Du(t)\] where \( x \in \mathbb{R}^n \) is the state, \( u \in \mathbb{R}^m \) is the control input, and \( y \in \mathbb{R}^p \) is the output.

The system is controllable if for any initial state \( x_0 \) and target state \( x_f \), there exists a control \( u(t) \) that drives the system from \( x_0 \) to \( x_f \) in finite time.

The system is stabilizable if there exists a feedback matrix \( K \) such that \( A - BK \) has all eigenvalues in the left half-plane.

Theorem and Proof

Theorem (Kalman Controllability): The pair \( (A, B) \) is controllable if and only if the controllability matrix \[\mathcal{C} = [B \quad AB \quad A^2B \quad \cdots \quad A^{n-1}B]\] has full row rank (rank \( n \)).

Proof Sketch:

(⇒) If \( \mathcal{C} \) does not have full rank, there exists a nonzero vector \( v \) such that \( v^T A^k B = 0 \) for all \( k \). The solution to the state equation is: \[x(t) = e^{At}x_0 + \int_0^t e^{A(t-\tau)}Bu(\tau)d\tau\]

For any control \( u \), we have \( v^T x(t) = v^T e^{At} x_0 \), so the component along \( v \) evolves independently of \( u \). Thus not all states are reachable.

(⇐) If \( \mathcal{C} \) has full rank, the reachable subspace is all of \( \mathbb{R}^n \). Using the Cayley-Hamilton theorem, \( e^{At} \) can be expressed as a polynomial in \( A \) of degree at most \( n-1 \). The integral term can then be written in terms of the columns of \( \mathcal{C} \), showing all directions are reachable. ∎

Notation Guide

  • \( A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n} \) — state matrix
  • \( B \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m} \) — input matrix
  • \( C \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times n} \) — output matrix
  • \( \mathcal{C} \) — controllability matrix
  • \( \mathcal{O} = [C^T \quad (CA)^T \quad \cdots]^T \) — observability matrix
  • \( K \) — feedback gain matrix

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy.integrate import odeint
from scipy.linalg import solve_continuous_are, eig

def check_controllability(A, B):
    """Check Kalman controllability condition."""
    n = A.shape[0]
    C = B
    for i in range(1, n):
        C = np.hstack([C, np.linalg.matrix_power(A, i) @ B])
    rank = np.linalg.matrix_rank(C)
    return rank == n, rank, C

def lqr_controller(A, B, Q, R):
    """
    Linear Quadratic Regulator.
    Find optimal K that minimizes integral(x^T Q x + u^T R u)dt.
    """
    # Solve Algebraic Riccati Equation: A^T P + PA - PBR^{-1}B^T P + Q = 0
    P = solve_continuous_are(A, B, Q, R)
    K = np.linalg.inv(R) @ B.T @ P
    return K, P

def system_dynamics(x, t, A, B, K=None, reference=None):
    """System dynamics with optional feedback control."""
    if K is not None:
        if reference is not None:
            u = -K @ (x - reference)
        else:
            u = -K @ x
    else:
        u = np.zeros(B.shape[1])
    return A @ x + B @ u

Information Theory: The Mathematics of Communication

Tags: information-theory entropy coding

Real-World Motivation

Claude Shannon's 1948 paper "A Mathematical Theory of Communication" founded information theory and revolutionized our understanding of communication. Before Shannon, engineers designed communication systems through trial and error. Shannon provided the fundamental limits: how much information can be transmitted reliably over a noisy channel? This question underpins everything from mobile phones to the internet, from DNA sequencing to black hole physics.

Definitions

The entropy of a discrete random variable \( X \) with probability mass function \( p(x) \) is: \[H(X) = -\sum_{x} p(x) \log p(x)\] It measures the average uncertainty or information content.

The conditional entropy \( H(Y|X) \) measures the remaining uncertainty in \( Y \) given knowledge of \( X \).

The mutual information \( I(X;Y) = H(X) - H(X|Y) = H(Y) - H(Y|X) \) measures the information shared between \( X \) and \( Y \).

The channel capacity is \( C = \max_{p(x)} I(X;Y) \), the maximum rate of reliable communication.

Theorem and Proof

Theorem (Shannon's Channel Coding Theorem): For a discrete memoryless channel with capacity \( C \), any rate \( R < C \) is achievable: for any \( \epsilon > 0 \) and sufficiently large block length \( n \), there exists a code with rate \( R \) and error probability less than \( \epsilon \). Conversely, any sequence of codes with rate \( R > C \) has error probability bounded away from zero.

Proof Sketch (Achievability):

Random Coding: Generate \( 2^{nR} \) codewords independently according to \( p(x)^n \).

Typicality: By the Asymptotic Equipartition Property, there are about \( 2^{nH(X)} \) typical sequences, each with probability about \( 2^{-nH(X)} \).

Decoding: The receiver looks for a codeword jointly typical with the received sequence. The number of jointly typical sequences is about \( 2^{nH(X,Y)} \).

Error Analysis: The probability of confusing the true codeword with a different one is roughly \( 2^{nR} \cdot 2^{-nI(X;Y)} \). If \( R < I(X;Y) \), this tends to zero. Optimizing over \( p(x) \) gives capacity. ∎

Notation Guide

  • \( H(X) \) — entropy (in bits if log base 2)
  • \( H(X|Y) \) — conditional entropy
  • \( I(X;Y) \) — mutual information
  • \( D(p \| q) = \sum p(x) \log \frac{p(x)}{q(x)} \) — KL divergence
  • \( C \) — channel capacity
  • \( R \) — code rate (bits per channel use)

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def entropy(p):
    """Calculate Shannon entropy of distribution p."""
    p = np.array(p)
    p = p[p > 0]  # Remove zeros
    return -np.sum(p * np.log2(p))

def mutual_information(joint_dist):
    """Calculate mutual information from joint distribution."""
    # joint_dist[i, j] = P(X=i, Y=j)
    p_x = np.sum(joint_dist, axis=1)
    p_y = np.sum(joint_dist, axis=0)
    
    mi = 0
    for i in range(len(p_x)):
        for j in range(len(p_y)):
            if joint_dist[i, j] > 0:
                mi += joint_dist[i, j] * np.log2(
                    joint_dist[i, j] / (p_x[i] * p_y[j])
                )
    return mi

def binary_entropy(p):
    """Entropy of Bernoulli(p) distribution."""
    if p <= 0 or p >= 1:
        return 0
    return -(p * np.log2(p) + (1-p) * np.log2(1-p))

def binary_symmetric_channel_capacity(epsilon):
    """Capacity of BSC with crossover probability epsilon."""
    return 1 - binary_entropy(epsilon)

Queueing Theory: Managing Congestion

Tags: queuing-theory stochastic-processes operations-research

Real-World Motivation

Queues are everywhere: customers waiting at checkout counters, data packets in network routers, jobs in computer systems, patients in emergency rooms. Queueing theory, founded by A.K. Erlang in the early 1900s to design telephone networks, provides mathematical models to predict congestion, determine optimal staffing levels, and design efficient service systems. The fundamental trade-off is between service cost (more servers) and waiting cost (customer dissatisfaction).

Definitions

A queue is characterized by Kendall's notation \( A/B/c/K/N/D \) where: - \( A \): arrival process (M = Markov/Poisson, D = Deterministic, G = General) - \( B \): service time distribution - \( c \): number of servers - \( K \): system capacity (optional) - \( N \): population size (optional) - \( D \): service discipline (optional)

For an \( M/M/1 \) queue: arrivals follow a Poisson process with rate \( \lambda \), service times are exponential with rate \( \mu \), and there is one server.

The traffic intensity is \( \rho = \lambda / (c\mu) \). The system is stable if \( \rho < 1 \).

Theorem and Proof

Theorem (Little's Law): For a stable queueing system in steady state: \[L = \lambda W\] where \( L \) is the average number of customers in the system, \( \lambda \) is the arrival rate, and \( W \) is the average time spent in the system. Similarly, \( L_q = \lambda W_q \) for the queue (excluding service).

Proof Sketch: Consider customers arriving during a long time interval \( [0, T] \). Let \( N(T) \) be the number of arrivals and \( A_i, D_i \) be the arrival and departure times of customer \( i \).

The total time spent in system by all customers is: \[\sum_{i=1}^{N(T)} (D_i - A_i) = \int_0^T L(t) dt\]

where \( L(t) \) is the number in system at time \( t \).

Dividing by \( N(T) \) and taking limits: \[W = \lim_{T \to \infty} \frac{\int_0^T L(t) dt}{N(T)} = \lim_{T \to \infty} \frac{\int_0^T L(t) dt}{T} \cdot \frac{T}{N(T)} = \frac{L}{\lambda}\]

Thus \( L = \lambda W \). ∎

Notation Guide

  • \( \lambda \) — arrival rate
  • \( \mu \) — service rate (per server)
  • \( \rho = \lambda / (c\mu) \) — traffic intensity
  • \( L \) — average number in system
  • \( L_q \) — average number in queue
  • \( W \) — average time in system
  • \( W_q \) — average waiting time in queue

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def mm1_queue_metrics(lam, mu):
    """
    Calculate performance metrics for M/M/1 queue.
    """
    rho = lam / mu
    if rho >= 1:
        return None  # Unstable
    
    L = rho / (1 - rho)  # Average in system
    Lq = rho**2 / (1 - rho)  # Average in queue
    W = 1 / (mu - lam)  # Average time in system
    Wq = rho / (mu - lam)  # Average waiting time
    
    return {
        'rho': rho,
        'L': L,
        'Lq': Lq,
        'W': W,
        'Wq': Wq,
        'P0': 1 - rho  # Probability system is empty
    }

def mm1_steady_state_probs(lam, mu, max_n=20):
    """Steady-state probabilities P_n for M/M/1 queue."""
    rho = lam / mu
    return [(1 - rho) * rho**n for n in range(max_n + 1)]

def mmc_queue_metrics(lam, mu, c):
    """Calculate metrics for M/M/c queue."""
    rho = lam / (c * mu)
    if rho >= 1:
        return None
    
    # Erlang C formula
    r = lam / mu
    sum_terms = sum(r**n / np.math.factorial(n) for n in range(c))
    erlang_c_num = (r**c / np.math.factorial(c)) * (c * mu / (c * mu - lam))
    erlang_c_den = sum_terms + erlang_c_num
    C = erlang_c_num / erlang_c_den
    
    Lq = C * rho / (1 - rho)
    Wq = Lq / lam
    W = Wq + 1/mu
    L = lam * W
    
    return {
        'rho': rho,
        'L': L,
        'Lq': Lq,
        'W': W,
        'Wq': Wq,
        'C': C  # Erlang C - probability of waiting
    }

Algebraic Topology: Homotopy Groups

Tags: algebraic-topology homotopy-theory higher-homotopy

Historical Context

Algebraic topology emerged in the early 20th century as mathematicians sought to classify spaces using algebraic invariants. While homology groups capture "holes" of various dimensions, homotopy groups provide a more refined invariant. The higher homotopy groups \( \pi_n(X) \) for \( n \geq 2 \) were introduced by Witold Hurewicz in 1935. Remarkably, these groups are abelian for \( n \geq 2 \), unlike the fundamental group. Computing homotopy groups of spheres remains one of the most challenging problems in mathematics, with connections to number theory, algebraic K-theory, and physics.

Definitions

The n-th homotopy group \( \pi_n(X, x_0) \) consists of homotopy classes of maps \( f: (S^n, s_0) \to (X, x_0) \), where the group operation is induced by concatenation of spheres.

A space is n-connected if \( \pi_k(X) = 0 \) for all \( k \leq n \).

The Hurewicz homomorphism \( h: \pi_n(X) \to H_n(X) \) sends a homotopy class to its image under the induced map on homology.

Theorem and Proof

Theorem (Hurewicz): Let \( X \) be an \( (n-1) \)-connected space with \( n \geq 2 \). Then \( \tilde{H}_k(X) = 0 \) for \( k < n \), and the Hurewicz homomorphism \( h: \pi_n(X) \to H_n(X) \) is an isomorphism.

Proof Sketch: The proof proceeds by induction on a Postnikov tower or using the long exact sequence of a fibration.

Key steps:

1. For \( n = 1 \), \( \pi_1(X) \) may not equal \( H_1(X) \) (they differ by abelianization).

2. For \( n \geq 2 \), use the fact that \( S^n \) has a cell structure with one 0-cell and one n-cell.

3. Any map \( f: S^n \to X \) can be homotoped to a cellular map. Since \( X \) has no cells below dimension \( n \), the cellular approximation theorem implies that maps factor through the n-skeleton.

4. The Hurewicz map is surjective because every n-cycle can be represented by a map from \( S^n \).

5. Injectivity follows from the homotopy addition theorem and the fact that homotopic maps induce the same homology class. ∎

Notation Guide

  • \( \pi_n(X, x_0) \) — n-th homotopy group
  • \( S^n \) — n-dimensional sphere
  • \( H_n(X) \) — n-th homology group
  • \( h: \pi_n(X) \to H_n(X) \) — Hurewicz homomorphism
  • \( \Omega X \) — loop space of \( X \)
  • \( \pi_n(X) = \pi_{n-1}(\Omega X) \) — looping isomorphism

Symplectic Geometry: The Geometry of Classical Mechanics

Tags: symplectic-geometry classical-mechanics differential-geometry

Historical Context

Symplectic geometry emerged from the study of classical mechanics in the 19th century. Hamilton's reformulation of Newtonian mechanics revealed that phase space—the space of positions and momenta—carries a natural geometric structure. This structure, now called a symplectic form, was systematically studied by Élie Cartan and later by Vladimir Arnold. Symplectic geometry has grown into a rich field with deep connections to topology, algebraic geometry, and quantum mechanics. The field received renewed attention with the development of Gromov's theory of pseudoholomorphic curves and the proof of the Arnold conjecture.

Definitions

A symplectic manifold is a pair \( (M, \omega) \) where \( M \) is a smooth manifold and \( \omega \) is a closed, non-degenerate 2-form (the symplectic form).

The Darboux theorem states that locally, every symplectic manifold looks like \( \mathbb{R}^{2n} \) with coordinates \( (q_1, \ldots, q_n, p_1, \ldots, p_n) \) and symplectic form \( \omega = \sum_{i=1}^n dq_i \wedge dp_i \).

A Hamiltonian vector field \( X_H \) is defined by \( \iota_{X_H}\omega = dH \) for a Hamiltonian function \( H: M \to \mathbb{R} \).

Theorem and Proof

Theorem (Liouville): The flow of a Hamiltonian vector field preserves the symplectic volume form \( \omega^n/n! \). Equivalently, Hamiltonian flows are volume-preserving.

Proof: Let \( \phi_t \) be the flow of \( X_H \). We need to show \( \phi_t^*\omega = \omega \).

Compute the Lie derivative: \[\mathcal{L}_{X_H}\omega = d(\iota_{X_H}\omega) + \iota_{X_H}(d\omega) = d(dH) + 0 = 0\]

where we used Cartan's magic formula and the fact that \( d\omega = 0 \).

Since \( \frac{d}{dt}\phi_t^*\omega = \phi_t^*\mathcal{L}_{X_H}\omega = 0 \), we have \( \phi_t^*\omega = \phi_0^*\omega = \omega \).

The volume form is \( \Omega = \omega^n/n! \), so \[\phi_t^*\Omega = (\phi_t^*\omega)^n/n! = \omega^n/n! = \Omega\]

Thus Hamiltonian flows preserve volume. ∎

Notation Guide

  • \( (M, \omega) \) — symplectic manifold
  • \( \omega \in \Omega^2(M) \) — symplectic form
  • \( \iota_X \omega \) — interior product (contraction)
  • \( X_H \) — Hamiltonian vector field
  • \( \{f, g\} = \omega(X_f, X_g) \) — Poisson bracket
  • \( \omega^n/n! \) — Liouville volume form

Model Theory: The Study of Mathematical Structures

Tags: model-theory logic first-order

Historical Context

Model theory, a branch of mathematical logic, studies the relationship between formal languages and their interpretations (models). Founded by Alfred Tarski and Abraham Robinson in the mid-20th century, the field has evolved from foundational questions to become a powerful tool in algebra, number theory, and geometry. The spectacular applications of model theory include Ax-Kochen's solution of Artin's conjecture on p-adic fields, Hrushovski's proof of the Mordell-Lang conjecture for function fields, and recent work on o-minimal structures by Wilkie and others.

Definitions

A structure \( \mathcal{M} = (M, \ldots) \) consists of a domain \( M \) together with interpretations of function symbols, relation symbols, and constant symbols from a signature \( \mathcal{L} \).

A theory \( T \) is a set of sentences in a first-order language \( \mathcal{L} \). A model of \( T \) is a structure \( \mathcal{M} \) where all sentences in \( T \) are true (written \( \mathcal{M} \models T \)).

A theory is complete if for every sentence \( \phi \), either \( T \models \phi \) or \( T \models \neg\phi \).

Theorem and Proof

Theorem (Compactness): A set of first-order sentences \( \Sigma \) has a model if and only if every finite subset of \( \Sigma \) has a model.

Proof Sketch: The proof uses the completeness theorem for first-order logic.

(⇒) If \( \mathcal{M} \models \Sigma \), then clearly \( \mathcal{M} \models \Sigma_0 \) for any finite \( \Sigma_0 \subseteq \Sigma \).

(⇐) Suppose every finite subset of \( \Sigma \) has a model, but \( \Sigma \) has no model. By completeness, \( \Sigma \) is inconsistent: there exists a contradiction \( \phi \wedge \neg\phi \) provable from \( \Sigma \).

Since proofs are finite, only finitely many axioms from \( \Sigma \) are used. Thus some finite \( \Sigma_0 \subseteq \Sigma \) proves a contradiction, contradicting that \( \Sigma_0 \) has a model. ∎

Notation Guide

  • \( \mathcal{L} \) — first-order language/signature
  • \( \mathcal{M} = (M, \ldots) \) — structure with domain \( M \)
  • \( \mathcal{M} \models \phi \) — structure satisfies formula
  • \( T \models \phi \) — theory entails formula
  • \( \text{Th}(\mathcal{M}) \) — complete theory of a structure

Grigori Perelman and the Poincaré Conjecture

Tags: perelman geometric-topology ricci-flow

Historical Context

The Poincaré Conjecture, posed by Henri Poincaré in 1904, stood as one of the most famous unsolved problems in mathematics for nearly a century. It asked whether every simply connected, closed 3-manifold is homeomorphic to the 3-sphere. In 2002-2003, Grigori Perelman, a reclusive Russian mathematician, posted three papers on the arXiv claiming to prove the conjecture as a corollary of a more general result: the Geometrization Conjecture of William Thurston. Perelman used Richard Hamilton's Ricci flow, a process that evolves the geometry of a manifold analogously to how heat flow smooths out temperature distributions.

Definitions

A 3-manifold is a space that locally looks like \( \mathbb{R}^3 \). It is closed if it is compact and has no boundary.

A manifold is simply connected if every loop can be continuously contracted to a point.

The Ricci flow is the partial differential equation: \[\frac{\partial g}{\partial t} = -2\text{Ric}(g)\] where \( g \) is the Riemannian metric and \( \text{Ric}(g) \) is the Ricci curvature tensor.

Theorem and Proof

Theorem (Perelman, 2002-2003): Every closed 3-manifold with finite fundamental group is a spherical space form (hence the Poincaré Conjecture is true).

Proof Sketch: The proof uses Hamilton's Ricci flow with surgery.

Step 1: Start with an arbitrary Riemannian metric on the 3-manifold and evolve it by Ricci flow. The Ricci flow tends to make the curvature more uniform, similar to how heat flow makes temperature uniform.

Step 2: Singularities may form where curvature blows up. Perelman showed these singularities have a specific structure: they are modeled on ancient solutions that look like shrinking spheres or cylinders.

Step 3: Before singularities form, perform "surgery" by cutting out the high-curvature regions and gluing in standard pieces. This produces a new manifold.

Step 4: Perelman's entropy functionals (the \( \mathcal{W} \) and \( \mathcal{F} \) functionals) are monotone under Ricci flow and provide enough control to show that after finitely many surgeries, the manifold becomes extinct or converges to a geometric structure.

Step 5: For simply connected manifolds, the only possible limit is the 3-sphere. ∎

  • \( \pi_1(M) \) — fundamental group

📜 Math History: The Birth of Probability Theory

The origins of probability theory are often traced to a correspondence between Blaise Pascal and Pierre de Fermat in 1654. The problem that sparked their exchange was deceptively simple: how should the stakes be divided if a gambling game is interrupted before completion? Yet this question led to the first rigorous mathematical treatment of chance, establishing what we now call the concept of expected value.

The problem, known as the "problem of points," concerned two players who had staked equal money on a game of chance. If the game is interrupted when one player needs two more wins to claim the pot while the other needs three, how should the money be divided? Before Pascal and Fermat, various heuristic solutions had been proposed, but none were mathematically satisfactory.

Pascal's approach was to consider what would happen if the game continued. He enumerated all possible outcomes and calculated how many favored each player. Fermat, meanwhile, used a more elegant method based on combinations. Both arrived at the same answer: the stakes should be divided in proportion to each player's probability of winning.

The significance of their work extended far beyond gambling. Christiaan Huygens, learning of their results, published the first book on probability theory in 1657. The field grew rapidly through the 18th century with contributions from Jacob Bernoulli, who proved the law of large numbers, and Abraham de Moivre, who derived the normal approximation to the binomial distribution.

Bernoulli's "Ars Conjectandi" (1713) was particularly influential. In it, he developed the theory of permutations and combinations, introduced Bernoulli numbers, and most importantly, provided the first proof that observed frequencies converge to theoretical probabilities as the number of trials increases—a result that justified the application of probability to real-world phenomena.

By the 19th century, probability had expanded from games of chance to encompass statistics, physics, and the social sciences. Laplace's "Analytical Theory of Probabilities" (1812) systematized the field and applied it to astronomical observations, jurisprudence, and even the existence of God. The foundations were further refined by Kolmogorov in 1933, who provided the measure-theoretic axiomatization that remains standard today.

Putnam Competition 2022 Problem B5

Let \( n \) be a positive integer. Determine the number of functions \( f: \{1, 2, \ldots, n\} \to \{1, 2, \ldots, n\} \) such that for all \( 1 \leq i \leq n \), the sequence \[i, f(i), f(f(i)), f(f(f(i))), \ldots\] eventually stabilizes at 1 (i.e., there exists \( k \) such that \( f^{(k)}(i) = 1 \)).

Hint

Think about the functional graph of such functions. What structure must it have? Consider the tree rooted at 1, and think about how other nodes must connect to it. Use recurrence relations or generating functions.

Solution

Step 1: Understand the structure. The condition means that 1 is a global attractor: every element eventually maps to 1. In the functional graph, this means the graph is a rooted tree at 1 (possibly with additional edges forming cycles that lead to 1, but actually since everything must reach 1, there can be no other cycles).

Step 2: The functional graph consists of a rooted tree with root 1, where edges are directed toward the root. Every node has exactly one outgoing edge (its image under \( f \)), and following edges always leads to 1.

Step 3: Let \( a_n \) be the number of such functions on \( \{1, \ldots, n\} \). We need \( f(1) = 1 \) (since 1 must eventually reach itself, and if \( f(1) \neq 1 \), then 1 would eventually map to something else, contradiction).

Step 4: For each other element \( i \in \{2, \ldots, n\} \), we need \( f(i) \) to be an element that eventually reaches 1. Consider building such functions recursively.

Step 5: For element \( n \), we can choose \( f(n) \) to be any of \( 1, 2, \ldots, n \) such that the resulting function still satisfies the property. The key insight: if we remove element \( n \), we need a valid function on \( n-1 \) elements. Then we add \( n \) back by choosing its image.

Step 6: Actually, let's use a different approach. The condition is equivalent to saying that the functional graph is a rooted tree at 1. The number of functions where every element eventually maps to 1 is the number of rooted trees on \( n \) labeled nodes with root 1, where edges point toward the root.

Step 7: For each node \( i \neq 1 \), we choose its parent \( f(i) \) from \( \{1, \ldots, n\} \setminus \{i\} \) such that no cycles are formed except the self-loop at 1. This is equivalent to counting forests of rooted trees with root 1.

Step 8: By Cayley's formula generalization: the number of rooted forests on \( n \) labeled vertices with specified roots is \( n^{n-k-1} \prod_{i=1}^{k} r_i \) where \( r_i \) are root sizes... Actually, let's use a cleaner approach.

Step 9: The answer is \( n^{n-1} \). Here's why: we need a functional digraph where 1 is the unique cycle (a fixed point), and all other nodes eventually flow into 1. This is equivalent to a rooted tree with root 1, but with edges directed toward the root. The number of such functions equals the number of trees on \( n \) labeled nodes with root 1, which by Cayley's formula is \( n^{n-2} \). But wait—we need to check this.

Step 10: Actually, the answer is \( n^{n-1} \). The number of functions where every element eventually reaches 1 is the number of rooted trees with root 1, where each non-root node chooses its parent. By a standard result, this equals \( n^{n-1} \).

Answer: The number of such functions is \( n^{n-1} \). ∎

🎨 Visualization of the Day: The Lorenz Attractor

The Lorenz attractor is a set of chaotic solutions to the Lorenz system, a simplified mathematical model for atmospheric convection. First studied by Edward Lorenz in 1963, it became a cornerstone of chaos theory, demonstrating how deterministic systems can exhibit unpredictable behavior.

The Lorenz system is defined by three ordinary differential equations: \[\frac{dx}{dt} = \sigma(y - x)\] \[\frac{dy}{dt} = x(\rho - z) - y\] \[\frac{dz}{dt} = xy - \beta z\]

With parameters \( \sigma = 10 \), \( \rho = 28 \), and \( \beta = 8/3 \), the system exhibits chaotic behavior. Trajectories spiral around two "wings" of a butterfly-shaped attractor, unpredictably switching from one wing to the other. This sensitive dependence on initial conditions—the butterfly effect—means that arbitrarily small changes in initial state lead to dramatically different outcomes.

Python Code

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from mpl_toolkits.mplot3d import Axes3D

def lorenz_system(state, sigma=10, rho=28, beta=8/3):
    """
    Lorenz system of ODEs.
    """
    x, y, z = state
    dxdt = sigma * (y - x)
    dydt = x * (rho - z) - y
    dzdt = x * y - beta * z
    return np.array([dxdt, dydt, dzdt])

def rk4_step(f, y, dt):
    """Single step of Runge-Kutta 4th order method."""
    k1 = f(y)
    k2 = f(y + 0.5 * dt * k1)
    k3 = f(y + 0.5 * dt * k2)
    k4 = f(y + dt * k3)
    return y + (dt / 6) * (k1 + 2*k2 + 2*k3 + k4)

def simulate_lorenz(initial_state, dt=0.01, n_steps=10000):
    """Simulate Lorenz system trajectory."""
    trajectory = np.zeros((n_steps, 3))
    trajectory[0] = initial_state
    
    state = initial_state.copy()
    for i in range(1, n_steps):
        state = rk4_step(lorenz_system, state, dt)
        trajectory[i] = state
    
    return trajectory

print("=== Lorenz Attractor Simulation ===")

# Simulate two trajectories with slightly different initial conditions
initial1 = np.array([1.0, 1.0, 1.0])
initial2 = np.array([1.001, 1.0, 1.0])  # Tiny perturbation

traj1 = simulate_lorenz(initial1, dt=0.01, n_steps=15000)
traj2 = simulate_lorenz(initial2, dt=0.01, n_steps=15000)

# Calculate divergence
distance = np.linalg.norm(traj1 - traj2, axis=1)
print(f"Initial distance: {distance[0]:.6f}")
print(f"Distance after 1000 steps: {distance[1000]:.6f}")
print(f"Distance after 5000 steps: {distance[5000]:.6f}")
print(f"Distance after 10000 steps: {distance[10000]:.6f}")

# Estimate Lyapunov exponent
# (exponential growth rate of perturbations)
t_fit = np.arange(100, 1000)
lyapunov = np.polyfit(t_fit * 0.01, np.log(distance[t_fit]), 1)[0]
print(f"\nEstimated largest Lyapunov exponent: {lyapunov:.4f}")
print("(Positive value indicates chaos)")

# Visualization
fig = plt.figure(figsize=(16, 12))

# 3D plot
ax1 = fig.add_subplot(2, 2, 1, projection='3d')
ax1.plot(traj1[:, 0], traj1[:, 1], traj1[:, 2], 'b-', linewidth=0.5, alpha=0.8)
ax1.set_xlabel('X')
ax1.set_ylabel('Y')
ax1.set_zlabel('Z')
ax1.set_title('Lorenz Attractor (3D)')

# XY projection
ax2 = fig.add_subplot(2, 2, 2)
ax2.plot(traj1[:, 0], traj1[:, 1], 'r-', linewidth=0.5, alpha=0.8)
ax2.set_xlabel('X')
ax2.set_ylabel('Y')
ax2.set_title('XY Projection')
ax2.grid(True, alpha=0.3)

# XZ projection
ax3 = fig.add_subplot(2, 2, 3)
ax3.plot(traj1[:, 0], traj1[:, 2], 'g-', linewidth=0.5, alpha=0.8)
ax3.set_xlabel('X')
ax3.set_ylabel('Z')
ax3.set_title('XZ Projection')
ax3.grid(True, alpha=0.3)

# Divergence of nearby trajectories (butterfly effect)
ax4 = fig.add_subplot(2, 2, 4)
t = np.arange(len(distance)) * 0.01
ax4.semilogy(t, distance, 'b-', linewidth=1)
ax4.set_xlabel('Time')
ax4.set_ylabel('Distance between trajectories')
ax4.set_title('Butterfly Effect: Sensitive Dependence')
ax4.grid(True, alpha=0.3)

plt.tight_layout()
plt.savefig('lorenz_attractor.png', dpi=150)
plt.close()

print("\nVisualization saved to lorenz_attractor.png")

print("\n=== Key Properties ===")
print("1. Deterministic: No randomness in the equations")
print("2. Chaotic: Sensitive dependence on initial conditions")
print("3. Attractor: Trajectories converge to a bounded set")
print("4. Strange: Fractal structure with non-integer dimension")
print(f"5. Lyapunov exponent ≈ {lyapunov:.2f} > 0: Chaos confirmed")
