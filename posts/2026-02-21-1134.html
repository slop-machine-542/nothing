Geometric Group Theory: Groups as Geometric Objects

geometric-group-theory

Motivation

Geometric group theory emerged in the late 20th century from the insight that finitely generated groups can be studied through their actions on metric spaces. By viewing groups geometrically, we can translate algebraic properties into geometric features. The Cayley graph of a group provides a way to visualize group structure, while quasi-isometry allows us to classify groups up to their large-scale geometry. This field connects group theory to topology, geometry, and dynamics, with applications to 3-manifold topology, rigidity theory, and the study of automorphism groups.

Definitions

  • Cayley Graph: For a group \( G \) with generating set \( S \), the Cayley graph \( \Gamma(G, S) \) has vertices corresponding to group elements and edges connecting \( g \) to \( gs \) for each \( s \in S \).
  • Word Metric: The distance \( d_S(g, h) \) is the length of the shortest word in \( S \cup S^{-1} \) representing \( g^{-1}h \).
  • Quasi-Isometry: A map \( f: X \to Y \) between metric spaces is a quasi-isometry if there exist constants \( K \geq 1 \), \( C \geq 0 \) such that: \[\frac{1}{K}d_X(x, x') - C \leq d_Y(f(x), f(x')) \leq Kd_X(x, x') + C\] and every point in \( Y \) is within distance \( C \) of the image.
  • Hyperbolic Group: A group whose Cayley graph is Gromov hyperbolic: all geodesic triangles are uniformly thin.
  • CAT(0) Space: A geodesic metric space where triangles are "thinner" than their comparison triangles in Euclidean space.
  • Gromov Boundary: The set of equivalence classes of geodesic rays in a hyperbolic space, compactifying the space at infinity.

Theorem: The Švarc-Milnor Lemma

Theorem (Švarc-Milnor): Let \( G \) be a group acting properly discontinuously and cocompactly by isometries on a proper geodesic metric space \( X \). Then \( G \) is finitely generated, and for any basepoint \( x_0 \in X \), the orbit map \( g \mapsto g \cdot x_0 \) is a quasi-isometry between \( G \) (with word metric) and \( X \).

Proof Sketch

Since the action is cocompact, there exists a compact set \( K \) whose \( G \)-translates cover \( X \). Let \( D \) be the diameter of \( K \). Choose a finite generating set \( S \) consisting of elements that move \( x_0 \) by at most \( 3D \).

To show the orbit map is a quasi-isometry, we establish bounds in both directions. For the upper bound, if \( d_S(g, h) = n \), then \( g^{-1}h = s_1 \cdots s_n \), so: \[d_X(g \cdot x_0, h \cdot x_0) = d_X(x_0, g^{-1}h \cdot x_0) \leq \sum_{i=1}^n d_X(x_0, s_i \cdot x_0) \leq 3Dn\]

For the lower bound, given a path from \( g \cdot x_0 \) to \( h \cdot x_0 \) in \( X \), cover it by translates of \( K \). The sequence of translates gives a word in \( S \) representing \( g^{-1}h \), yielding a linear lower bound on word length in terms of distance.

Notation Guide

  • \( \Gamma(G, S) \): Cayley graph of group \( G \) with generators \( S \)
  • \( d_S \): Word metric with respect to generating set \( S \)
  • \( |g|_S \): Word length of \( g \) in generators \( S \)
  • \( \partial G \): Gromov boundary of hyperbolic group \( G \)
  • \( \delta \): Hyperbolicity constant
  • \( QI(X, Y) \): Space of quasi-isometries between \( X \) and \( Y \)
  • \( \text{Comm}(G) \): Abstract commensurator of \( G \)
  • \( \text{Out}(G) \): Outer automorphism group of \( G \)
  • \( \text{Mod}(S) \): Mapping class group of surface \( S \)

from typing import Set, Tuple, List, Dict
from collections import deque

class FinitelyGeneratedGroup:
    """Represents a finitely generated group with word metric."""
    
    def __init__(self, generators: Set[str], relations: List[Tuple[str, str]] = None):
        self.generators = generators
        self.relations = relations or []
        self.inv_map = {g: g + "⁻¹" for g in generators}
        self.inv_map.update({g + "⁻¹": g for g in generators})
    
    def reduce_word(self, word: str) -> str:
        """Reduce word using group relations (simplified)."""
        # Cancel adjacent inverses
        result = []
        for char in word:
            if result and self.inv_map.get(result[-1]) == char:
                result.pop()
            else:
                result.append(char)
        return ''.join(result)
    
    def word_metric(self, g: str, h: str) -> int:
        """Distance between group elements (simplified for free group)."""
        # For free group, distance is length of reduced word representing g⁻¹h
        g_inv = ''.join(reversed([self.inv_map.get(c, c) for c in g]))
        product = self.reduce_word(g_inv + h)
        return len(product)

class CayleyGraph:
    """Cayley graph visualization and analysis."""
    
    def __init__(self, group: FinitelyGeneratedGroup, radius: int = 3):
        self.group = group
        self.radius = radius
        self.vertices = self._build_ball()
        self.edges = self._build_edges()
    
    def _build_ball(self) -> Set[str]:
        """Build ball of given radius in Cayley graph."""
        ball = {""}  # Identity
        frontier = {""}
        
        for _ in range(self.radius):
            new_frontier = set()
            for word in frontier:
                for gen in self.group.generators:
                    for g in [gen, self.group.inv_map[gen]]:
                        new_word = self.group.reduce_word(word + g)
                        if new_word not in ball:
                            ball.add(new_word)
                            new_frontier.add(new_word)
            frontier = new_frontier
        
        return ball
    
    def _build_edges(self) -> List[Tuple[str, str]]:
        """Build edges in the ball."""
        edges = []
        for word in self.vertices:
            for gen in self.group.generators:
                neighbor = self.group.reduce_word(word + gen)
                if neighbor in self.vertices:
                    edges.append((word if word else "e", neighbor if neighbor else "e"))
        return edges
    
    def growth_function(self, n: int) -> int:
        """Number of elements in ball of radius n."""
        return len([v for v in self.vertices if len(v) <= n])

Complex Dynamics: Iteration in the Complex Plane

complex-dynamics

Motivation

Complex dynamics studies the iteration of holomorphic functions, revealing intricate fractal structures that emerge from simple recursive formulas. Beginning with the work of Fatou and Julia in the early 20th century, the field lay dormant until computer graphics revealed the stunning beauty of the Mandelbrot set. The subject connects complex analysis, hyperbolic geometry, and ergodic theory, with applications to Newton's method, the study of Kleinian groups, and even the dynamics of quadratic polynomials. The interplay between the geometry of Julia sets and the parameter space of the Mandelbrot set remains an active area of research.

Definitions

  • Julia Set \( J(f) \): The boundary of the set of points with bounded orbits under iteration of \( f \). Equivalently, the closure of the set of repelling periodic points.
  • Fatou Set \( F(f) \): The complement of the Julia set; the domain of normality where iterates form a normal family.
  • Periodic Point: A point \( z \) such that \( f^n(z) = z \) for some \( n \geq 1 \). The multiplier is \( \lambda = (f^n)'(z) \).
  • Classification of Periodic Points:
    • Attracting: \( |\lambda| < 1 \)
    • Repelling: \( |\lambda| > 1 \)
    • Indifferent: \( |\lambda| = 1 \) (parabolic if \( \lambda \) is root of unity, irrationally indifferent otherwise)
  • Mandelbrot Set: For the family \( f_c(z) = z^2 + c \), the set of parameters \( c \) for which the orbit of 0 remains bounded.
  • Connectedness Locus: The set of parameters for which the Julia set is connected.

Theorem: The Fundamental Dichotomy

Theorem (Fatou-Julia): For a polynomial \( f \) of degree \( d \geq 2 \), exactly one of the following holds:

  1. All critical orbits remain bounded, the filled Julia set \( K(f) \) is connected, and \( J(f) \) is connected.
  2. Some critical orbit escapes to infinity, \( K(f) \) is a Cantor set of infinitely many components, and \( J(f) \) is totally disconnected.

For the quadratic family \( f_c(z) = z^2 + c \), case (1) occurs if and only if \( c \in M \) (the Mandelbrot set).

Proof Sketch

The proof relies on the Bottcher coordinate near infinity. For large \( |z| \), there exists a conformal map \( \phi \) conjugating \( f \) to \( z \mapsto z^d \), i.e., \( \phi(f(z)) = \phi(z)^d \).

If all critical orbits are bounded, the Bottcher coordinate extends to the entire basin of infinity, and the Julia set is the boundary of this basin, hence connected.

If a critical orbit escapes, the Bottcher coordinate cannot extend through the critical point. The preimage structure creates infinitely many components, each homeomorphic to a disk, accumulating on a Cantor set. The dynamics on the Julia set is conjugate to the full shift on \( d \) symbols.

Notation Guide

  • \( f^n \): n-th iterate of \( f \)
  • \( J(f) \): Julia set of \( f \)
  • \( F(f) \): Fatou set of \( f \)
  • \( K(f) \): Filled Julia set
  • \( M \): Mandelbrot set
  • \( f_c(z) = z^2 + c \): Quadratic family
  • \( \text{Crit}(f) \): Set of critical points
  • \( \lambda = (f^n)'(z_0) \): Multiplier of periodic point
  • \( B(\infty) \): Basin of attraction to infinity
  • \( \phi_c \): Bottcher coordinate for \( f_c \)

Higher Category Theory: Beyond Categories

higher-category-theory

Motivation

While ordinary category theory studies objects and morphisms between them, many mathematical structures naturally involve higher-order relationships. In topology, we have paths between points, homotopies between paths, and higher homotopies between homotopies. Higher category theory provides a framework for these structures, where we have morphisms between morphisms (2-morphisms), morphisms between 2-morphisms (3-morphisms), and so on. This perspective unifies concepts from homotopy theory, algebraic geometry, and mathematical physics, providing the language for derived algebraic geometry, topological quantum field theory, and the study of homotopy types.

Definitions

  • 2-Category: A category enriched over Cat: objects, 1-morphisms between objects, and 2-morphisms between 1-morphisms, with horizontal and vertical composition.
  • Bicategory: A weakened 2-category where associativity and unit laws hold only up to coherent isomorphism.
  • ∞-Category: A higher category where all morphisms above some dimension are invertible (quasi-category or complete Segal space model).
  • Quasi-Category: A simplicial set satisfying the inner horn filling condition (weak Kan complex).
  • Homotopy Category: For an ∞-category \( \mathcal{C} \), the ordinary category \( h\mathcal{C} \) with the same objects but homotopy classes of morphisms.
  • Mapping Space: For objects \( X, Y \) in an ∞-category, the simplicial set \( \text{Map}(X, Y) \) of morphisms.

Theorem: The Homotopy Hypothesis

Theorem (Grothendieck, Baez-Dolan, Lurie): There is an equivalence of (∞,1)-categories between:

  1. Homotopy n-types (spaces with trivial homotopy groups above dimension n)
  2. n-Groupoids (n-categories where all k-morphisms are invertible for \( k \leq n \))

In particular, homotopy types correspond to ∞-groupoids. This justifies the fundamental ∞-groupoid construction \( \Pi_\infty(X) \) of a space.

Proof Sketch

The proof in the (∞,1)-categorical setting uses the theory of quasi-categories. The key steps are:

  1. Define the fundamental ∞-groupoid \( \Pi_\infty(X) \) of a Kan complex \( X \) as the simplicial set itself (every Kan complex is a quasi-category).
  2. Show that the geometric realization functor \( |\cdot| \) from simplicial sets to topological spaces and the singular complex functor \( \text{Sing} \) form a Quillen equivalence.
  3. Prove that weak homotopy equivalences correspond to categorical equivalences of ∞-groupoids.
  4. Use the Whitehead theorem for spaces: a map inducing isomorphisms on all homotopy groups is a weak equivalence.

The result establishes that the homotopy theory of spaces is equivalent to the theory of ∞-groupoids, validating the intuition that homotopy theory is the study of higher-dimensional paths.

Notation Guide

  • \( \mathcal{C}, \mathcal{D} \): (∞,1)-categories (quasi-categories)
  • \( \text{Cat}_\infty \): (∞,1)-category of small ∞-categories
  • \( \mathcal{S} \): ∞-category of spaces (\(\infty\)-groupoids)
  • \( \text{Map}_{\mathcal{C}}(X, Y) \): Mapping space in \( \mathcal{C} \)
  • \( h\mathcal{C} \): Homotopy category of \( \mathcal{C} \)
  • \( \Pi_\infty(X) \): Fundamental ∞-groupoid of space \( X \)
  • \( \Delta^n \): Standard n-simplex
  • \( \Lambda^n_i \): i-th horn of n-simplex
  • \( \text{Set}_\Delta \): Category of simplicial sets

Mathematical Epidemiology: Modeling Disease Spread

mathematical-epidemiology

Real-World Motivation

Mathematical epidemiology provides the quantitative framework for understanding how infectious diseases spread through populations. From the 1918 influenza pandemic to COVID-19, mathematical models have guided public health policy, predicting outbreak trajectories and evaluating intervention strategies. The field combines differential equations, stochastic processes, and network theory to capture transmission dynamics. Modern challenges include modeling emerging pathogens, understanding vaccine hesitancy, and addressing health disparities. The COVID-19 pandemic demonstrated both the power and limitations of epidemiological models, highlighting the need for real-time data integration and uncertainty quantification.

Definitions

  • SIR Model: Compartmental model dividing population into Susceptible (\( S \)), Infected (\( I \)), and Recovered (\( R \)): \[\frac{dS}{dt} = -\beta SI, \quad \frac{dI}{dt} = \beta SI - \gamma I, \quad \frac{dR}{dt} = \gamma I\]
  • Basic Reproduction Number \( R_0 \): The expected number of secondary infections from a single infected individual in a fully susceptible population. For SIR: \( R_0 = \frac{\beta N}{\gamma} \).
  • Effective Reproduction Number \( R_t \): The time-varying reproduction number accounting for accumulated immunity and interventions.
  • Herd Immunity Threshold: The proportion of population that must be immune to prevent sustained transmission: \( p_c = 1 - \frac{1}{R_0} \).
  • SEIR Model: Extension with Exposed (\( E \)) compartment for latent period.
  • Next-Generation Matrix: Method for computing \( R_0 \) in structured models with multiple infected compartments.

Theorem: Threshold Behavior in SIR Models

Theorem: Consider the SIR model with initial conditions \( S(0) = S_0 \approx N \), \( I(0) = I_0 > 0 \), \( R(0) = 0 \).

  1. If \( R_0 < 1 \), the disease dies out exponentially: \( I(t) \to 0 \) as \( t \to \infty \).
  2. If \( R_0 > 1 \), an epidemic occurs with \( I(t) \) initially increasing. The final epidemic size \( R_\infty \) satisfies: \[R_\infty = N\left(1 - e^{-R_0 \cdot R_\infty/N}\right]\]

Proof Sketch

From the SIR equations, we derive the conserved quantity: \[\frac{dI}{dS} = -1 + \frac{\gamma}{\beta S}\]

Integrating from initial to final state (where \( I = 0 \)): \[I_\infty - I_0 = -(S_\infty - S_0) + \frac{\gamma}{\beta}\ln\frac{S_\infty}{S_0}\]

Since \( I_\infty = 0 \) and \( S_0 + I_0 = N \), we obtain: \[S_\infty = S_0 \exp\left(-\frac{\beta}{\gamma}(N - S_\infty)\right)\]

Using \( R_0 = \frac{\beta N}{\gamma} \) and \( R_\infty = N - S_\infty \), the final size equation follows. The threshold at \( R_0 = 1 \) follows from linear stability analysis of the disease-free equilibrium.

Notation Guide

  • \( S(t) \): Number of susceptible individuals
  • \( I(t) \): Number of infected individuals
  • \( R(t) \): Number of recovered/removed individuals
  • \( N = S + I + R \): Total population
  • \( \beta \): Transmission rate
  • \( \gamma = 1/D \): Recovery rate (\( D \) = infectious period)
  • \( R_0 = \beta/\gamma \): Basic reproduction number (normalized)
  • \( s = S/N, i = I/N, r = R/N \): Fractions
  • \( \mathcal{R}_0 \): Spectral radius of next-generation matrix
  • \( F, V \): New infection and transition matrices

import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import odeint
from scipy.optimize import fsolve

class SIRModel:
    """Standard SIR compartmental model."""
    
    def __init__(self, beta: float, gamma: float, N: float):
        self.beta = beta
        self.gamma = gamma
        self.N = N
        self.R0 = beta / gamma
    
    def derivatives(self, state, t):
        """Compute dS/dt, dI/dt, dR/dt."""
        S, I, R = state
        dSdt = -self.beta * S * I / self.N
        dIdt = self.beta * S * I / self.N - self.gamma * I
        dRdt = self.gamma * I
        return [dSdt, dIdt, dRdt]
    
    def simulate(self, S0, I0, R0, t_max, dt=0.1):
        """Simulate the model."""
        t = np.arange(0, t_max, dt)
        initial_state = [S0, I0, R0]
        solution = odeint(self.derivatives, initial_state, t)
        return t, solution
    
    def final_size_equation(self, r_inf):
        """Final size equation to solve: r_inf = 1 - exp(-R0 * r_inf)."""
        return r_inf - (1 - np.exp(-self.R0 * r_inf))
    
    def final_epidemic_size(self):
        """Compute final epidemic size."""
        # Solve r_inf = 1 - exp(-R0 * r_inf)
        r_inf = fsolve(self.final_size_equation, 0.5)[0]
        return r_inf * self.N
    
    def herd_immunity_threshold(self):
        """Compute herd immunity threshold."""
        if self.R0 <= 1:
            return 0
        return 1 - 1 / self.R0
    
    def peak_infection_time(self, S0, I0, R0):
        """Estimate time of peak infection."""
        t, sol = self.simulate(S0, I0, R0, t_max=100)
        I = sol[:, 1]
        peak_idx = np.argmax(I)
        return t[peak_idx]

class SEIRModel(SIRModel):

    def __init__(self, beta: float, sigma: float, gamma: float, N: float):

        super().__init__(beta, gamma, N)
        self.sigma = sigma
    
    def derivatives(self, state, t):

        S, E, I, R = state
        dSdt = -self.beta * S * I / self.N
        dEdt = self.beta * S * I / self.N - self.sigma * E
        dIdt = self.sigma * E - self.gamma * I
        dRdt = self.gamma * I
        return [dSdt, dEdt, dIdt, dRdt]

Definitions

  • Linear Program (LP): Optimization problem with linear objective and constraints: \[\min c^T x \text{ subject to } Ax \leq b, \quad x \geq 0\]
  • Integer Program (IP): Linear program with additional constraint that some or all variables must be integers.
  • Mixed-Integer Program (MIP): Optimization with both continuous and integer variables.
  • Dual Problem: For primal LP above, the dual is: \[\max b^T y \text{ subject to } A^T y \leq c, \quad y \geq 0\]
  • Simplex Method: Algorithm that moves along edges of the feasible polytope to find optimal vertex.
  • Branch and Bound: Method for solving integer programs by systematically exploring the solution space and pruning suboptimal branches.
  • Cutting Plane: Constraint that removes part of the feasible region without excluding any integer solutions.

Theorem: Strong Duality in Linear Programming

Theorem: Consider the primal linear program: \[\min c^T x \text{ subject to } Ax \geq b, \quad x \geq 0\] and its dual: \[\max b^T y \text{ subject to } A^T y \leq c, \quad y \geq 0\]

If either problem has a finite optimal solution, then so does the other, and the optimal objective values are equal:

\[\min_{\text{primal}} c^T x = \max_{\text{dual}} b^T y\]

Furthermore, if \( x^* \) is optimal for the primal and \( y^* \) is optimal for the dual, then complementary slackness holds: \[y_i^* \cdot (A_i x^* - b_i) = 0 \text{ for all } i\] \[(c_j - A_j^T y^*) \cdot x_j^* = 0 \text{ for all } j\]

Proof Sketch

The proof uses Farkas' Lemma, which states that exactly one of the following holds: (1) \( Ax \leq b \) has a solution \( x \geq 0 \), or (2) there exists \( y \geq 0 \) such that \( y^T A \geq 0 \) and \( y^T b < 0 \).

Apply Farkas' Lemma to the system \( Ax \geq b, x \geq 0, c^T x \leq z \). If this is infeasible for all \( z < z^* \) (the primal optimum), then by Farkas, there exists a certificate of infeasibility that can be interpreted as a dual feasible solution with objective \( z^* \).

Complementary slackness follows from the equality of primal and dual objectives at optimality: \[c^T x^* = b^T y^* = y^{*T} A x^*\] where the last equality uses \( Ax^* = b \) for active constraints.

Notation Guide

  • \( c \): Objective function coefficients
  • \( A \): Constraint matrix
  • \( b \): Right-hand side vector
  • \( x \): Decision variables
  • \( y \): Dual variables (shadow prices)
  • \( z^* \): Optimal objective value
  • \( B, N \): Basic and non-basic variable indices
  • \( A_B \): Basis matrix
  • \( x_B = A_B^{-1}b \): Basic feasible solution
  • \( \bar{c} = c - A^T y \): Reduced costs
  • \( z_{IP}, z_{LP} \): Integer and linear programming optimal values

import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import linprog
from typing import Tuple, Optional

class LinearProgram:
    """Linear programming solver wrapper."""
    
    def __init__(self, c: np.ndarray, A_ub: np.ndarray = None, 
                 b_ub: np.ndarray = None, A_eq: np.ndarray = None,
                 b_eq: np.ndarray = None, bounds: list = None):
        self.c = c
        self.A_ub = A_ub
        self.b_ub = b_ub
        self.A_eq = A_eq
        self.b_eq = b_eq
        self.bounds = bounds or [(0, None) for _ in range(len(c))]
        self.solution = None
    
    def solve(self, method: str = 'highs') -> dict:
        """Solve the linear program."""
        result = linprog(
            self.c, 
            A_ub=self.A_ub, 
            b_ub=self.b_ub,
            A_eq=self.A_eq,
            b_eq=self.b_eq,
            bounds=self.bounds,
            method=method
        )
        self.solution = result
        return {
            'success': result.success,
            'x': result.x,
            'fun': result.fun,
            'slack': result.slack if hasattr(result, 'slack') else None,
            'con': result.con if hasattr(result, 'con') else None
        }
    
    def get_dual_variables(self) -> np.ndarray:
        """Extract dual variables (shadow prices) from solution."""
        if self.solution is None:
            raise ValueError("Must solve LP first")
        # For scipy's linprog, dual variables are in 'ineqlin' and 'eqlin'
        dual = []
        if hasattr(self.solution, 'ineqlin'):
            dual.extend(self.solution.ineqlin.get('marginals', []))
        if hasattr(self.solution, 'eqlin'):
            dual.extend(self.solution.eqlin.get('marginals', []))
        return np.array(dual)

class TransportationProblem:
    """Transportation problem: minimize shipping costs."""
    
    def __init__(self, supply: np.ndarray, demand: np.ndarray, costs: np.ndarray):
        """
        Args:
            supply: Array of supply amounts at each source
            demand: Array of demand amounts at each destination
            costs: Matrix of shipping costs from source i to destination j
        """
        self.supply = supply
        self.demand = demand
        self.costs = costs
        self.n_sources = len(supply)
        self.n_dests = len(demand)
        
        # Check balance
        if not np.isclose(sum(supply), sum(demand)):
            raise ValueError("Total supply must equal total demand")
    
    def solve_northwest_corner(self) -> np.ndarray:
        """Find initial basic feasible solution using Northwest Corner rule."""
        solution = np.zeros((self.n_sources, self.n_dests))
        supply_remaining = self.supply.copy()
        demand_remaining = self.demand.copy()
        
        i, j = 0, 0
        while i < self.n_sources and j < self.n_dests:
            amount = min(supply_remaining[i], demand_remaining[j])
            solution[i, j] = amount
            supply_remaining[i] -= amount
            demand_remaining[j] -= amount
            
            if supply_remaining[i] == 0:
                i += 1
            if demand_remaining[j] == 0:
                j += 1
        
        return solution
    
    def calculate_cost(self, solution: np.ndarray) -> float:
        """Calculate total transportation cost."""
        return np.sum(self.costs * solution)

Data Assimilation: Merging Models and Observations

data-assimilation

Real-World Motivation

Data assimilation combines computational models with observational data to produce optimal estimates of the state of a system. Essential for numerical weather prediction, oceanography, and climate modeling, it enables forecasts that outperform either models or observations alone. The challenge lies in the mismatch between model states and observations, the presence of errors in both, and the often nonlinear dynamics of the systems being studied. Modern data assimilation handles millions of variables and observations, running on supercomputers to produce real-time analyses. The field draws on estimation theory, numerical analysis, and high-performance computing, with applications extending to neuroscience, robotics, and autonomous systems.

Definitions

  • State Vector \( x \): The complete description of the system at a given time.
  • Observation Vector \( y \): Measurements of the system, related to state by \( y = H(x) + \epsilon \) where \( H \) is the observation operator.
  • Background (Prior) \( x_b \): Model forecast before assimilating observations.
  • Analysis (Posterior) \( x_a \): Optimal estimate after combining background and observations.
  • Kalman Filter: Optimal recursive estimator for linear systems with Gaussian errors.
  • Extended Kalman Filter (EKF): Linearization of nonlinear systems for Kalman filtering.
  • Ensemble Kalman Filter (EnKF): Monte Carlo approach using an ensemble of state estimates.
  • 4D-Var: Variational assimilation over a time window, minimizing a cost function.

Theorem: The Kalman Filter Update

Theorem: Consider a linear system with state transition \( x_k = F x_{k-1} + w_k \) and observations \( y_k = H x_k + v_k \), where \( w_k \sim \mathcal{N}(0, Q) \) and \( v_k \sim \mathcal{N}(0, R) \) are independent process and observation noise.

Given prior estimate \( \hat{x}_{k|k-1} \) with covariance \( P_{k|k-1} \), the optimal (minimum variance) posterior estimate is:

\[\hat{x}_{k|k} = \hat{x}_{k|k-1} + K_k(y_k - H\hat{x}_{k|k-1})\]

where the Kalman gain \( K_k \) is:

\[K_k = P_{k|k-1}H^T(HP_{k|k-1}H^T + R)^{-1}\]

The posterior covariance is:

\[P_{k|k} = (I - K_k H)P_{k|k-1}\]

Proof Sketch

The proof minimizes the mean squared error of the estimate. The innovation \( \nu_k = y_k - H\hat{x}_{k|k-1} \) represents the new information from observations.

The Kalman gain is derived by requiring that the posterior estimate be the linear minimum mean square error (LMMSE) estimator. This is equivalent to requiring orthogonality between the estimation error and the observations (innovation).

Alternatively, view the problem as Bayesian inference with Gaussian priors and likelihoods. The posterior is Gaussian with mean and covariance given by the Kalman update equations. The Kalman gain optimally weights the background and observation based on their relative uncertainties.

Notation Guide

  • \( x \in \mathbb{R}^n \): State vector
  • \( y \in \mathbb{R}^m \): Observation vector
  • \( x_b, x_a \): Background and analysis states
  • \( B, P \): Background and analysis error covariances
  • \( R \): Observation error covariance
  • \( H \): Observation operator (linear or linearized)
  • \( K \): Kalman gain matrix
  • \( F \): State transition matrix
  • \( Q \): Process noise covariance
  • \( d = y - H(x_b) \): Innovation (observation minus background)
  • \( J(x) \): Cost function in variational assimilation

import numpy as np
from scipy.linalg import solve
from typing import Tuple, Callable

class KalmanFilter:
    """Standard Kalman filter for linear systems."""
    
    def __init__(self, F: np.ndarray, H: np.ndarray, Q: np.ndarray, R: np.ndarray):
        """
        Args:
            F: State transition matrix
            H: Observation matrix
            Q: Process noise covariance
            R: Observation noise covariance
        """
        self.F = F
        self.H = H
        self.Q = Q
        self.R = R
        self.n = F.shape[0]  # State dimension
        self.m = H.shape[0]  # Observation dimension
        
        # Initialize
        self.x = np.zeros(self.n)
        self.P = np.eye(self.n) * 100  # Large initial uncertainty
    
    def predict(self) -> Tuple[np.ndarray, np.ndarray]:
        """Prediction step (time update)."""
        # State prediction
        self.x = self.F @ self.x
        # Covariance prediction
        self.P = self.F @ self.P @ self.F.T + self.Q
        return self.x, self.P
    
    def update(self, y: np.ndarray) -> Tuple[np.ndarray, np.ndarray]:
        """Update step (measurement update)."""
        # Innovation
        innovation = y - self.H @ self.x
        # Innovation covariance
        S = self.H @ self.P @ self.H.T + self.R
        # Kalman gain
        K = self.P @ self.H.T @ np.linalg.inv(S)
        # State update
        self.x = self.x + K @ innovation
        # Covariance update (Joseph form for numerical stability)
        I_KH = np.eye(self.n) - K @ self.H
        self.P = I_KH @ self.P @ I_KH.T + K @ self.R @ K.T
        return self.x, self.P
    
    def filter(self, measurements: np.ndarray) -> Tuple[np.ndarray, np.ndarray]:
        """Run filter on sequence of measurements."""
        n_steps = len(measurements)
        states = np.zeros((n_steps, self.n))
        covariances = np.zeros((n_steps, self.n, self.n))
        
        for i, y in enumerate(measurements):
            self.predict()
            x, P = self.update(y)
            states[i] = x
            covariances[i] = P
        
        return states, covariances

class EnsembleKalmanFilter:
    """Ensemble Kalman filter for nonlinear systems."""
    
    def __init__(self, state_dim: int, obs_dim: int, n_ensemble: int = 50):
        self.n = state_dim
        self.m = obs_dim
        self.N = n_ensemble
        self.ensemble = None
    
    def initialize(self, x0: np.ndarray, P0: np.ndarray):
        """Initialize ensemble from Gaussian distribution."""
        self.ensemble = np.random.multivariate_normal(x0, P0, self.N).T
    
    def ensemble_mean(self) -> np.ndarray:
        return np.mean(self.ensemble, axis=1)
    
    def ensemble_covariance(self) -> np.ndarray:
        x_mean = self.ensemble_mean()
        anomalies = self.ensemble - x_mean[:, np.newaxis]
        return (anomalies @ anomalies.T) / (self.N - 1)
    
    def predict(self, model: Callable, Q: np.ndarray):
        """Propagate ensemble through nonlinear model."""
        for i in range(self.N):
            self.ensemble[:, i] = model(self.ensemble[:, i])
            # Add process noise
            self.ensemble[:, i] += np.random.multivariate_normal(np.zeros(self.n), Q)
    
    def update(self, y: np.ndarray, obs_operator: Callable, R: np.ndarray):
        """Update ensemble with observations."""
        # Ensemble mean
        x_mean = self.ensemble_mean()
        
        # Predicted observations
        Y = np.zeros((self.m, self.N))
        for i in range(self.N):
            Y[:, i] = obs_operator(self.ensemble[:, i])
        y_mean = np.mean(Y, axis=1)
        
        # Covariances
        X_prime = self.ensemble - x_mean[:, np.newaxis]
        Y_prime = Y - y_mean[:, np.newaxis]
        
        P_xy = (X_prime @ Y_prime.T) / (self.N - 1)
        P_yy = (Y_prime @ Y_prime.T) / (self.N - 1)
        
        # Kalman gain
        K = P_xy @ np.linalg.inv(P_yy + R)
        
        # Update ensemble
        for i in range(self.N):
            innovation = y + np.random.multivariate_normal(np.zeros(self.m), R) - Y[:, i]
            self.ensemble[:, i] = self.ensemble[:, i] + K @ innovation

Set-Theoretic Topology: Beyond Metric Spaces

set-theoretic-topology

Historical Context

Set-theoretic topology emerged in the early 20th century as mathematicians explored the foundations of continuity and convergence. While metric spaces suffice for most applications in analysis and geometry, many topological phenomena require the full generality of arbitrary topological spaces. The field addresses fundamental questions: What separation axioms are needed for standard theorems? When does compactness imply sequential compactness? How do cardinal invariants of topological spaces behave? The development of forcing and large cardinal axioms in set theory has revealed deep connections between set-theoretic assumptions and topological properties, showing that many natural questions are independent of ZFC.

Definitions

  • Topological Space: A set \( X \) with a collection \( \tau \) of open sets satisfying: \( \emptyset, X \in \tau \); arbitrary unions of open sets are open; finite intersections of open sets are open.
  • Separation Axioms:
    • \( T_0 \): For any two distinct points, at least one has a neighborhood not containing the other.
    • \( T_1 \): Points are closed.
    • \( T_2 \) (Hausdorff): Distinct points have disjoint neighborhoods.
    • \( T_3 \) (Regular): Points and closed sets can be separated by neighborhoods.
    • \( T_4 \) (Normal): Disjoint closed sets can be separated by neighborhoods.
  • Compactness: Every open cover has a finite subcover.
  • Countably Compact: Every countable open cover has a finite subcover.
  • Sequentially Compact: Every sequence has a convergent subsequence.
  • Lindelöf: Every open cover has a countable subcover.

Theorem: Urysohn's Lemma and the Tietze Extension Theorem

Theorem (Urysohn): A topological space \( X \) is normal (\( T_4 \)) if and only if for any two disjoint closed sets \( A, B \subseteq X \), there exists a continuous function \( f: X \to [0, 1] \) such that \( f|_A = 0 \) and \( f|_B = 1 \).

Theorem (Tietze Extension): Let \( X \) be normal and \( A \subseteq X \) closed. Any continuous function \( f: A \to \mathbb{R} \) extends to a continuous function \( F: X \to \mathbb{R} \). If \( f \) is bounded, \( F \) can be chosen with the same bounds.

Proof Sketch (Urysohn's Lemma)

The proof constructs the separating function using dyadic rationals. Let \( \{q_n\} \) enumerate the rationals in \( [0, 1] \) with \( q_0 = 0, q_1 = 1 \).

By normality, construct a family of open sets \( U_q \) for dyadic rationals \( q \) such that: \[A \subseteq U_0, \quad U_1 = X \setminus B, \quad \text{and } \overline{U_p} \subseteq U_q \text{ when } p < q\]

Define \( f(x) = \inf\{q : x \in U_q\} \). This function is continuous because the dyadic structure ensures the preimage of any open interval is open.

Notation Guide

  • \( (X, \tau) \): Topological space
  • \( \overline{A} \): Closure of set \( A \)
  • \( A^\circ \): Interior of set \( A \)
  • \( \partial A \): Boundary of set \( A \)
  • \( \chi(X) \): Character of space \( X \)
  • \( w(X) \): Weight of space \( X \) (minimum size of basis)
  • \( \pi w(X) \): \(\pi\)-weight of space \( X \)
  • \( d(X) \): Density of space \( X \)
  • \( C(X) \): Space of continuous real-valued functions on \( X \)
  • \( \beta X \): Stone-Čech compactification

@dataclass
class TopologicalSpace:
    """Simplified representation of a finite topological space."""
    points: Set[int]
    open_sets: List[Set[int]]
    
    def __post_init__(self):
        # Verify topology axioms
        assert set() in [set(o) for o in self.open_sets], "Empty set must be open"
        assert self.points in [set(o) for o in self.open_sets], "Whole space must be open"
    
    def is_open(self, subset: Set[int]) -> bool:
        return subset in [set(o) for o in self.open_sets]
    
    def closure(self, subset: Set[int]) -> Set[int]:
        """Closure is intersection of all closed sets containing subset."""
        closed_sets = [self.points - set(o) for o in self.open_sets]
        containing_closed = [c for c in closed_sets if subset <= c]
        if not containing_closed:
            return self.points
        result = self.points.copy()
        for c in containing_closed:
            result &= c
        return result
    
    def is_closed(self, subset: Set[int]) -> bool:
        return self.points - subset in [set(o) for o in self.open_sets]
    
    def is_t0(self) -> bool:
        """Kolmogorov separation: distinct points have distinct neighborhoods."""
        for x in self.points:
            for y in self.points - {x}:
                # Check if there's an open set containing x but not y, or vice versa
                has_sep = False
                for o in self.open_sets:
                    o_set = set(o)
                    if (x in o_set and y not in o_set) or (y in o_set and x not in o_set):
                        has_sep = True
                        break
                if not has_sep:
                    return False
        return True
    
    def is_t1(self) -> bool:
        """Points are closed."""
        return all(self.is_closed({x}) for x in self.points)
    
    def is_hausdorff(self) -> bool:
        """T2: distinct points have disjoint neighborhoods."""
        for x in self.points:
            for y in self.points - {x}:
                has_sep = False
                for o1 in self.open_sets:
                    for o2 in self.open_sets:
                        if x in o1 and y in o2 and set(o1) & set(o2) == set():
                            has_sep = True
                            break
                    if has_sep:
                        break
                if not has_sep:
                    return False
        return True
    
    def is_compact(self) -> bool:
        """Every open cover has finite subcover (for finite spaces, always true)."""
        return True  # Finite spaces are compact

Computability Theory: What Can Be Computed?

computability-theory

Historical Context

Computability theory, founded by Turing, Church, Gödel, and Kleene in the 1930s, investigates the fundamental limits of computation. The question "What is computable?" arose from Hilbert's Entscheidungsproblem (decision problem), asking whether there exists an algorithm to determine the truth of arbitrary mathematical statements. Turing's introduction of the abstract computing machine that bears his name provided a precise definition of algorithm and led to the discovery of undecidable problems—problems no algorithm can solve. The Church-Turing thesis posits that this formal notion captures all effectively computable functions. Modern computability theory explores the fine structure of the computable and non-computable, with applications to complexity theory, logic, and theoretical computer science.

Definitions

  • Turing Machine: A mathematical model of computation consisting of an infinite tape, a read-write head, a finite set of states, and a transition function.
  • Computable Function: A function \( f: \mathbb{N} \to \mathbb{N} \) for which there exists a Turing machine that, on input \( n \), halts with output \( f(n) \).
  • Decidable (Recursive) Set: A set \( A \subseteq \mathbb{N} \) for which the characteristic function is computable.
  • Recursively Enumerable (r.e.): A set \( A \) is r.e. if it is the domain of some partial computable function, equivalently if there exists a Turing machine that halts on exactly the elements of \( A \).
  • Halting Problem: The set \( K = \{e : \varphi_e(e) \downarrow\} \) of indices of Turing machines that halt on their own input. This set is r.e. but not decidable.
  • Many-One Reduction: \( A \leq_m B \) if there exists a computable function \( f \) such that \( x \in A \iff f(x) \in B \).
  • Turing Reduction: \( A \leq_T B \) if \( A \) is computable relative to an oracle for \( B \).

Theorem: Undecidability of the Halting Problem

Theorem (Turing, 1936): The halting problem \( K = \{e : \varphi_e(e) \downarrow\} \) is undecidable.

Proof: Suppose \( K \) were decidable. Then there exists a computable function \( H \) such that \( H(e) = 1 \) if \( \varphi_e(e) \downarrow \) and \( H(e) = 0 \) otherwise.

Define a new computable function: \[D(e) = \begin{cases} \uparrow & \text{if } H(e) = 1 \\ 0 & \text{if } H(e) = 0 \end{cases}\]

Let \( d \) be an index for \( D \). Then: \[D(d) \downarrow \iff H(d) = 0 \iff \varphi_d(d) \uparrow \iff D(d) \uparrow\]

This is a contradiction. Therefore, \( K \) is undecidable.

Proof Sketch (Rice's Theorem)

Rice's Theorem generalizes the undecidability of the halting problem: any non-trivial semantic property of programs is undecidable.

Theorem (Rice): Let \( \mathcal{P} \) be a property of partial computable functions. If there exist computable functions \( f, g \) such that \( \mathcal{P}(f) \) holds and \( \mathcal{P}(g) \) does not hold, then the set \( \{e : \mathcal{P}(\varphi_e)\} \) is undecidable.

The proof reduces the halting problem to deciding \( \mathcal{P} \). Given input \( e \), construct a program that simulates \( \varphi_e(e) \); if it halts, output \( f(x) \), otherwise diverge. This construction shows that deciding \( \mathcal{P} \) would decide the halting problem.

Notation Guide

  • \( \varphi_e \): Partial computable function with index \( e \)
  • \( \varphi_e(x) \downarrow \): Computation halts (converges)
  • \( \varphi_e(x) \uparrow \): Computation does not halt (diverges)
  • \( W_e = \text{dom}(\varphi_e) \): The e-th r.e. set
  • \( K \): The halting set
  • \( \emptyset' \): Jump of the empty set (equivalent to \( K \))
  • \( A \leq_m B \): Many-one reducibility
  • \( A \leq_T B \): Turing reducibility
  • \( A' \): Turing jump of \( A \)
  • \( 0, 0', 0'', \ldots \): The arithmetical hierarchy
  • \( \Sigma^0_n, \Pi^0_n, \Delta^0_n \): Arithmetical hierarchy classes

class TMState(Enum):
    """Turing machine states."""
    RUNNING = "running"
    HALTED = "halted"
    ERROR = "error"

@dataclass
class TuringMachine:
    """
    Simplified Turing machine simulation.
    For demonstration of computability concepts.
    """
    states: Set[str]
    alphabet: Set[str]
    transition: Dict[tuple, tuple]  # (state, symbol) -> (new_state, new_symbol, direction)
    initial_state: str
    final_states: Set[str]
    blank: str = '_'
    
    def __post_init__(self):
        self.tape: Dict[int, str] = {}
        self.head = 0
        self.current_state = self.initial_state
        self.steps = 0
        self.max_steps = 1000  # Prevent infinite loops in simulation
    
    def read(self) -> str:
        return self.tape.get(self.head, self.blank)
    
    def write(self, symbol: str):
        if symbol != self.blank:
            self.tape[self.head] = symbol
        elif self.head in self.tape:
            del self.tape[self.head]
    
    def step(self) -> TMState:
        """Execute one step."""
        if self.current_state in self.final_states:
            return TMState.HALTED
        
        if self.steps >= self.max_steps:
            return TMState.ERROR  # Treat as non-halting for practical purposes
        
        symbol = self.read()
        key = (self.current_state, symbol)
        
        if key not in self.transition:
            return TMState.ERROR  # No transition defined
        
        new_state, new_symbol, direction = self.transition[key]
        self.write(new_symbol)
        self.current_state = new_state
        
        if direction == 'R':
            self.head += 1
        elif direction == 'L':
            self.head -= 1
        
        self.steps += 1
        return TMState.RUNNING
    
    def run(self, input_str: str = "", max_steps: int = 1000) -> tuple:
        """Run the machine on input."""
        self.max_steps = max_steps
        # Initialize tape with input
        for i, char in enumerate(input_str):
            if char in self.alphabet:
                self.tape[i] = char
        
        while True:
            state = self.step()
            if state in [TMState.HALTED, TMState.ERROR]:
                break
        
        # Extract output (tape contents)
        output = ''.join(self.tape.get(i, self.blank) 
                        for i in range(min(self.tape.keys()), 
                                      max(self.tape.keys()) + 1))
        return state, output, self.steps

Algebraic Statistics: Geometry of Statistical Models

algebraic-statistics

Historical Context

Algebraic statistics emerged at the turn of the 21st century from the recognition that many statistical models can be described by polynomial equations. This insight connects statistics to computational algebraic geometry, commutative algebra, and combinatorics. The field addresses questions about model identifiability, maximum likelihood estimation, and hypothesis testing using tools like Gröbner bases, toric varieties, and semidefinite programming. Applications include phylogenetics, where evolutionary models are algebraic varieties; causal inference, where graphical models have polynomial constraints; and computational biology, where gene regulatory networks are studied through their algebraic structure.

Definitions

  • Algebraic Statistical Model: A family of probability distributions where the probabilities are polynomial (or rational) functions of parameters.
  • Parametric Model: A model of the form \( \mathcal{M} = \{p(\theta) : \theta \in \Theta\} \) where \( p(\theta) \) is a vector of probabilities parameterized by \( \theta \).
  • Implicit Model: The variety \( V \) defined by polynomial equations satisfied by all distributions in \( \mathcal{M} \).
  • Toric Model: A model where the probability vector lies in the image of a monomial map; the implicit ideal is toric.
  • Maximum Likelihood Degree (ML degree): The number of complex critical points of the likelihood function for generic data.
  • Model Invariants: Polynomial relations that vanish on all distributions in the model.
  • Identifiability: A model is identifiable if the map from parameters to distributions is one-to-one.

Theorem: Birch's Theorem for Toric Models

Theorem (Birch): Consider a toric model \( \mathcal{M}_A \) specified by an integer matrix \( A \). For observed data \( u \) with sufficient statistics \( t = Au \), the maximum likelihood estimate \( \hat{p} \) is the unique point in \( \mathcal{M}_A \) satisfying:

  1. \( \hat{p} \in \mathcal{M}_A \) (lies in the model)
  2. \( A\hat{p} = \frac{t}{|u|} \) (matches sufficient statistics)

Equivalently, the MLE is the solution to the system of equations: \[p \in \mathcal{M}_A \quad \text{and} \quad \frac{p_i}{\sum_j p_j} = \frac{u_i}{|u|} \text{ whenever } (Au)_i = t_i\]

Proof Sketch

The log-likelihood function for multinomial data is: \[\ell(p) = \sum_i u_i \log p_i\]

For a toric model parameterized by \( p_i = \frac{1}{Z(\theta)} \theta^{a_i} \) where \( a_i \) are columns of \( A \), the likelihood equations become: \[\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \theta_j} = \sum_i u_i \frac{a_{ij}}{\theta_j} - |u| \frac{\partial \log Z}{\partial \theta_j} = 0\]

Using the structure of toric models, these simplify to the condition that the model expectations of the sufficient statistics equal the observed sufficient statistics. The uniqueness follows from the convexity of the log-likelihood function on the convex polytope of the model.

Notation Guide

  • \( \Delta_{n-1} \): Probability simplex in \( \mathbb{R}^n \)
  • \( p = (p_1, \ldots, p_n) \): Probability vector
  • \( u = (u_1, \ldots, u_n) \): Observed count data
  • \( A \): Design matrix (integer matrix defining toric model)
  • \( t = Au \): Sufficient statistics
  • \( \mathcal{M}_A \): Toric model associated to matrix \( A \)
  • \( I_{\mathcal{M}} \): Ideal of model invariants
  • \( \hat{p} \): Maximum likelihood estimate
  • \( \text{ML-degree}(\mathcal{M}) \): Maximum likelihood degree
  • \( \chi^2 \): Chi-squared statistic for goodness-of-fit
  • \( G^2 \): Likelihood ratio statistic

import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import minimize
from scipy.special import factorial
from typing import List, Callable, Tuple

class ToricModel:
    """
    Toric statistical model defined by integer matrix A.
    Probabilities have form p_i ∝ θ^{a_i} where a_i are columns of A.
    """
    
    def __init__(self, A: np.ndarray):
        """
        Args:
            A: Integer matrix (k × n) defining the model
        """
        self.A = A
        self.k, self.n = A.shape  # k parameters, n outcomes
    
    def probability(self, theta: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
        """Compute probability vector for parameter θ."""
        # p_i ∝ prod_j θ_j^{A_{j,i}}
        log_p = self.A.T @ np.log(theta + 1e-10)
        p = np.exp(log_p - np.max(log_p))  # Numerical stability
        return p / np.sum(p)
    
    def sufficient_statistics(self, data: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
        """Compute sufficient statistics t = A·u."""
        return self.A @ data
    
    def log_likelihood(self, theta: np.ndarray, data: np.ndarray) -> float:
        """Log-likelihood function."""
        p = self.probability(theta)
        # Avoid log(0)
        p = np.clip(p, 1e-10, 1)
        return np.sum(data * np.log(p))
    
    def mle(self, data: np.ndarray, theta0: np.ndarray = None) -> Tuple[np.ndarray, float]:
        """Compute maximum likelihood estimate."""
        if theta0 is None:
            theta0 = np.ones(self.k)
        
        # Optimize log-likelihood
        def neg_ll(theta):
            return -self.log_likelihood(np.abs(theta), data)
        
        result = minimize(neg_ll, theta0, method='L-BFGS-B', 
                         bounds=[(0.01, None) for _ in range(self.k)])
        
        theta_mle = np.abs(result.x)
        p_mle = self.probability(theta_mle)
        return p_mle, -result.fun

class IndependenceModel:
    """
    Independence model for two categorical variables.
    p_{ij} = p_{i+} · p_{+j}
    """
    
    def __init__(self, n_rows: int, n_cols: int):
        self.n_rows = n_rows
        self.n_cols = n_cols
        self.n = n_rows * n_cols
    
    def flatten_index(self, i: int, j: int) -> int:
        return i * self.n_cols + j
    
    def mle(self, contingency_table: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
        """
        MLE for independence model has closed form:
        p̂_{ij} = (row sum) · (col sum) / (total)²
        """
        row_sums = np.sum(contingency_table, axis=1)
        col_sums = np.sum(contingency_table, axis=0)
        total = np.sum(contingency_table)
        
        p_mle = np.outer(row_sums, col_sums) / (total ** 2)
        return p_mle.flatten()
    
    def pearson_chi2(self, observed: np.ndarray, expected: np.ndarray) -> float:
        """Pearson chi-squared statistic."""
        mask = expected > 0
        return np.sum((observed[mask] - expected[mask])**2 / expected[mask])
    
    def likelihood_ratio(self, observed: np.ndarray, expected: np.ndarray) -> float:
        """Likelihood ratio statistic G²."""
        mask = observed > 0
        return 2 * np.sum(observed[mask] * np.log(observed[mask] / expected[mask]))

📜 Math History: Bernhard Riemann (1826–1866)

Bernhard Riemann was one of the most influential mathematicians of the 19th century, whose work transformed geometry, analysis, and number theory. Despite a short life—he died at 39—his contributions continue to shape mathematics today.

Born in Hanover, Germany, Riemann studied at the University of Göttingen under Carl Friedrich Gauss. His doctoral thesis (1851) introduced what we now call Riemann surfaces, providing the geometric framework for multi-valued complex functions. This work unified analysis and topology, showing that the domain of a complex function could be understood as a geometric space.

Riemann's 1854 habilitation lecture, "On the Hypotheses Which Lie at the Foundations of Geometry," revolutionized our understanding of space. Rejecting the Euclidean framework that had dominated for millennia, Riemann proposed that space could be curved and that its geometry was determined by a metric tensor. This Riemannian geometry became the mathematical language of Einstein's general relativity sixty years later.

In analysis, the Riemann integral provided the first rigorous definition of integration. Though later superseded by the Lebesgue integral, Riemann's approach remains the standard introduction to integration theory. His work on Fourier series, the Riemann-Lebesgue lemma, and the concept of the Riemann zeta function laid groundwork for modern analytic number theory.

The Riemann Hypothesis, proposed in 1859, remains one of the most famous unsolved problems in mathematics. It conjectures that all non-trivial zeros of the zeta function lie on the critical line with real part 1/2. This seemingly technical statement about complex analysis has profound implications for the distribution of prime numbers. The hypothesis is one of the seven Millennium Prize Problems, with a million-dollar reward for its solution.

Riemann's approach to mathematics was characterized by deep geometric intuition and the search for unifying principles. He saw connections where others saw only differences, transforming disparate areas of mathematics into a coherent whole. His legacy lives on in Riemannian geometry, complex analysis, algebraic geometry, and number theory—fields that continue to evolve from foundations he laid nearly two centuries ago.

🎯 Problem Corner

IMO Shortlist Problem

Problem: Let \( n \) be a positive integer. A sequence of integers \( a_0, a_1, \ldots, a_n \) is called good if it satisfies:

  1. \( a_0 = 0 \) and \( a_n = n \)
  2. For all \( i = 1, 2, \ldots, n \): \( a_i - a_{i-1} \in \{1, 2\} \)

Determine the number of good sequences.

Hint

Let \( f(n) \) be the number of good sequences ending at \( n \). Find a recurrence relation by considering the last step: either \( a_n - a_{n-1} = 1 \) or \( a_n - a_{n-1} = 2 \). What constraint does this place on \( a_{n-1} \)?

Solution

Let \( f(n) \) denote the number of good sequences from 0 to \( n \).

For a good sequence ending at \( n \), we have two cases for the last step:

  • If \( a_n - a_{n-1} = 1 \), then \( a_{n-1} = n-1 \). The number of ways to reach \( n-1 \) is \( f(n-1) \).
  • If \( a_n - a_{n-1} = 2 \), then \( a_{n-1} = n-2 \). The number of ways to reach \( n-2 \) is \( f(n-2) \).

Therefore, the recurrence is: \[f(n) = f(n-1) + f(n-2)\]

With initial conditions: \[f(0) = 1, \quad f(1) = 1\]

This is the Fibonacci sequence! Specifically, \( f(n) = F_{n+1} \) where \( F_1 = F_2 = 1 \).

Answer: The number of good sequences is \( F_{n+1} \), the \( (n+1) \)-th Fibonacci number.

Verification:
\( n = 1 \): Only sequence is \( (0, 1) \). \( F_2 = 1 \). ✓
\( n = 2 \): Sequences are \( (0, 1, 2) \) and \( (0, 2) \). \( F_3 = 2 \). ✓
\( n = 3 \): Sequences are \( (0, 1, 2, 3) \), \( (0, 1, 3) \), \( (0, 2, 3) \). \( F_4 = 3 \). ✓

📊 Visualization of the Day: The Lorenz Attractor

The Lorenz attractor is a set of chaotic solutions to the Lorenz system, a simplified mathematical model for atmospheric convection. Discovered by Edward Lorenz in 1963, it was one of the first examples of deterministic chaos—systems where small changes in initial conditions lead to vastly different outcomes.

The system is defined by three ordinary differential equations:

\[\begin{aligned} \frac{dx}{dt} &= \sigma(y - x) \\ \frac{dy}{dt} &= x(\rho - z) - y \\ \frac{dz}{dt} &= xy - \beta z \end{aligned}\]

With parameters \( \sigma = 10 \), \( \rho = 28 \), and \( \beta = 8/3 \), the system exhibits chaotic behavior, with trajectories forming the famous butterfly-shaped attractor.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy.integrate import odeint
from mpl_toolkits.mplot3d import Axes3D

def lorenz_system(state, t, sigma=10.0, rho=28.0, beta=8.0/3.0):
    """
    Lorenz system of differential equations.
    dx/dt = σ(y - x)
    dy/dt = x(ρ - z) - y
    dz/dt = xy - βz
    """
    x, y, z = state
    dxdt = sigma * (y - x)
    dydt = x * (rho - z) - y
    dzdt = x * y - beta * z
    return [dxdt, dydt, dzdt]

def generate_lorenz_attractor(initial_state=[1.0, 1.0, 1.0], 
                               t_max=50.0, dt=0.01):
    """Generate trajectory of the Lorenz attractor."""
    t = np.arange(0, t_max, dt)
    trajectory = odeint(lorenz_system, initial_state, t)
    return t, trajectory

def plot_lorenz_attractor(trajectory, title="Lorenz Attractor",

def demonstrate_sensitivity(initial1=[1.0, 1.0, 1.0],
                            initial2=[1.0, 1.0, 1.001],
