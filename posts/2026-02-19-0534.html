Riemannian Geometry: Curvature and the Shape of Space

riemannian-geometry

Motivation

How do we measure the curvature of space? Riemannian geometry, founded by Bernhard Riemann in his 1854 habilitation lecture, generalizes Euclidean geometry to curved spaces. It provides the mathematical language for Einstein's general relativity, where gravity is understood as the curvature of spacetime. Beyond physics, Riemannian geometry has profound applications in topology (the sphere theorem), group theory (Mostow rigidity), and even machine learning (manifold learning, natural gradient descent).

Definitions

  • Riemannian Metric: A smooth inner product \(g_p\) on each tangent space \(T_p M\) of a manifold \(M\), varying smoothly with \(p\). In local coordinates: \(g = g_{ij} dx^i \otimes dx^j\).
  • Levi-Civita Connection: The unique torsion-free connection \(\nabla\) compatible with the metric: \(\nabla g = 0\).
  • Riemann Curvature Tensor: \(R(X,Y)Z = \nabla_X \nabla_Y Z - \nabla_Y \nabla_X Z - \nabla_{[X,Y]} Z\), measuring the failure of parallel transport to commute.
  • Sectional Curvature: For a 2-plane \(\sigma \subset T_p M\) spanned by orthonormal \(X, Y\): \(K(\sigma) = \langle R(X,Y)Y, X \rangle\).
  • Ricci Curvature: \(\text{Ric}(X,Y) = \text{tr}(Z \mapsto R(Z,X)Y)\), the average curvature in all directions containing \(X\).
  • Scalar Curvature: \(R = \text{tr}_g(\text{Ric}) = g^{ij}\text{Ric}_{ij}\), the total curvature at a point.

Theorem: The Gauss-Bonnet Theorem

Theorem (Gauss-Bonnet): Let \(M\) be a compact, oriented 2-dimensional Riemannian manifold without boundary. Then:

\[\int_M K \, dA = 2\pi \chi(M)\]

where \(K\) is the Gaussian curvature and \(\chi(M) = 2 - 2g\) is the Euler characteristic (\(g\) = genus).

Theorem (Bonnet-Myers): If \(\text{Ric} \geq (n-1)k g\) for some \(k > 0\), then \(M\) is compact with diameter \(\leq \pi/\sqrt{k}\).

Theorem (Sphere Theorem): If \(M\) is simply connected and \(1/4 < K \leq 1\), then \(M\) is homeomorphic to \(S^n\).

Proof Sketch (Gauss-Bonnet)

The proof proceeds by triangulating \(M\) and applying the local Gauss-Bonnet formula to each triangle. The local formula states that for a geodesic triangle with angles \(\alpha, \beta, \gamma\): \(\int_{\triangle} K \, dA = \alpha + \beta + \gamma - \pi\). Summing over all triangles, the interior angles sum to \(\pi F\) (where \(F\) is the number of faces), and the angle defects sum to \(2\pi(V - E + F) = 2\pi\chi(M)\).

Notation Guide

  • \((M, g)\): Riemannian manifold with metric \(g\)
  • \(g_{ij}\): Metric tensor components
  • \(g^{ij}\): Inverse metric tensor
  • \(\Gamma^k_{ij}\): Christoffel symbols of the Levi-Civita connection
  • \(R^l_{ijk}\): Riemann curvature tensor components
  • \(R_{ij} = R^k_{ikj}\): Ricci curvature tensor
  • \(R = g^{ij}R_{ij}\): Scalar curvature
  • \(\text{Sec}(\sigma)\): Sectional curvature of plane \(\sigma\)

Proof Theory: The Mathematics of Mathematical Reasoning

proof-theory

Motivation

What makes a proof valid? Can we prove that certain statements are unprovable? Proof theory, founded by David Hilbert in the early 20th century as part of his program to establish the consistency of mathematics, studies proofs as mathematical objects in their own right. After Gödel's incompleteness theorems showed that Hilbert's original goal was impossible, proof theory evolved into a rich field with applications in computer science (type theory, programming language semantics), philosophy (foundations of mathematics), and automated theorem proving.

Definitions

  • Formal System: A language with rules for forming well-formed formulas, axioms, and inference rules for deriving theorems.
  • Natural Deduction: A proof system with introduction and elimination rules for each logical connective, mirroring mathematical reasoning.
  • Sequent: A statement of the form \(\Gamma \vdash \Delta\) meaning "from assumptions \(\Gamma\), we can derive conclusions \(\Delta\)".
  • Cut Elimination: The process of removing "lemmas" (intermediate results) from proofs, showing that any provable statement has a direct proof.
  • Proof Ordinal: A countable ordinal measuring the strength of a formal system; larger ordinals mean stronger systems.
  • Curry-Howard Correspondence: The isomorphism between proofs and programs, where propositions correspond to types and proofs to programs.

Theorem: Gentzen's Cut Elimination Theorem

Theorem (Gentzen's Hauptsatz, 1934): In sequent calculus, any proof can be transformed into a cut-free proof of the same sequent. That is, the cut rule:

\[\frac{\Gamma \vdash \Delta, A \quad A, \Sigma \vdash \Pi}{\Gamma, \Sigma \vdash \Delta, \Pi}\]

is admissible: if the premises are provable without cut, so is the conclusion.

Corollary (Subformula Property): In a cut-free proof, every formula appearing in the proof is a subformula of the conclusion. This gives a decision procedure for propositional logic.

Theorem (Gentzen's Consistency Proof): Peano Arithmetic is consistent if transfinite induction up to \(\varepsilon_0\) is valid.

Proof Sketch (Cut Elimination)

The proof proceeds by induction on the complexity of the cut formula and the height of the proof tree. Key steps: (1) Push cuts upward until they reach axioms, (2) Eliminate cuts on atomic formulas directly, (3) For compound formulas, decompose the cut into cuts on smaller formulas (e.g., cut on \(A \land B\) becomes cuts on \(A\) and \(B\)).

Notation Guide

  • \(\Gamma, \Delta, \Sigma, \Pi\): Multisets of formulas (contexts)
  • \(\vdash\): Turnstile (derivability)
  • \(\Gamma \vdash A\): From assumptions \(\Gamma\), derive \(A\)
  • \(\land I\), \(\land E\): Conjunction introduction and elimination
  • \(\rightarrow I\), \(\rightarrow E\): Implication introduction (→E is modus ponens)
  • \(\forall I\), \(\forall E\): Universal quantifier rules
  • \(\varepsilon_0\): The proof-theoretic ordinal of Peano Arithmetic

Quantum Groups: Deformations of Symmetry

quantum-groups

Motivation

What happens when we "deform" the notion of a group? Quantum groups, introduced independently by Drinfeld and Jimbo in the 1980s, are deformations of universal enveloping algebras of Lie algebras and coordinate rings of algebraic groups. They emerged from studies of integrable systems in statistical mechanics (the Yang-Baxter equation) and have become fundamental objects in representation theory, knot theory (via the Jones polynomial), noncommutative geometry, and quantum field theory. Quantum groups provide the algebraic framework for quantum symmetries.

Definitions

  • Hopf Algebra: An algebra \(H\) with multiplication \(\mu\), unit \(\eta\), comultiplication \(\Delta: H \to H \otimes H\), counit \(\varepsilon: H \to k\), and antipode \(S: H \to H\) satisfying compatibility axioms.
  • Quantum Group \(U_q(\mathfrak{g})\): A deformation of the universal enveloping algebra of a Lie algebra \(\mathfrak{g}\), with generators \(E_i, F_i, K_i^{\pm 1}\) and \(q\)-deformed relations.
  • Yang-Baxter Equation: \(R_{12}R_{13}R_{23} = R_{23}R_{13}R_{12}\) for an \(R\)-matrix in \(\text{End}(V \otimes V)\), ensuring consistency of transfer matrices.
  • Quasitriangular Structure: An invertible \(R \in H \otimes H\) satisfying \(\Delta^{op}(h) = R\Delta(h)R^{-1}\) and relations with \(\Delta\).
  • \(q\)-Integer: \([n]_q = \frac{q^n - q^{-n}}{q - q^{-1}} = q^{n-1} + q^{n-3} + \cdots + q^{-(n-1)}\).

Theorem: The Quantum Yang-Baxter Equation

Theorem: Let \(U_q(\mathfrak{sl}_2)\) be the quantum group with generators \(E, F, K^{\pm 1}\) and relations:

\[KEK^{-1} = q^2 E, \quad KFK^{-1} = q^{-2} F, \quad [E, F] = \frac{K - K^{-1}}{q - q^{-1}}\]

Then the universal \(R\)-matrix satisfies the Yang-Baxter equation, and the category of finite-dimensional representations is a braided monoidal category.

Theorem (Drinfeld): For \(q\) not a root of unity, the representation theory of \(U_q(\mathfrak{g})\) parallels that of \(U(\mathfrak{g})\), with highest weight modules and Weyl character formula analogues.

Theorem (Kashiwara-Lusztig): There exists a canonical basis (crystal basis) with remarkable positivity and integrality properties.

Mean Field Games: Strategic Interactions in Large Populations

mean-field-games

Motivation

How do large populations of rational agents make strategic decisions? Mean field games (MFG), introduced by Lasry and Lions (2006) and independently by Caines, Huang, and Malhamé, study differential games with infinitely many players. Each player optimizes their objective while reacting to the aggregate behavior of the population. The "mean field" approximation simplifies the analysis: instead of tracking individual interactions, players respond to the statistical distribution of the population. MFG has applications in economics (market dynamics), finance (systemic risk), crowd dynamics, and energy systems (smart grids).

Definitions

  • Player State: Each player has state \(X_t \in \mathbb{R}^d\) evolving via SDE: \(dX_t = b(X_t, \alpha_t, m_t)dt + \sigma dW_t\).
  • Control: \(\alpha_t\) is the player's control/strategy.
  • Mean Field: \(m_t\) is the probability distribution of states across all players.
  • Cost Functional: \(J(\alpha) = \mathbb{E}\left[\int_0^T f(X_t, \alpha_t, m_t)dt + g(X_T, m_T)\right]\).
  • Value Function: \(u(x,t) = \inf_{\alpha} J(\alpha)\) given \(X_t = x\).
  • Nash Equilibrium: A strategy profile where no player can benefit by unilaterally changing strategy.

Theorem: The MFG System

Theorem (Lasry-Lions): A Nash equilibrium for the mean field game satisfies the coupled PDE system:

Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman (backward):

\[-\partial_t u - \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\Delta u + H(x, \nabla u, m) = 0, \quad u(x,T) = g(x, m_T)\]

Fokker-Planck (forward):

\[\partial_t m - \frac{\sigma^2}{2}\Delta m - \text{div}(m \cdot \partial_p H(x, \nabla u, m)) = 0, \quad m(x,0) = m_0(x)\]

where \(H(x,p,m) = \inf_{\alpha}\{b(x,\alpha,m) \cdot p + f(x,\alpha,m)\}\) is the Hamiltonian.

Theorem (Existence): Under standard assumptions (Lipschitz continuity, convexity), the MFG system has at least one solution.

Theorem (Uniqueness): If \(f\) and \(g\) satisfy a monotonicity condition (e.g., \((f(x,m) - f(x,m'))(m - m') \geq 0\)), the solution is unique.

Notation Guide

  • \(u(x,t)\): Value function (optimal cost-to-go)
  • \(m(x,t)\): Density of players at state \(x\), time \(t\)
  • \(H(x,p,m)\): Hamiltonian (optimized running cost)
  • \(\partial_p H\): Optimal control feedback
  • \(\mathcal{L}\): Infinitesimal generator of the diffusion
  • \(b(x,\alpha,m)\): Drift function
  • \(\sigma\): Diffusion coefficient

import numpy as np
from scipy.sparse import diags
from scipy.sparse.linalg import spsolve
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def solve_mfg_linear_quadratic(x_grid, t_grid, sigma, r, q, m0):
    """
    Solve a simple linear-quadratic MFG.
    
    Dynamics: dX = α dt + σ dW
    Cost: J = E[∫(α²/2 + q(X - m̄)²/2)dt + r(X_T - m̄_T)²/2]
    
    where m̄(t) = ∫ x·m(x,t)dx is the mean state.
    
    For this LQ case, the solution is Gaussian.
    """
    dx = x_grid[1] - x_grid[0]
    dt = t_grid[1] - t_grid[0]
    Nx, Nt = len(x_grid), len(t_grid)
    
    # Initialize
    m = np.zeros((Nx, Nt))
    u = np.zeros((Nx, Nt))
    
    # Initial density
    m[:, 0] = m0
    
    # Terminal condition for u
    mean_final = np.sum(x_grid * m[:, -1]) * dx
    u[:, -1] = 0.5 * r * (x_grid - mean_final)**2
    
    # Backward solve for u (simplified - explicit scheme)
    for n in range(Nt - 2, -1, -1):
        # Compute mean at current time
        mean_t = np.sum(x_grid * m[:, n]) * dx if n > 0 else mean_final
        
        # HJB: -∂u/∂t - (σ²/2)Δu + |∇u|²/2 + q(x - m̄)²/2 = 0
        u_xx = np.gradient(np.gradient(u[:, n+1], dx), dx)
        u_x = np.gradient(u[:, n+1], dx)
        
        running_cost = 0.5 * q * (x_grid - mean_t)**2
        hamiltonian = 0.5 * u_x**2
        
        u[:, n] = u[:, n+1] + dt * (-0.5 * sigma**2 * u_xx + hamiltonian + running_cost)
    
    # Forward solve for m
    for n in range(Nt - 1):
        # Optimal control: α* = -∇u
        u_x = np.gradient(u[:, n], dx)
        alpha = -u_x
        
        # Fokker-Planck: ∂m/∂t = (σ²/2)Δm - div(m·α)
        m_x = np.gradient(m[:, n], dx)
        m_xx = np.gradient(m_x, dx)
        div_m_alpha = np.gradient(m[:, n] * alpha, dx)
        
        m[:, n+1] = m[:, n] + dt * (0.5 * sigma**2 * m_xx - div_m_alpha)
        
        # Normalize
        m[:, n+1] = np.maximum(m[:, n+1], 0)
        m[:, n+1] /= np.sum(m[:, n+1]) * dx
    
    return u, m

# Setup

Topological Data Analysis: Finding Shape in Data

topological-data-analysis

Motivation

What is the shape of a dataset? Topological data analysis (TDA), developed by Gunnar Carlsson, Herbert Edelsbrunner, and others in the 2000s, applies algebraic topology to extract robust qualitative features from complex, high-dimensional data. Unlike traditional statistical methods that focus on means and variances, TDA captures connectivity, holes, and voids—features that persist across different scales. Applications include materials science (crystalline structure), neuroscience (brain connectivity networks), genomics (tumor classification), and machine learning (feature engineering).

Definitions

  • Point Cloud: A finite set of points \(P \subset \mathbb{R}^n\) sampled from some underlying space.
  • Vietoris-Rips Complex: For radius \(\epsilon\), \(VR_\epsilon(P)\) has a simplex for every finite subset of \(P\) with diameter \(\leq \epsilon\).
  • Persistent Homology: Tracks how homology groups change as \(\epsilon\) varies, identifying features that persist across scales.
  • Persistence Diagram: A multiset of points \((b, d)\) where \(b\) = birth scale, \(d\) = death scale of a topological feature.
  • Betti Numbers: \(\beta_k\) = number of \(k\)-dimensional holes (\(\beta_0\) = connected components, \(\beta_1\) = loops, \(\beta_2\) = voids).
  • Wasserstein Distance: Distance between persistence diagrams, enabling statistical analysis.

Theorem: Stability of Persistent Homology

Theorem (Cohen-Steiner, Edelsbrunner, Harer, 2007): Let \(f, g: X \to \mathbb{R}\) be tame functions on a triangulable space. The bottleneck distance between their persistence diagrams satisfies:

\[d_B(\text{Dgm}(f), \text{Dgm}(g)) \leq \|f - g\|_\infty\]

Theorem (Stability for Rips): For finite metric spaces \(X, Y\):

\[d_B(\text{Dgm}(VR(X)), \text{Dgm}(VR(Y))) \leq 2 \cdot d_{GH}(X, Y)\]

where \(d_{GH}\) is the Gromov-Hausdorff distance.

Corollary: Small perturbations of the data result in small perturbations of the persistence diagram—TDA is robust to noise.

def persistence_diagram_0d(points, epsilon_range):
    """
    Compute 0-dimensional persistence (connected components).
    Returns list of (birth, death) for each component.
    """
    n = len(points)
    dists = squareform(pdist(points))
    
    # Union-find structure
    parent = list(range(n))
    def find(x):
        if parent[x] != x:
            parent[x] = find(parent[x])
        return parent[x]
    def union(x, y):
        px, py = find(x), find(y)
        if px != py:
            parent[px] = py
    
    # Track births and deaths
    births = {i: 0 for i in range(n)}  # Each point born at ε = 0
    deaths = {}
    
    # Sort edges by length
    edges = []
    for i in range(n):
        for j in range(i+1, n):
            edges.append((dists[i,j], i, j))
    edges.sort()
    
    active_components = set(range(n))
    
    for eps, i, j in edges:
        pi, pj = find(i), find(j)
        if pi != pj:
            # Two components merge: one dies
            if pi in active_components:
                deaths[pi] = eps
                active_components.remove(pi)
            if pj in active_components:
                deaths[pj] = eps
                active_components.remove(pj)
            union(i, j)
            active_components.add(find(i))
    
    # Remaining components die at infinity
    diagram = []
    for i in range(n):
        if i in births:
            death = deaths.get(i, np.inf)
            diagram.append((births[i], death))
    
    return diagram

Arakelov Theory: Arithmetic Geometry at Infinity

arakelov-theory

Motivation

How do we do intersection theory on arithmetic schemes, where the "infinite places" (archimedean valuations) have no direct geometric interpretation? Arakelov theory, developed by Suren Arakelov in the 1970s and extended by Faltings, Gillet, Soulé, and others, provides a framework for extending algebraic geometry over \(\mathbb{Z}\) to include the infinite places. By adding Hermitian metrics to vector bundles at infinity, Arakelov theory enables intersection theory, Riemann-Roch theorems, and a theory of heights that unifies number theory and complex geometry. It has been crucial in proving results like Faltings' theorem on rational points.

Definitions

  • Arithmetic Surface: A regular scheme \(X\) flat and projective over \(\text{Spec}(\mathbb{Z})\) of relative dimension 1.
  • Arakelov Divisor: A formal sum \(D = D_{\text{fin}} + D_{\infty}\) where \(D_{\text{fin}}\) is a Weil divisor and \(D_{\infty} = \sum_\sigma \alpha_\sigma \cdot \sigma\) is a real linear combination of infinite places.
  • Metrized Line Bundle: A line bundle \(\mathcal{L}\) on \(X\) with Hermitian metrics \(\|\cdot\|_\sigma\) on \(\mathcal{L} \otimes_\sigma \mathbb{C}\) for each embedding \(\sigma: K \to \mathbb{C}\).
  • Arakelov Intersection: For divisors \(D, E\): \((D \cdot E) = (D_{\text{fin}} \cdot E_{\text{fin}})_{\text{fin}} + (D \cdot E)_\infty\) where the finite part is scheme-theoretic and the infinite part involves Green's functions.
  • Arakelov Height: \(h_{\overline{\mathcal{L}}}(P) = \frac{1}{[K:\mathbb{Q}]} \deg(\phi_P^* \overline{\mathcal{L}})\) for a point \(P\) with Zariski closure meeting \(D\) properly.

Theorem: The Arakelov Adjunction Formula

Theorem (Arakelov, 1974): Let \(X\) be an arithmetic surface and \(\omega_{X/\mathbb{Z}}\) the relative dualizing sheaf with Arakelov metric. For a section \(P: \text{Spec}(\mathbb{Z}) \to X\):

\[(P \cdot P) + (P \cdot \omega_{X/\mathbb{Z}}) = \sum_\sigma \log\|d\pi\|_\sigma\]

where the sum is over archimedean places.

Theorem (Faltings, 1984): For a semistable arithmetic surface of genus \(g \geq 2\):

\[(\omega_{X/\mathbb{Z}} \cdot \omega_{X/\mathbb{Z}}) \geq 0\]

with equality if and only if the generic fiber is smooth and the surface is isotrivial.

Theorem (Arithmetic Riemann-Roch): For a metrized line bundle \(\overline{\mathcal{L}}\):

\[\widehat{\deg}(R\Gamma(X, \overline{\mathcal{L}})) = \frac{1}{2}(\overline{\mathcal{L}} \cdot \overline{\mathcal{L}} - \overline{\mathcal{L}} \cdot \overline{\omega}) + \widehat{\chi}(\mathcal{O}_X)\]

def green_function_unit_disc(z, w):
    """
    Green's function on the unit disc:
    g(z,w) = -log|z-w| + boundary terms
    
    For the hyperbolic metric on the upper half-plane,
    the Green's function involves log|z - w̄| - log|z - w|.
    """
    if abs(z - w) < 1e-10:
        return float('inf')
    # Simplified Green's function
    return -np.log(abs(z - w))

def arakelov_degree(divisor_finite, metrics_infinite):
    """
    Compute the Arakelov degree of a metrized line bundle.
    
    deg(L̄) = deg(L) + Σ_σ (-log ||s||_σ)
    
    where s is a rational section.
    """
    finite_degree = sum(divisor_finite.values())
    infinite_contribution = sum(-np.log(m) for m in metrics_infinite)
    return finite_degree + infinite_contribution

def intersection_number(divisor1_finite, divisor2_finite, 
                        green_function_values):
    """
    Compute Arakelov intersection number.
    
    (D₁ · D₂) = (D₁,fin · D₂,fin)_fin + Σ_{P,Q} n_P m_Q g_σ(P,Q)
    
    where g_σ is the Green's function at archimedean place σ.
    """
    # Finite part (scheme-theoretic intersection)
    finite_intersection = 0
    for p1, n1 in divisor1_finite.items():
        for p2, n2 in divisor2_finite.items():
            if p1 == p2:
                # Local intersection multiplicity
                finite_intersection += n1 * n2
    
    # Infinite part (via Green's functions)
    infinite_intersection = sum(green_function_values)
    
    return finite_intersection + infinite_intersection

Perfectoid Spaces: The Revolution in p-adic Geometry

perfectoid-spaces

Historical Context

In 2011, Peter Scholze, then a graduate student, introduced perfectoid spaces—a new class of algebro-geometric objects that revolutionized p-adic geometry. By studying spaces that are "almost" perfect (in characteristic p), Scholze discovered a deep connection between characteristic 0 and characteristic p geometry: the tilting equivalence. This "perfectoid bridge" allowed mathematicians to transfer results from the well-understood world of perfect fields in characteristic p to the mysterious world of p-adic fields. The theory has led to breakthroughs in the Langlands program, p-adic Hodge theory, and the resolution of long-standing conjectures.

Definitions

  • Perfectoid Field: A complete non-archimedean field \(K\) of residue characteristic \(p\) such that the Frobenius is surjective on \(\mathcal{O}_K/p\) and \(|p| < 1\).
  • Tilt \(K^\flat\): For a perfectoid field \(K\), its tilt is \(K^\flat = \varprojlim_{x \mapsto x^p} K\), a perfect field of characteristic \(p\).
  • Perfectoid Algebra: A Banach \(K\)-algebra \(A\) such that the subalgebra of power-bounded elements is uniform and Frobenius is surjective modulo \(p\).
  • Perfectoid Space: An adic space locally of the form \(\text{Spa}(A, A^+)\) where \(A\) is perfectoid.
  • Tilting Equivalence: The equivalence of categories between perfectoid spaces over \(K\) and over \(K^\flat\).

Theorem: The Tilting Equivalence

Theorem (Scholze, 2011): Let \(K\) be a perfectoid field. There is an equivalence of categories:

\[\{\text{Perfectoid spaces over } K\} \cong \{\text{Perfectoid spaces over } K^\flat\}\]

given by the tilting functor \(X \mapsto X^\flat\). This equivalence preserves the underlying topological space and the étale topos.

Theorem (Almost Purity): For a perfectoid algebra \(A\) and a finite étale covering \(B/A\), \(B\) is also perfectoid and \(B^\circ/A^\circ\) is almost finite étale.

Theorem (p-adic Hodge Theory): For a proper smooth variety \(X\) over a p-adic field, there is a comparison isomorphism between étale cohomology and de Rham cohomology, realized via perfectoid spaces.

Geometric Group Theory: Groups as Geometric Objects

geometric-group-theory

Historical Context

Geometric group theory emerged in the late 1980s from the work of Mikhail Gromov, who realized that finitely generated groups could be studied as geometric objects via their Cayley graphs. This perspective shift transformed group theory: instead of studying groups purely algebraically, one examines their large-scale geometric properties. The field has grown to encompass hyperbolic groups, automatic groups, CAT(0) spaces, and the study of boundaries at infinity. It connects to topology (3-manifold groups), logic (decision problems), and dynamics (group actions).

Definitions

  • Cayley Graph: For a group \(G\) with generating set \(S\), the graph with vertices \(G\) and edges \((g, gs)\) for \(g \in G, s \in S\).
  • Word Metric: \(d_S(g, h) = \) length of shortest word in \(S \cup S^{-1}\) representing \(g^{-1}h\).
  • Quasi-Isometry: A map \(f: X \to Y\) such that \(\frac{1}{K}d_X(x,y) - C \leq d_Y(f(x),f(y)) \leq Kd_X(x,y) + C\) and every point of \(Y\) is within bounded distance of the image.
  • Hyperbolic Group: A group whose Cayley graph satisfies the thin triangle condition: any side of a geodesic triangle is contained in a bounded neighborhood of the other two sides.
  • Gromov Boundary: The set of equivalence classes of geodesic rays in a hyperbolic space, compactifying the space.
  • Ends: The connected components "at infinity" of a graph; a group has 0, 1, 2, or infinitely many ends.

Theorem: The Milnor-Švarc Lemma

Theorem (Milnor-Švarc): Let \(G\) act properly discontinuously and cocompactly by isometries on a proper geodesic metric space \(X\). Then \(G\) is finitely generated, and for any basepoint \(x_0 \in X\), the orbit map \(g \mapsto g \cdot x_0\) is a quasi-isometry between \(G\) (with any word metric) and \(X\).

Corollary: The word metric on a group is well-defined up to quasi-isometry. Properties invariant under quasi-isometry are intrinsic to the group.

Theorem (Gromov): A group is hyperbolic if and only if it has a linear isoperimetric inequality: area of filling a loop is bounded by length of the loop.

Theorem (Stallings): A finitely generated group has more than one end if and only if it splits as an amalgamated product or HNN extension over a finite subgroup.

from collections import deque, defaultdict
import numpy as np

class CayleyGraph:
    """
    Represents the Cayley graph of a finitely generated group.
    For computational purposes, we work with finite balls in the graph.
    """
    
    def __init__(self, group_elements, generators, multiplication):
        """
        group_elements: list of group elements
        generators: list of generators
        multiplication: function (g, h) -> gh
        """
        self.elements = group_elements
        self.generators = generators
        self.mult = multiplication
        self.element_to_idx = {g: i for i, g in enumerate(group_elements)}
        
    def word_metric(self, g, h):
        """
        Compute word distance using BFS in the Cayley graph.
        """
        if g == h:
            return 0
        
        target_idx = self.element_to_idx[h]
        start_idx = self.element_to_idx[g]
        
        visited = {start_idx: 0}
        queue = deque([start_idx])
        
        while queue:
            current = queue.popleft()
            current_dist = visited[current]
            current_elem = self.elements[current]
            
            for s in self.generators + [self.inv(gen) for gen in self.generators]:
                neighbor_elem = self.mult(current_elem, s)
                if neighbor_elem not in self.element_to_idx:
                    continue
                neighbor = self.element_to_idx[neighbor_elem]
                
                if neighbor == target_idx:
                    return current_dist + 1
                if neighbor not in visited:
                    visited[neighbor] = current_dist + 1
                    queue.append(neighbor)
        
        return float('inf')
    
    def inv(self, g):
        """Compute inverse (naive search)."""
        identity = self.elements[0]  # Assume first element is identity
        for h in self.elements:
            if self.mult(g, h) == identity:
                return h
        return None
    
    def growth_function(self, max_radius):
        """
        Count elements in balls of radius r.
        Returns list where result[r] = |B(e, r)|.
        """
        identity_idx = 0
        counts = [1]  # Ball of radius 0
        
        visited = {identity_idx}
        frontier = {identity_idx}
        
        for r in range(1, max_radius + 1):
            new_frontier = set()
            for idx in frontier:
                elem = self.elements[idx]
                for s in self.generators + [self.inv(g) for g in self.generators]:
                    neighbor = self.mult(elem, s)
                    if neighbor in self.element_to_idx:
                        nidx = self.element_to_idx[neighbor]
                        if nidx not in visited:
                            visited.add(nidx)
                            new_frontier.add(nidx)
            frontier = new_frontier
            counts.append(len(visited))
        
        return counts

Historical Context

The classification of finite simple groups, completed in 2004 after decades of work by hundreds of mathematicians, is one of the greatest achievements in mathematics. Among the 26 sporadic simple groups—exceptional groups that don't fit into infinite families—the Monster is the largest. With approximately \(8 \times 10^{53}\) elements, it was predicted by Fischer and Griess in 1973 and constructed by Griess in 1981 as the automorphism group of the Griess algebra. Its existence is connected to deep phenomena: Monstrous Moonshine (relating the Monster to modular functions), vertex operator algebras, and string theory.

Definitions

  • Simple Group: A group with no nontrivial normal subgroups; the building blocks of finite groups.
  • Sporadic Group: One of 26 exceptional simple groups not belonging to any infinite family.
  • The Monster \(\mathbb{M}\): The largest sporadic simple group, of order \(|\mathbb{M}| = 2^{46} \cdot 3^{20} \cdot 5^9 \cdot 7^6 \cdot 11^2 \cdot 13^3 \cdot 17 \cdot 19 \cdot 23 \cdot 29 \cdot 31 \cdot 41 \cdot 47 \cdot 59 \cdot 71\).
  • Griess Algebra: A 196884-dimensional commutative nonassociative algebra whose automorphism group is the Monster.
  • Monstrous Moonshine: The unexpected connection between the Monster's representation theory and the modular function \(j(\tau)\).
  • Fischer-Griess Algebra: The algebraic structure underlying the Monster's construction.

Theorem: Monstrous Moonshine

Theorem (Borcherds, 1992): The Monster group acts naturally on the Monster Lie algebra, a generalized Kac-Moody algebra. This explains the McKay observation: the dimensions of the smallest irreducible representations of the Monster:

\[1, 196883, 21296876, 842609326, \ldots\]

satisfy \(1 + 196883 = 196884\), which is the coefficient of \(q\) in the modular function:

\[j(\tau) = q^{-1} + 744 + 196884q + 21493760q^2 + \cdots\]

where \(q = e^{2\pi i \tau}\).

Theorem (Norton's Commuting Pairs): The graded character of the Monster module satisfies replication formulas connecting different elements.

Theorem (Conway-Norton): To each conjugacy class of the Monster, one can associate a genus-zero modular function (the "moonshine functions").

The Continuum Hypothesis: Independence from ZFC

set-theory

Historical Context

The continuum hypothesis (CH), first posed by Georg Cantor in 1878, asks whether there exists a set whose cardinality is strictly between that of the natural numbers and the real numbers. Cantor believed the answer was no—every infinite subset of \(\mathbb{R}\) is either countable or has the cardinality of the continuum. Despite tremendous effort, Cantor could not prove CH. In 1940, Gödel showed that CH is consistent with ZFC set theory. Then in 1963, Paul Cohen shocked the mathematical world by inventing the method of forcing and proving that the negation of CH is also consistent with ZFC. CH is therefore independent: neither provable nor refutable from the standard axioms of set theory.

Definitions

  • Cardinality: Two sets have the same cardinality if there exists a bijection between them.
  • \(\aleph_0\): The cardinality of the natural numbers; the smallest infinite cardinal.
  • \(\mathfrak{c} = 2^{\aleph_0}\): The cardinality of the continuum (real numbers).
  • Continuum Hypothesis (CH): There is no cardinal \(\kappa\) such that \(\aleph_0 < \kappa < \mathfrak{c}\); equivalently, \(\mathfrak{c} = \aleph_1\).
  • Generalized Continuum Hypothesis (GCH): For every infinite cardinal \(\kappa\), \(2^\kappa = \kappa^+\) (the successor cardinal).
  • Forcing: Cohen's method for constructing models of set theory where specific statements are true or false.
  • Inner Model: A transitive class containing all ordinals that satisfies ZFC; Gödel's constructible universe \(L\) is the canonical inner model.

Theorem: Independence of CH

Theorem (Gödel, 1940): If ZFC is consistent, then ZFC + CH is consistent. Specifically, the constructible universe \(L\) satisfies GCH.

Theorem (Cohen, 1963): If ZFC is consistent, then ZFC + \(\neg\)CH is consistent. There exist models of ZFC where \(2^{\aleph_0} = \aleph_2\), or \(2^{\aleph_0} = \aleph_{17}\), or any cardinal with uncountable cofinality.

Theorem (Easton, 1970): For regular cardinals, the continuum function \(\kappa \mapsto 2^\kappa\) is essentially arbitrary, subject only to monotonicity and König's theorem (\(\text{cf}(2^\kappa) > \kappa\)).

Theorem (Shelah): For singular cardinals of countable cofinality, there are restrictions (Shelah's pcf theory).

Proof Sketch (Cohen Forcing)

To add \(\aleph_2\) many real numbers to a model \(M\) of ZFC: (1) Define the forcing poset \(P\) of finite partial functions from \(\aleph_2 \times \omega\) to \(\{0,1\}\). (2) A generic filter \(G\) defines \(\aleph_2\) many characteristic functions, i.e., reals. (3) The extension \(M[G]\) satisfies ZFC and has at least \(\aleph_2\) many reals. (4) Cardinal collapse is prevented by the countable chain condition.

📜 Math History: The Development of Modern Algebra

The development of modern algebra represents one of the most profound transformations in mathematical thought, shifting from the study of specific numbers and equations to the investigation of abstract structures and their relationships. This revolution began in the early 19th century and continued through the 20th century, fundamentally changing how mathematicians understand symmetry, structure, and the nature of mathematical objects themselves.

The story begins with Évariste Galois (1811–1832), whose work on the solvability of polynomial equations laid the foundation for group theory. Galois discovered that the symmetries of a polynomial's roots—captured in what we now call the Galois group—determine whether the polynomial can be solved by radicals. His insight that structure matters more than calculation was revolutionary. Tragically, Galois died in a duel at age 20, but his manuscripts, published posthumously by Liouville in 1846, transformed mathematics.

Following Galois, Camille Jordan's 1870 "Traité des substitutions" systematized group theory, while Felix Klein's Erlangen Program (1872) proposed that geometry could be understood through the lens of transformation groups. This unified Euclidean, non-Euclidean, and projective geometries under a common algebraic framework.

The late 19th century saw the emergence of abstract algebra through the work of Richard Dedekind, who introduced ideals in ring theory, and Heinrich Weber, who axiomatized groups and fields. But the true abstraction came in the 20th century with Emmy Noether (1882–1935), whose work at Göttingen revolutionized algebra. Noether's 1921 paper "Idealtheorie in Ringbereichen" established the foundation of modern commutative algebra, introducing the ascending chain condition and showing that every ideal in a polynomial ring over a field has a finite basis.

Noether's influence extended through her students and collaborators. Her abstract approach—focusing on structures and homomorphisms rather than computations—became the standard methodology. Bartel van der Waerden's 1930 "Moderne Algebra," based on Noether's lectures and Emil Artin's work, became the definitive text, establishing the axiomatic approach that dominates algebra to this day.

The mid-20th century brought further abstraction with category theory (Eilenberg and Mac Lane, 1945), which provided a framework for studying mathematical structures across different domains. Homological algebra, developed by Cartan, Eilenberg, and later Grothendieck, created powerful tools for measuring the "holes" in algebraic structures.

Today, modern algebra permeates every area of mathematics and its applications. Group theory underlies crystallography and particle physics; ring theory is essential to algebraic geometry and number theory; field theory enables error-correcting codes and cryptography. The abstract viewpoint pioneered by Galois and perfected by Noether has proven to be one of mathematics' most powerful and enduring contributions to human knowledge.

IMO 2008 Problem 2

Problem: Let \(x, y, z\) be real numbers such that \(xyz = 1\). Prove that:

\[\frac{x^2}{(x-1)^2} + \frac{y^2}{(y-1)^2} + \frac{z^2}{(z-1)^2} \geq 1\]

Click for hint

Try the substitution \(x = \frac{a}{b}\), \(y = \frac{b}{c}\), \(z = \frac{c}{a}\) which automatically satisfies \(xyz = 1\). Then express each term in terms of \(a, b, c\).

Click for solution

Solution: With the substitution \(x = \frac{a}{b}\), \(y = \frac{b}{c}\), \(z = \frac{c}{a}\), we have:

\[\frac{x^2}{(x-1)^2} = \frac{a^2}{(a-b)^2}\]

So we need to prove:

\[\sum_{cyc} \frac{a^2}{(a-b)^2} \geq 1\]

Let \(u = \frac{a}{a-b}\), \(v = \frac{b}{b-c}\), \(w = \frac{c}{c-a}\). Then:

\[u + v + w = \frac{a}{a-b} + \frac{b}{b-c} + \frac{c}{c-a}\]

One can verify that \(u + v + w = uv + vw + wu\) (by clearing denominators). Therefore:

\[u^2 + v^2 + w^2 = (u+v+w)^2 - 2(uv+vw+wu) = (u+v+w)^2 - 2(u+v+w)\]

Let \(t = u + v + w\). Then \(u^2 + v^2 + w^2 = t^2 - 2t = (t-1)^2 - 1\).

We need to show \(u^2 + v^2 + w^2 \geq 1\), i.e., \((t-1)^2 - 1 \geq 1\), or \((t-1)^2 \geq 2\).

Actually, a cleaner approach: note that \((u-1)(v-1)(w-1) = uvw - (uv+vw+wu) + (u+v+w) - 1 = uvw - 1\).

Since \(uvw = \frac{abc}{(a-b)(b-c)(c-a)}\), and by symmetry arguments, one can show the minimum is achieved when two variables are equal, giving the bound of 1.

Equality holds when \((x,y,z) = (t, t, 1/t^2)\) where \(t\) satisfies \(2\frac{t^2}{(t-1)^2} + \frac{1/t^4}{(1/t^2-1)^2} = 1\).

🎨 Visualization of the Day: The Hopf Fibration

The Hopf fibration is one of the most beautiful structures in mathematics: a decomposition of the 3-sphere \(S^3\) into circles (fibers), each linked with every other. It was discovered by Heinz Hopf in 1931 and provides the first example of a nontrivial fiber bundle.

The fibration can be understood via the map \(h: S^3 \to S^2\) defined by \(h(z_1, z_2) = (2\text{Re}(z_1\bar{z}_2), 2\text{Im}(z_1\bar{z}_2), |z_1|^2 - |z_2|^2)\) where \((z_1, z_2) \in \mathbb{C}^2\) with \(|z_1|^2 + |z_2|^2 = 1\). Each point on \(S^2\) has a fiber that is a great circle in \(S^3\).

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from mpl_toolkits.mplot3d import Axes3D

def hopf_fibration(theta, phi, psi):
    """
    Parametrize S^3 using Hopf coordinates.
    (theta, phi, psi) -> (z1, z2) in C^2 with |z1|^2 + |z2|^2 = 1
    
    z1 = cos(theta) * e^(i*phi)
    z2 = sin(theta) * e^(i*psi)
    """
    z1_real = np.cos(theta) * np.cos(phi)
    z1_imag = np.cos(theta) * np.sin(phi)
    z2_real = np.sin(theta) * np.cos(psi)
    z2_imag = np.sin(theta) * np.sin(psi)
    
    return z1_real, z1_imag, z2_real, z2_imag

def hopf_map(z1_real, z1_imag, z2_real, z2_imag):
    """
    Hopf map h: S^3 -> S^2
    h(z1, z2) = (2*Re(z1*conj(z2)), 2*Im(z1*conj(z2)), |z1|^2 - |z2|^2)
    """
    # z1 * conj(z2) = (z1_real + i*z1_imag) * (z2_real - i*z2_imag)
    prod_real = z1_real * z2_real + z1_imag * z2_imag
    prod_imag = -z1_real * z2_imag + z1_imag * z2_real
    
    x = 2 * prod_real
    y = 2 * prod_imag
    z = (z1_real**2 + z1_imag**2) - (z2_real**2 + z2_imag**2)
    
    return x, y, z

def stereographic_projection(z1_real, z1_imag, z2_real, z2_imag):
    """
    Stereographic projection from S^3 (minus north pole) to R^3.
    Project from (0, 0, 0, 1).
    """
    denom = 1 - z2_imag
    # Avoid division by zero
    denom = np.where(np.abs(denom) < 1e-10, 1e-10, denom)
    
    x = z1_real / denom
    y = z1_imag / denom
    z = z2_real / denom
    
    return x, y, z

def get_fiber(phi0, n_points=100):
    """
    Get a single fiber of the Hopf fibration.
    The fiber over a point on S^2 is parameterized by varying psi
    while keeping the image point fixed.
    """
    theta = np.pi / 4  # Fixed for a particular fiber
    psi = np.linspace(0, 2*np.pi, n_points)
    
    # Vary phi and psi together to stay on the same fiber
    phi = phi0 + psi
    
    z1r, z1i, z2r, z2i = hopf_fibration(theta, phi, psi)
    x, y, z = stereographic_projection(z1r, z1i, z2r, z2i)
    
    return x, y, z

ax1 = fig.add_subplot(121, projection='3d')

colors = plt.cm.hsv(np.linspace(0, 1, 8))
for i, phi0 in enumerate(np.linspace(0, 2*np.pi, 8, endpoint=False)):
    x, y, z = get_fiber(phi0)
    ax1.plot(x, y, z, color=colors[i], alpha=0.7, linewidth=2)

ax1.set_title('Hopf Fibration: Linked Circles in S³')
ax1.set_xlabel('X')

ax1.set_zlabel('Z')
