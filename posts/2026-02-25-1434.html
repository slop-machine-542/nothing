1. Knot Theory: The Topology of Embedded Circles

Tags: knot-theory topology algebraic-topology

Motivation

Knot theory studies embeddings of circles in three-dimensional space, asking fundamental questions like: How can we tell if two knots are equivalent? Can a given knot be untangled? These questions, which seem simple on the surface, connect to deep areas of topology, algebra, and even quantum physics. The field has applications in DNA topology, molecular chemistry, and quantum computing.

Definitions

  • Knot: An embedding of the circle \( S^1 \) into \( \mathbb{R}^3 \) (or \( S^3 \)). Two knots are equivalent if one can be continuously deformed into the other without cutting or passing through itself.
  • Link: An embedding of multiple disjoint circles into \( \mathbb{R}^3 \).
  • Knot Invariant: A quantity or algebraic object assigned to a knot that remains unchanged under equivalence. Examples include the crossing number, Alexander polynomial, Jones polynomial, and HOMFLY polynomial.
  • Seifert Surface: An orientable surface whose boundary is a given knot. Every knot has a Seifert surface.
  • Genus of a Knot: The minimum genus of any Seifert surface for the knot.

Theorem: The Alexander Polynomial is a Knot Invariant

Theorem: For any knot \( K \), the Alexander polynomial \( \Delta_K(t) \in \mathbb{Z}[t, t^{-1}] \) is invariant under ambient isotopy. That is, if \( K_1 \) and \( K_2 \) are equivalent knots, then \( \Delta_{K_1}(t) = \Delta_{K_2}(t) \) up to multiplication by \( \pm t^k \).

Proof Sketch

The Alexander polynomial is defined using the infinite cyclic cover of the knot complement. Let \( X = S^3 \setminus K \) be the knot complement and \( \tilde{X} \to X \) be the infinite cyclic cover corresponding to the kernel of the abelianization map \( \pi_1(X) \to H_1(X) \cong \mathbb{Z} \).

The first homology \( H_1(\tilde{X}; \mathbb{Z}) \) is a module over \( \mathbb{Z}[t, t^{-1}] \) (the group ring of \( \mathbb{Z} \)), called the Alexander module. The Alexander polynomial is the greatest common divisor of the generators of the elementary ideal of this module.

Since the Alexander module is defined purely in terms of the topological space \( X \), which is invariant under ambient isotopy of \( K \), the Alexander polynomial is a knot invariant. The indeterminacy \( \pm t^k \) arises from choices of generator for \( H_1(X) \). ∎

Notation Guide

  • \( K \) — A knot
  • \( \Delta_K(t) \) — Alexander polynomial of knot \( K \)
  • \( V_K(t) \) — Jones polynomial of knot \( K \)
  • \( c(K) \) — Crossing number of knot \( K \)
  • \( g(K) \) — Genus of knot \( K \)
  • \( \pi_1(X) \) — Fundamental group of space \( X \)

2. p-adic Numbers: Analytic Number Theory Beyond the Reals

Tags: p-adic-numbers number-theory analysis

Motivation

The p-adic numbers, introduced by Kurt Hensel in 1897, provide an alternative way to complete the rational numbers. While the real numbers complete \(\mathbb{Q}\) with respect to the usual absolute value, the p-adic numbers complete \(\mathbb{Q}\) with respect to the p-adic absolute value. This completion reveals deep arithmetic structure and has become fundamental in modern number theory, particularly in the study of Diophantine equations and the Langlands program.

Definitions

  • p-adic Valuation: For a prime \( p \) and nonzero integer \( n \), the p-adic valuation \( v_p(n) \) is the highest power of \( p \) dividing \( n \). For \( n = \pm p^k m \) with \( p \nmid m \), we have \( v_p(n) = k \).
  • p-adic Absolute Value: For \( x \in \mathbb{Q} \), \(|x|_p = p^{-v_p(x)}\) if \( x \neq 0 \), and \(|0|_p = 0\).
  • p-adic Numbers \(\mathbb{Q}_p\): The completion of \(\mathbb{Q}\) with respect to \(|\cdot|_p\).
  • p-adic Integers \(\mathbb{Z}_p\): The closure of \(\mathbb{Z}\) in \(\mathbb{Q}_p\), equivalently \(\{x \in \mathbb{Q}_p : |x|_p \leq 1\}\).
  • Strong Triangle Inequality: \(|x + y|_p \leq \max(|x|_p, |y|_p)\), with equality when \(|x|_p \neq |y|_p\).

Theorem: Ostrowski's Theorem

Theorem (Ostrowski): Every nontrivial absolute value on \(\mathbb{Q}\) is equivalent to either:

  1. The usual absolute value \( |\cdot|_\infty \) (giving the real numbers)
  2. The p-adic absolute value \( |\cdot|_p \) for some prime \( p \) (giving the p-adic numbers)

That is, if \( |\cdot| \) is a nontrivial absolute value on \(\mathbb{Q}\), then there exists \( \alpha > 0 \) such that either \( |x| = |x|_\infty^\alpha \) for all \( x \), or \( |x| = |x|_p^\alpha \) for some prime \( p \).

Proof Sketch

Let \( |\cdot| \) be a nontrivial absolute value on \(\mathbb{Q}\). Consider the set \( S = \{n \in \mathbb{N} : |n| \leq 1\} \).

Case 1: If \( S = \mathbb{N} \), then \( |n| \leq 1 \) for all integers \( n \). Let \( p \) be the smallest positive integer with \( |p| < 1 \) (such a \( p \) exists since the absolute value is nontrivial). One shows that \( p \) must be prime and that \( |n| = 1 \) for all \( n \) not divisible by \( p \). It follows that \( |\cdot| \) is equivalent to \( |\cdot|_p \).

Case 2: If \( S \neq \mathbb{N} \), let \( n_0 \) be the smallest positive integer with \( |n_0| > 1 \). One shows that \( |n| = n^\alpha \) for some \( \alpha > 0 \), hence \( |\cdot| \) is equivalent to the usual absolute value. ∎

Notation Guide

  • \( v_p(x) \) — p-adic valuation of \( x \)
  • \(|x|_p = p^{-v_p(x)}\) — p-adic absolute value
  • \(\mathbb{Q}_p\) — Field of p-adic numbers
  • \(\mathbb{Z}_p\) — Ring of p-adic integers
  • \(\mathbb{Q}_p^\times\) — Multiplicative group of nonzero p-adic numbers
  • \(x = \sum_{n=N}^{\infty} a_n p^n\) — p-adic expansion (\(a_n \in \{0, 1, ..., p-1\}\))

from fractions import Fraction
import math

class PAdicNumber:
    """
    Representation of p-adic numbers with finite precision.
    Stores a p-adic number as its p-adic expansion.
    """
    
    def __init__(self, p, coefficients, valuation):
        """
        p: prime
        coefficients: list of digits [a_0, a_1, ..., a_n] where number is
                     sum(a_i * p^(valuation + i))
        valuation: p-adic valuation (lowest power of p with nonzero coefficient)
        """
        self.p = p
        self.coeffs = coefficients
        self.valuation = valuation
        self.precision = len(coefficients)
    
    @classmethod
    def from_rational(cls, p, frac, precision=20):
        """Convert a rational number to p-adic representation."""
        frac = Fraction(frac)
        numerator = frac.numerator
        denominator = frac.denominator
        
        # Find p-adic valuation
        val_num = 0
        temp = numerator
        while temp % p == 0 and temp != 0:
            temp //= p
            val_num += 1
        
        val_den = 0
        temp = denominator
        while temp % p == 0:
            temp //= p
            val_den += 1
        
        valuation = val_num - val_den
        
        # Compute p-adic expansion
        # We need to solve: numerator/denominator = sum(a_i * p^(valuation + i))
        # Multiply both sides by denominator
        coeffs = []
        
        # Working with p-adic integers first
        # x = numerator * denominator^{-1} in Z_p
        
        # Find modular inverse of denominator (mod p^precision)
        den_mod = denominator % (p ** precision)
        try:
            den_inv = pow(den_mod, -1, p ** precision)
        except ValueError:
            # denominator shares factor with p
            den_inv = pow(den_mod // (p ** val_den), -1, p ** precision)
        
        x = (numerator * den_inv) % (p ** precision)
        
        # Extract digits
        for i in range(precision):
            coeffs.append(x % p)
            x //= p
        
        return cls(p, coeffs, valuation)
    
    def __repr__(self):
        s = f"{self.p}-adic: "
        terms = []
        for i, a in enumerate(self.coeffs[:10]):  # Show first 10 terms
            if a != 0:
                power = self.valuation + i
                if power == 0:
                    terms.append(f"{a}")
                elif power == 1:
                    terms.append(f"{a}·{self.p}")
                else:
                    terms.append(f"{a}·{self.p}^{power}")
        if len(self.coeffs) > 10:
            terms.append("...")
        return s + " + ".join(terms) if terms else s + "0"
    
    def norm(self):
        """p-adic norm (absolute value)."""
        if all(c == 0 for c in self.coeffs):
            return 0
        return self.p ** (-self.valuation)
    
    def add(self, other):
        """Add two p-adic numbers (simplified)."""
        assert self.p == other.p
        # Simplified: assumes same valuation for now
        min_prec = min(self.precision, other.precision)
        new_coeffs = []
        carry = 0
        for i in range(min_prec):
            s = self.coeffs[i] + other.coeffs[i] + carry
            new_coeffs.append(s % self.p)
            carry = s // self.p
        return PAdicNumber(self.p, new_coeffs, self.valuation)

3. Ergodic Ramsey Theory: Recurrence and Patterns in Dynamics

Tags: ergodic-ramsey-theory ergodic-theory combinatorics

Motivation

Ergodic Ramsey theory emerged from Furstenberg's revolutionary proof of Szemerédi's theorem using ergodic theory. The central insight is that combinatorial patterns in sets of integers correspond to recurrence properties in dynamical systems. This connection has led to powerful new methods for proving Ramsey-type results and has deepened our understanding of the structure of measure-preserving systems.

Definitions

  • Measure-Preserving System: A quadruple \((X, \mathcal{B}, \mu, T)\) where \((X, \mathcal{B}, \mu)\) is a probability space and \(T: X \to X\) is a measurable transformation with \(\mu(T^{-1}(A)) = \mu(A)\) for all \(A \in \mathcal{B}\).
  • Ergodic System: A measure-preserving system where the only invariant sets have measure 0 or 1. Equivalently, if \(T^{-1}(A) = A\) then \(\mu(A) \in \{0, 1\}\).
  • Multiple Recurrence: A system has the multiple recurrence property if for any measurable set \(A\) with \(\mu(A) > 0\) and any integer \(k \geq 1\), there exists \(n \geq 1\) such that \(\mu(A \cap T^{-n}A \cap T^{-2n}A \cap \cdots \cap T^{-kn}A) > 0\).
  • Upper Density: For \(S \subseteq \mathbb{N}\), \(\bar{d}(S) = \limsup_{N \to \infty} \frac{|S \cap \{1, ..., N\}|}{N}\).

Theorem: Furstenberg's Multiple Recurrence Theorem

Theorem (Furstenberg, 1977): Let \((X, \mathcal{B}, \mu, T)\) be a measure-preserving system and let \(A \in \mathcal{B}\) with \(\mu(A) > 0\). Then for any integer \(k \geq 1\):

\[\liminf_{N \to \infty} \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=1}^{N} \mu(A \cap T^{-n}A \cap T^{-2n}A \cap \cdots \cap T^{-kn}A) > 0\]

In particular, there exist infinitely many \(n\) such that:

\[\mu(A \cap T^{-n}A \cap T^{-2n}A \cap \cdots \cap T^{-kn}A) > 0\]

Proof Sketch (for k=1, Poincaré Recurrence)

For the case \(k=1\) (Poincaré recurrence), consider the sequence of sets \(T^{-n}A\). Since \(T\) is measure-preserving, \(\mu(T^{-n}A) = \mu(A)\) for all \(n\).

If the sets \(T^{-n}A\) were essentially disjoint, we would have:

\[\mu\left(\bigcup_{n=0}^{N-1} T^{-n}A\right) = \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} \mu(T^{-n}A) = N \cdot \mu(A)\]

But \(\mu(X) = 1\), so for \(N > 1/\mu(A)\), this is impossible. Therefore, there exist \(m < n\) with \(\mu(T^{-m}A \cap T^{-n}A) > 0\).

Applying \(T^m\), we get \(\mu(A \cap T^{-(n-m)}A) > 0\), proving recurrence.

The full proof for general \(k\) requires the structure theory of measure-preserving systems and the introduction of characteristic factors. ∎

Notation Guide

  • \((X, \mathcal{B}, \mu, T)\) — Measure-preserving system
  • \(T^{-n}A\) — Preimage of \(A\) under \(n\)-fold composition
  • \(L^2(X, \mu)\) — Hilbert space of square-integrable functions
  • \(U_T f = f \circ T\) — Koopman operator
  • \(\mathbb{E}(f|\mathcal{F})\) — Conditional expectation

import numpy as np
from collections import Counter

class SymbolicDynamicalSystem:
    """
    A symbolic dynamical system on sequences.
    For demonstration of ergodic concepts.
    """
    
    def __init__(self, alphabet, shift_map=True):
        """
        alphabet: set of symbols
        shift_map: if True, use the shift transformation T(x)_n = x_{n+1}
        """
        self.alphabet = alphabet
        self.shift_map = shift_map
    
    def shift(self, sequence, n=1):
        """Apply the shift transformation n times."""
        return sequence[n:] + sequence[:n] if n > 0 else sequence
    
    def cylinder_measure(self, sequences, pattern, positions):
        """
        Estimate measure of cylinder set defined by pattern at positions.
        sequences: list of sequences (sample from the system)
        pattern: tuple of symbols
        positions: tuple of positions
        """
        count = 0
        for seq in sequences:
            if all(seq[pos % len(seq)] == pat for pos, pat in zip(positions, pattern)):
                count += 1
        return count / len(sequences)
    
    def check_recurrence(self, sequences, pattern, n, k):
        """
        Check multiple recurrence: pattern appears at positions
        i, i+n, i+2n, ..., i+kn for some i.
        """
        count = 0
        for seq in sequences:
            found = False
            for i in range(len(seq)):
                if all(seq[(i + j*n) % len(seq)] == pattern for j in range(k+1)):
                    found = True
                    break
            if found:
                count += 1
        return count / len(sequences)

class CircleRotation:
    """
    Circle rotation: X = R/Z, T(x) = x + α (mod 1)
    This is the prototypical example of a uniquely ergodic system.
    """
    
    def __init__(self, alpha):
        """
        alpha: rotation angle (irrational for ergodicity)
        """
        self.alpha = alpha % 1.0
    
    def iterate(self, x0, n):
        """Generate n iterates starting from x0."""
        return [(x0 + i * self.alpha) % 1.0 for i in range(n)]
    
    def is_ergodic(self, tolerance=1e-10):
        """
        Check if rotation is ergodic (alpha irrational).
        This is a numerical check.
        """
        # Check if alpha is rational by looking at continued fraction
        alpha = self.alpha
        for q in range(1, 1000):
            if abs(alpha * q - round(alpha * q)) < tolerance:
                return False
        return True
    
    def recurrence_time(self, x0, epsilon, max_iter=10000):
        """
        Find return time to epsilon-neighborhood of x0.
        """
        for n in range(1, max_iter):
            x_n = (x0 + n * self.alpha) % 1.0
            dist = min(abs(x_n - x0), 1 - abs(x_n - x0))
            if dist < epsilon:
                return n
        return None
    
    def multiple_recurrence(self, x0, k, epsilon, max_iter=10000):
        """
        Find n such that x0, T^n(x0), T^{2n}(x0), ..., T^{kn}(x0)
        are all within epsilon of each other.
        """
        for n in range(1, max_iter):
            points = [(x0 + j * n * self.alpha) % 1.0 for j in range(k+1)]
            # Check if all points are in same epsilon-interval
            for center in points:
                if all(min(abs(p - center), 1 - abs(p - center)) < epsilon 
                       for p in points):
                    return n
        return None

1. Information Theory: Quantifying Uncertainty and Communication

Tags: information-theory probability coding-theory

Real-World Motivation

Information theory, founded by Claude Shannon in 1948, provides the mathematical foundation for all modern digital communication. From text messages to video streaming, from DNA sequencing to deep space probes, information theory establishes the fundamental limits of data compression and reliable transmission. It quantifies information in bits, defines channel capacity, and guides the design of error-correcting codes that make our digital world possible.

Definitions

  • Entropy: For a discrete random variable \(X\) with distribution \(P(X = x_i) = p_i\): \[H(X) = -\sum_i p_i \log_2 p_i\] Entropy measures the average uncertainty or information content.
  • Joint Entropy: \(H(X, Y) = -\sum_{x,y} P(x,y) \log_2 P(x,y)\)
  • Conditional Entropy: \(H(Y|X) = \sum_x P(x) H(Y|X=x)\)
  • Mutual Information: \(I(X; Y) = H(X) - H(X|Y) = H(Y) - H(Y|X)\)
  • Channel Capacity: \(C = \max_{P_X} I(X; Y)\), the maximum mutual information between input and output.

Theorem: Shannon's Source Coding Theorem

Theorem (Noiseless Coding): Let \(X\) be a random variable with entropy \(H(X)\). For any \(\epsilon > 0\):

  1. There exists a code with average length \(L\) satisfying \(L < H(X) + \epsilon\).
  2. No uniquely decodable code can have average length \(L < H(X)\).

Thus, entropy is the fundamental limit of lossless compression.

Proof Sketch

Achievability (upper bound): Use Huffman coding or arithmetic coding. The key insight is that typical sequences (those with empirical frequencies close to the true distribution) occur with high probability, and there are approximately \(2^{nH(X)}\) such sequences of length \(n\). Assigning roughly equal-length codes to typical sequences achieves compression near the entropy rate.

Converse (lower bound): For any uniquely decodable code, Kraft's inequality gives \(\sum_i 2^{-l_i} \leq 1\) where \(l_i\) are codeword lengths. By convexity of the logarithm (Gibbs' inequality), the expected length satisfies:

\[L = \sum_i p_i l_i \geq -\sum_i p_i \log_2 p_i = H(X)\]

with equality when \(l_i = -\log_2 p_i\). ∎

from collections import Counter

class InformationTheory:
    """Information theory calculations and demonstrations."""
    
    @staticmethod
    def entropy(prob_dist):
        """
        Compute Shannon entropy H(X) = -sum(p * log2(p))
        prob_dist: array of probabilities (should sum to 1)
        """
        prob_dist = np.array(prob_dist)
        prob_dist = prob_dist[prob_dist > 0]  # Remove zeros
        return -np.sum(prob_dist * np.log2(prob_dist))
    
    @staticmethod
    def joint_entropy(joint_dist):
        """
        Compute joint entropy H(X,Y).
        joint_dist: 2D array of joint probabilities
        """
        flat = joint_dist.flatten()
        flat = flat[flat > 0]
        return -np.sum(flat * np.log2(flat))
    
    @staticmethod
    def conditional_entropy(joint_dist):
        """
        Compute H(Y|X) from joint distribution.
        """
        # H(Y|X) = sum_x P(x) * H(Y|X=x)
        marginal_x = np.sum(joint_dist, axis=1)
        cond_ent = 0
        for i, px in enumerate(marginal_x):
            if px > 0:
                cond_dist = joint_dist[i, :] / px
                cond_ent += px * InformationTheory.entropy(cond_dist)
        return cond_ent
    
    @staticmethod
    def mutual_information(joint_dist):
        """
        Compute I(X;Y) = H(X) + H(Y) - H(X,Y)
        """
        marginal_x = np.sum(joint_dist, axis=1)
        marginal_y = np.sum(joint_dist, axis=0)
        h_x = InformationTheory.entropy(marginal_x)
        h_y = InformationTheory.entropy(marginal_y)
        h_xy = InformationTheory.joint_entropy(joint_dist)
        return h_x + h_y - h_xy
    
    @staticmethod
    def huffman_code(prob_dist):
        """
        Generate Huffman code for given probability distribution.
        Returns dict mapping symbol index to code string.
        """
        # Build Huffman tree
        symbols = [(p, i) for i, p in enumerate(prob_dist) if p > 0]
        
        if len(symbols) == 1:
            return {symbols[0][1]: '0'}
        
        # Tree nodes: (probability, [symbol indices], code)
        nodes = [(p, [i], '') for p, i in symbols]
        
        codes = {}
        
        while len(nodes) > 1:
            nodes.sort(key=lambda x: x[0])
            p1, syms1, _ = nodes.pop(0)
            p2, syms2, _ = nodes.pop(0)
            
            # Assign codes
            for s in syms1:
                codes[s] = '0' + codes.get(s, '')
            for s in syms2:
                codes[s] = '1' + codes.get(s, '')
            
            nodes.append((p1 + p2, syms1 + syms2, ''))
        
        return codes

2. Compressed Sensing: Recovering Signals from Few Measurements

Tags: compressed-sensing optimization signal-processing

Real-World Motivation

Compressed sensing revolutionized signal acquisition by showing that sparse signals can be recovered from far fewer measurements than the Nyquist-Shannon theorem would suggest. This has profound implications for MRI imaging (reducing scan times from hours to minutes), single-pixel cameras, and sensor networks. The key insight is that if a signal is sparse in some basis, we can exploit this structure to reconstruct it from random linear measurements.

Definitions

  • Sparsity: A vector \(x \in \mathbb{R}^n\) is \(k\)-sparse if it has at most \(k\) nonzero entries.
  • Restricted Isometry Property (RIP): A matrix \(A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}\) satisfies RIP of order \(k\) with constant \(\delta_k\) if for all \(k\)-sparse vectors \(x\): \[(1 - \delta_k)\|x\|_2^2 \leq \|Ax\|_2^2 \leq (1 + \delta_k)\|x\|_2^2\]
  • Basis Pursuit: The convex optimization problem: \[\min_x \|x\|_1 \quad \text{subject to} \quad Ax = b\]
  • Mutual Coherence: \(\mu(A) = \max_{i \neq j} \frac{|\langle a_i, a_j \rangle|}{\|a_i\|_2 \|a_j\|_2}\)

Theorem: Exact Recovery via Basis Pursuit

Theorem (Candès-Tao, 2005): Let \(A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}\) satisfy RIP of order \(2k\) with \(\delta_{2k} < \sqrt{2} - 1\). Let \(x^* \in \mathbb{R}^n\) be \(k\)-sparse and let \(b = Ax^*\). Then the solution \(\hat{x}\) to basis pursuit satisfies \(\hat{x} = x^*\).

Moreover, if \(b = Ax^* + e\) where \(\|e\|_2 \leq \epsilon\), then:

\[\|\hat{x} - x^*\|_2 \leq C_0 \frac{\|x^* - x^*_k\|_1}{\sqrt{k}} + C_1 \epsilon\]

where \(x^*_k\) is the best \(k\)-term approximation of \(x^*\).

Proof Sketch

Let \(h = \hat{x} - x^*\) be the error. From the constraints, \(Ah = 0\). Let \(T_0\) be the support of \(x^*\) (\(|T_0| = k\)) and partition the complement into sets \(T_1, T_2, ...\) where \(T_1\) contains the \(k\) largest entries of \(h_{T_0^c}\), \(T_2\) the next \(k\) largest, etc.

From the optimality of \(\hat{x}\) and the triangle inequality for \(\ell_1\):

\[\|x^*\|_1 \geq \|\hat{x}\|_1 = \|x^* + h\|_1 \geq \|x^*_{T_0}\|_1 - \|h_{T_0}\|_1 + \|h_{T_0^c}\|_1\]

This implies \(\|h_{T_0^c}\|_1 \leq \|h_{T_0}\|_1\).

Using RIP and the cone constraint \(\|h_{T_0^c}\|_1 \leq \|h_{T_0}\|_1\), one shows \(\|h\|_2 \leq C \|Ah\|_2 = 0\), hence \(h = 0\). ∎

Notation Guide

  • \(x\) — Signal vector (often sparse)
  • \(A\) — Measurement matrix (\(m \times n\), typically \(m \ll n\))
  • \(b = Ax\) — Measurements
  • \(\|x\|_0\) — "\(\ell_0\) norm" (count of nonzero entries)
  • \(\|x\|_1 = \sum |x_i|\) — \(\ell_1\) norm
  • \(\|x\|_2 = \sqrt{\sum x_i^2}\) — \(\ell_2\) norm
  • \(\delta_k\) — RIP constant

import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import minimize, linprog
from scipy.fft import dct, idct

class CompressedSensing:
    """Compressed sensing reconstruction algorithms."""
    
    @staticmethod
    def generate_measurement_matrix(m, n, matrix_type='gaussian'):
        """
        Generate measurement matrix A of size m x n.
        m: number of measurements
        n: signal dimension
        """
        if matrix_type == 'gaussian':
            A = np.random.randn(m, n) / np.sqrt(m)
        elif matrix_type == 'bernoulli':
            A = np.random.choice([-1, 1], size=(m, n)) / np.sqrt(m)
        elif matrix_type == 'partial_fft':
            # Random rows of DFT matrix
            indices = np.random.choice(n, m, replace=False)
            A = np.fft.fft(np.eye(n))[indices, :] / np.sqrt(m)
        else:
            raise ValueError(f"Unknown matrix type: {matrix_type}")
        return A
    
    @staticmethod
    def basis_pursuit(A, b, method='scipy'):
        """
        Solve min ||x||_1 subject to Ax = b.
        """
        m, n = A.shape
        
        if method == 'scipy':
            # Convert to linear program
            # min c^T z subject to Gz <= h, Az = b
            # where z = [x^+, x^-] and x = x^+ - x^-, |x| = x^+ + x^-
            
            c = np.ones(2 * n)  # minimize sum of x^+ + x^-
            
            # Equality constraints: A(x^+ - x^-) = b
            A_eq = np.hstack([A, -A])
            b_eq = b
            
            # Bounds: x^+, x^- >= 0
            bounds = [(0, None) for _ in range(2 * n)]
            
            result = linprog(c, A_eq=A_eq, b_eq=b_eq, bounds=bounds, method='highs')
            
            if result.success:
                z = result.x
                x = z[:n] - z[n:]
                return x
            else:
                raise RuntimeError("Optimization failed")
        
        return None
    
    @staticmethod
    def basis_pursuit_denoising(A, b, lambda_reg):
        """
        Solve min ||Ax - b||_2^2 + lambda * ||x||_1
        (LASSO formulation)
        """
        m, n = A.shape
        
        def objective(x):
            residual = A @ x - b
            return np.sum(residual**2) + lambda_reg * np.sum(np.abs(x))
        
        result = minimize(objective, np.zeros(n), method='L-BFGS-B')
        return result.x
    
    @staticmethod
    def ista(A, b, lambda_reg, max_iter=1000, step_size=None):
        """
        Iterative Soft Thresholding Algorithm (ISTA).
        Solves min ||Ax - b||_2^2 + lambda * ||x||_1
        """
        m, n = A.shape
        if step_size is None:
            # Lipschitz constant of gradient is ||A^T A||
            step_size = 1.0 / np.linalg.norm(A.T @ A, 2)
        
        x = np.zeros(n)
        
        for _ in range(max_iter):
            # Gradient step
            gradient = 2 * A.T @ (A @ x - b)
            y = x - step_size * gradient
            
            # Soft thresholding
            x = np.sign(y) * np.maximum(np.abs(y) - lambda_reg * step_size / 2, 0)
        
        return x
    
    @staticmethod
    def create_sparse_signal(n, k, signal_type='random'):
        """
        Create a k-sparse signal of length n.
        """
        x = np.zeros(n)
        support = np.random.choice(n, k, replace=False)
        
        if signal_type == 'random':
            x[support] = np.random.randn(k)
        elif signal_type == 'constant':
            x[support] = 1
        elif signal_type == 'decaying':
            x[support] = np.exp(-np.arange(k) / 5)
        
        return x

3. Queueing Theory: Modeling Waiting Lines and Service Systems

Tags: queueing-theory stochastic-processes operations-research

Real-World Motivation

Queueing theory provides the mathematical framework for analyzing systems where customers arrive for service and may have to wait. From call centers and hospital emergency rooms to computer networks and manufacturing systems, queues are ubiquitous. Understanding queue behavior helps determine optimal staffing levels, buffer sizes, and service policies to balance customer satisfaction against operational costs.

Definitions

  • Kendall's Notation: A queueing system is denoted A/B/c/K/N/D where:
    • A: Arrival process (M=Poisson, D=Deterministic, G=General)
    • B: Service time distribution
    • c: Number of servers
    • K: System capacity (optional)
    • N: Population size (optional)
    • D: Service discipline (optional)
  • Traffic Intensity: \(\rho = \lambda / (c\mu)\) where \(\lambda\) is arrival rate and \(\mu\) is service rate. For stability, need \(\rho < 1\).
  • Little's Law: \(L = \lambda W\) where \(L\) is average number in system, \(\lambda\) is arrival rate, and \(W\) is average time in system.
  • Steady-State: The long-run behavior of the system, independent of initial conditions.

Theorem: Pollaczek-Khinchine Formula

Theorem (M/G/1 Queue): For an M/G/1 queue with arrival rate \(\lambda\), service rate \(\mu\), and service time variance \(\sigma^2\), the expected waiting time in the queue is:

\[W_q = \frac{\lambda(\sigma^2 + 1/\mu^2)}{2(1 - \rho)} = \frac{\lambda \mathbb{E}[S^2]}{2(1 - \rho)}\]

where \(\rho = \lambda/\mu\) and \(S\) is the service time random variable.

The expected number in the queue is \(L_q = \lambda W_q\) by Little's Law.

Proof Sketch

Consider the waiting time \(W_{n+1}\) of the \((n+1)\)-th customer. Let \(X_n\) be the number of customers found in the system by customer \(n\), and \(S_n\) be the service time of customer \(n\).

By the memoryless property of exponential interarrival times:

\[W_{n+1} = \max(0, W_n + S_n - I_n)\]

where \(I_n\) is the interarrival time between customers \(n\) and \(n+1\).

In steady state, taking expectations and using the fact that \(\mathbb{E}[W] = \mathbb{E}[W + S - I]\) for customers who wait, combined with the independence of arrivals and service, yields after algebraic manipulation:

\[W_q = \frac{\lambda \mathbb{E}[S^2]}{2(1 - \rho)}\]

The key insight is that waiting time depends on the second moment of service time, not just the mean. ∎

Notation Guide

  • \(\lambda\) — Arrival rate (customers per unit time)
  • \(\mu\) — Service rate (customers per unit time per server)
  • \(\rho = \lambda/(c\mu)\) — Traffic intensity
  • \(L\) — Average number in system
  • \(L_q\) — Average number in queue
  • \(W\) — Average time in system
  • \(W_q\) — Average waiting time in queue
  • \(P_n\) — Steady-state probability of \(n\) customers

import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import poisson, expon

class QueueingSystem:
    """Analysis and simulation of queueing systems."""
    
    @staticmethod
    def mm1_metrics(lam, mu):
        """
        Compute exact metrics for M/M/1 queue.
        lam: arrival rate
        mu: service rate
        """
        rho = lam / mu
        if rho >= 1:
            raise ValueError("System unstable: rho >= 1")
        
        metrics = {
            'rho': rho,
            'L': rho / (1 - rho),           # Average number in system
            'L_q': rho**2 / (1 - rho),      # Average number in queue
            'W': 1 / (mu - lam),            # Average time in system
            'W_q': rho / (mu - lam),        # Average waiting time in queue
            'P_0': 1 - rho,                  # Probability system is empty
            'P_busy': rho                    # Probability server is busy
        }
        return metrics
    
    @staticmethod
    def mm1_probability_n(n, rho):
        """Steady-state probability of n customers in M/M/1."""
        return (1 - rho) * rho**n
    
    @staticmethod
    def mmc_metrics(lam, mu, c):
        """
        Compute exact metrics for M/M/c queue.
        """
        rho = lam / (c * mu)
        if rho >= 1:
            raise ValueError("System unstable: rho >= 1")
        
        # Compute P_0 (probability of 0 customers)
        sum_term = sum((lam/mu)**n / np.math.factorial(n) for n in range(c))
        last_term = (lam/mu)**c / (np.math.factorial(c) * (1 - rho))
        P_0 = 1 / (sum_term + last_term)
        
        # Probability of waiting (Erlang C formula)
        P_wait = last_term * P_0
        
        # Average queue length
        L_q = P_wait * rho / (1 - rho)
        
        # Average number in system
        L = L_q + lam / mu
        
        # Average times
        W_q = L_q / lam
        W = W_q + 1/mu
        
        return {
            'rho': rho,
            'P_0': P_0,
            'P_wait': P_wait,
            'L': L,
            'L_q': L_q,
            'W': W,
            'W_q': W_q
        }
    
    @staticmethod
    def mg1_metrics(lam, mu, sigma_s):
        """
        Compute metrics for M/G/1 queue using Pollaczek-Khinchine.
        lam: arrival rate
        mu: service rate
        sigma_s: standard deviation of service time
        """
        rho = lam / mu
        if rho >= 1:
            raise ValueError("System unstable: rho >= 1")
        
        ES = 1 / mu
        ES2 = sigma_s**2 + ES**2
        
        W_q = lam * ES2 / (2 * (1 - rho))
        L_q = lam * W_q
        W = W_q + ES
        L = lam * W
        
        return {
            'rho': rho,
            'L': L,
            'L_q': L_q,
            'W': W,
            'W_q': W_q
        }
    
    @staticmethod
    def simulate_mm1(lam, mu, T, seed=None):
        """
        Simulate M/M/1 queue for time T.
        Returns sample paths and statistics.
        """
        if seed is not None:
            np.random.seed(seed)
        
        # Generate arrivals (Poisson process)
        arrivals = []
        t = 0
        while t < T:
            dt = np.random.exponential(1/lam)
            t += dt
            if t < T:
                arrivals.append(t)
        
        # Generate service times
        service_times = np.random.exponential(1/mu, len(arrivals))
        
        # Simulate queue
        departure_times = []
        queue_lengths = []
        queue_times = []
        
        server_free_at = 0
        for arrival, service in zip(arrivals, service_times):
            start_service = max(arrival, server_free_at)
            departure = start_service + service
            departure_times.append(departure)
            
            wait_time = start_service - arrival
            queue_times.append(wait_time)
            
            server_free_at = departure
        
        # Compute metrics from simulation
        system_times = [d - a for a, d in zip(arrivals, departure_times)]
        
        return {
            'arrivals': arrivals,
            'departures': departure_times,
            'avg_wait': np.mean(queue_times),
            'avg_system_time': np.mean(system_times),
            'avg_queue_length': np.sum(queue_times) / T
        }

Fermat's Last Theorem: A 358-Year Journey to Resolution

Tags: fermats-last-theorem algebraic-number-theory modular-forms

Historical Context

In 1637, Pierre de Fermat wrote in the margin of his copy of Diophantus's Arithmetica that he had discovered a "truly marvelous proof" that the equation \(a^n + b^n = c^n\) has no solutions in positive integers for \(n > 2\). He claimed the margin was too small to contain it. This simple statement became the most famous unsolved problem in mathematics, resisting proof for 358 years.

The theorem attracted the attention of countless mathematicians, including Euler (who proved the case \(n=3\)), Dirichlet and Legendre (\(n=5\)), Lamé and Cauchy (\(n=7\)), and Kummer (who developed ideal theory to handle many cases). The final proof came in 1994 from Andrew Wiles, building on the modularity conjecture and the work of Taniyama, Shimura, Weil, Frey, Serre, and Ribet.

Definitions

  • Fermat Equation: \(x^n + y^n = z^n\) for integers \(n \geq 3\).
  • Semistable Elliptic Curve: An elliptic curve with good or multiplicative reduction at all primes.
  • Modular Form: A holomorphic function on the upper half-plane satisfying specific transformation properties under the modular group.
  • Modularity Conjecture: Every rational elliptic curve is modular (arises from a modular form).

Theorem: Fermat's Last Theorem (Wiles, 1994)

Theorem: For any integer \(n > 2\), the equation \(a^n + b^n = c^n\) has no solutions in positive integers.

Proof Outline

The proof follows from the modularity theorem for semistable elliptic curves:

  1. Frey's Insight (1984): If \(a^p + b^p = c^p\) is a counterexample for prime \(p \geq 5\), then the elliptic curve \(y^2 = x(x - a^p)(x + b^p)\) (the Frey curve) is semistable but not modular.
  2. Ribet's Theorem (1986): If the modularity conjecture holds, then the Frey curve cannot exist. Thus, Fermat's Last Theorem follows from modularity.
  3. Wiles's Proof (1994): Wiles proved the modularity conjecture for semistable elliptic curves, completing the proof.

The key technical achievement was establishing a correspondence between Galois representations and modular forms via deformation theory. ∎

        """Add two points on the curve."""
        if P is None:
            return Q
        if Q is None:
            return P

        x2, y2 = Q

            return None  # Point at infinity

            # Point doubling

        y3 = m*(x1 - x3) - y1
        
        return (x3, y3)

Julia Sets: Fractal Boundaries of Complex Dynamics

Tags: julia-sets complex-dynamics fractals

Historical Context

Julia sets are named after French mathematician Gaston Julia, who studied their properties during World War I while hospitalized. Working independently, Pierre Fatou also discovered many of their fundamental properties. However, the true beauty of Julia sets remained hidden until the advent of computers in the 1980s, when Benoit Mandelbrot and others visualized these intricate fractal structures. Julia sets arise from iterating complex functions and exhibit self-similarity at all scales.

Definitions

  • Complex Dynamics: The study of iteration of holomorphic functions on the complex plane.
  • Filled Julia Set: For a polynomial \(f\), \(K(f) = \{z \in \mathbb{C} : f^n(z) \not\to \infty\}\), where \(f^n\) denotes \(n\)-fold iteration.
  • Julia Set: \(J(f) = \partial K(f)\), the boundary of the filled Julia set.
  • Fatou Set: The complement of the Julia set; points where the dynamics are stable.
  • Connected Julia Set: A Julia set that is connected as a topological space; occurs when the critical point does not escape to infinity.

Theorem: The Julia Set is Perfect and Nowhere Dense

Theorem: Let \(f\) be a polynomial of degree \(d \geq 2\). Then:

  1. The Julia set \(J(f)\) is nonempty, compact, and perfect (no isolated points).
  2. \(J(f)\) is completely invariant: \(f(J(f)) = J(f) = f^{-1}(J(f))\).
  3. The dynamics of \(f\) on \(J(f)\) are chaotic (sensitive dependence on initial conditions, topological transitivity, dense periodic points).

Proof Sketch

Non-empty: For polynomials of degree \(\geq 2\), there are always repelling periodic points, which lie in \(J(f)\).

Perfect: Repelling periodic points are dense in \(J(f)\) (Fatou's theorem). Since repelling periodic points accumulate on themselves, \(J(f)\) has no isolated points.

Invariance: If \(z \in J(f)\), then arbitrarily close to \(z\) there are points with different long-term behavior. Since \(f\) is continuous and open (as a non-constant holomorphic map), this property propagates to \(f(z)\) and preimages of \(z\).

Chaos: The density of periodic points gives topological transitivity. Sensitive dependence follows from the presence of expanding behavior near repelling periodic points. ∎

Notation Guide

  • \(f_c(z) = z^2 + c\) — Quadratic family
  • \(f^n(z)\) — \(n\)-fold composition of \(f\)
  • \(K_c\) — Filled Julia set for \(f_c\)
  • \(J_c\) — Julia set for \(f_c\)
  • \(M = \{c : J_c \text{ is connected}\}\) — Mandelbrot set
  • \(z_0 = 0\) — Critical point for quadratic family

import numpy as np

class JuliaSet:
    """Compute and analyze Julia sets for quadratic polynomials."""
    
    def __init__(self, c, max_iter=100, escape_radius=2.0):
        """
        c: complex parameter
        max_iter: maximum iterations
        escape_radius: escape radius
        """
        self.c = c
        self.max_iter = max_iter
        self.escape_radius = escape_radius
    
    def iterate(self, z, n):
        """Compute f^n(z) where f(z) = z^2 + c."""
        for _ in range(n):
            z = z**2 + self.c
            if abs(z) > self.escape_radius:
                break
        return z
    
    def escape_time(self, z0):
        """
        Compute escape time for initial point z0.
        Returns number of iterations until escape, or max_iter if bounded.
        """
        z = z0
        for i in range(self.max_iter):
            if abs(z) > self.escape_radius:
                return i
            z = z**2 + self.c
        return self.max_iter
    
    def compute_grid(self, xlim=(-2, 2), ylim=(-2, 2), resolution=800):
        """
        Compute escape times on a grid.
        """
        x = np.linspace(xlim[0], xlim[1], resolution)
        y = np.linspace(ylim[0], ylim[1], resolution)
        X, Y = np.meshgrid(x, y)
        Z = X + 1j*Y
        
        escape_times = np.zeros(Z.shape, dtype=int)
        
        for i in range(resolution):
            for j in range(resolution):
                escape_times[i, j] = self.escape_time(Z[i, j])
        
        return X, Y, escape_times
    
    def is_connected(self):
        """
        Check if Julia set is connected.
        For f_c(z) = z^2 + c, J_c is connected iff 0 does not escape.
        """
        return self.escape_time(0) == self.max_iter
    
    def periodic_points(self, period, tolerance=1e-6):
        """
        Find periodic points of given period numerically.
        Returns approximate locations.
        """
        # This is a simplified search on a grid
        points = []
        grid_size = 200
        x = np.linspace(-2, 2, grid_size)
        y = np.linspace(-2, 2, grid_size)
        
        for xi in x:
            for yi in y:
                z = xi + 1j*yi
                z_period = self.iterate(z, period)
                if abs(z_period - z) < tolerance:
                    points.append(z)
        
        return points
    
    def lyapunov_exponent(self, z0, n=1000):
        """
        Compute Lyapunov exponent at point z0.
        Positive exponent indicates chaos.
        """
        z = z0
        lyap_sum = 0
        
        for _ in range(n):
            if abs(z) > self.escape_radius:
                return float('inf')
            # Derivative of f(z) = z^2 + c is 2z
            lyap_sum += np.log(2 * abs(z))
            z = z**2 + self.c
        
        return lyap_sum / n

def mandelbrot_membership(c, max_iter=100):
    """Check if c is in the Mandelbrot set."""
    z = 0
    for _ in range(max_iter):
        if abs(z) > 2:
            return False
        z = z**2 + c
    return True

Markov Chains: Memoryless Stochastic Processes

Tags: markov-chains stochastic-processes probability

Historical Context

Markov chains are named after Andrey Markov, a Russian mathematician who introduced them in 1906 as a way to extend the law of large numbers to dependent random variables. Markov was motivated by a disagreement with Pavel Nekrasov, who claimed that independence was necessary for the law of large numbers. Markov's work demonstrated that a weaker condition—the Markov property—suffices. Today, Markov chains are ubiquitous in physics, biology, economics, computer science, and machine learning.

Definitions

  • Markov Property: A stochastic process \(\{X_n\}\) satisfies the Markov property if: \[P(X_{n+1} = j | X_n = i, X_{n-1} = i_{n-1}, ..., X_0 = i_0) = P(X_{n+1} = j | X_n = i)\]
  • Transition Matrix: For a finite state space, \(P = (p_{ij})\) where \(p_{ij} = P(X_{n+1} = j | X_n = i)\).
  • Stationary Distribution: A probability distribution \(\pi\) satisfying \(\pi P = \pi\).
  • Irreducible Chain: Every state can be reached from every other state.
  • Aperiodic State: A state with period 1, where period is \(\gcd\{n > 0 : p_{ii}^{(n)} > 0\}\).

Theorem: Fundamental Theorem of Markov Chains

Theorem: Let \(P\) be the transition matrix of a finite-state Markov chain that is:

  1. Irreducible: All states communicate with each other.
  2. Aperiodic: The chain is not trapped in cycles.

Then:

  1. There exists a unique stationary distribution \(\pi\).
  2. For all initial distributions \(\mu\): \(\lim_{n \to \infty} \mu P^n = \pi\).
  3. For all states \(i, j\): \(\lim_{n \to \infty} p_{ij}^{(n)} = \pi_j\).

Proof Sketch

Existence: Consider the Cesàro averages \(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} P^n\). By compactness, a subsequence converges to a stochastic matrix with identical rows, giving a stationary distribution.

Uniqueness: If \(\pi\) and \(\pi'\) are both stationary, consider the total variation distance. Irreducibility ensures that disagreement would propagate, leading to a contradiction.

Convergence: Aperiodicity ensures that \(P^n\) has all positive entries for large \(n\). The Perron-Frobenius theorem guarantees that 1 is the unique eigenvalue of maximum modulus, with eigenvector \(\pi\). All other eigenvalues satisfy \(|\lambda| < 1\), giving convergence. ∎

Notation Guide

  • \(S = \{1, 2, ..., n\}\) — State space
  • \(P = (p_{ij})\) — Transition matrix
  • \(p_{ij}^{(n)}\) — \(n\)-step transition probability
  • \(\pi = (\pi_1, ..., \pi_n)\) — Stationary distribution
  • \(T_i = \min\{n \geq 1 : X_n = i\}\) — Return time to state \(i\)
  • \(\mathbb{E}_i[T_i]\) — Expected return time

import numpy as np
from scipy.linalg import eig

class MarkovChain:
    """Finite-state Markov chain analysis."""
    
    def __init__(self, transition_matrix):
        """
        transition_matrix: n x n stochastic matrix
        """
        self.P = np.array(transition_matrix, dtype=float)
        self.n = self.P.shape[0]
        
        # Verify stochastic
        assert np.allclose(self.P.sum(axis=1), 1), "Rows must sum to 1"
        assert np.all(self.P >= 0), "Probabilities must be non-negative"
    
    def step(self, distribution):
        """Evolve distribution one step: π → πP."""
        return distribution @ self.P
    
    def n_step(self, distribution, n):
        """Evolve distribution n steps."""
        return distribution @ np.linalg.matrix_power(self.P, n)
    
    def stationary_distribution(self):
        """
        Compute stationary distribution.
        Solve πP = π with sum(π) = 1.
        """
        # Find left eigenvector with eigenvalue 1
        eigenvalues, eigenvectors = eig(self.P.T)
        
        # Find eigenvalue closest to 1
        idx = np.argmin(np.abs(eigenvalues - 1))
        pi = eigenvectors[:, idx].real
        
        # Normalize
        pi = pi / np.sum(pi)
        return pi
    
    def is_irreducible(self):
        """Check if chain is irreducible (all states communicate)."""
        # Compute (I + P)^(n-1) - if all entries positive, irreducible
        M = np.linalg.matrix_power(np.eye(self.n) + self.P, self.n - 1)
        return np.all(M > 0)
    
    def period(self, state):
        """Compute period of a state."""
        # Find all n where p_ii^(n) > 0
        periods = []
        Pn = np.eye(self.n)
        for n in range(1, 100):  # Check up to 100 steps
            Pn = Pn @ self.P
            if Pn[state, state] > 1e-10:
                periods.append(n)
        
        if not periods:
            return 0  # Transient
        
        # Compute GCD
        from math import gcd
        from functools import reduce
        return reduce(gcd, periods)
    
    def is_aperiodic(self):
        """Check if chain is aperiodic."""
        return all(self.period(i) == 1 for i in range(self.n))
    
    def expected_return_time(self, state):
        """Expected return time to state (if stationary)."""
        pi = self.stationary_distribution()
        return 1 / pi[state]
    
    def simulate(self, initial_state, steps, seed=None):
        """Simulate a trajectory."""
        if seed is not None:
            np.random.seed(seed)
        
        trajectory = [initial_state]
        current = initial_state
        
        for _ in range(steps):
            current = np.random.choice(self.n, p=self.P[current])
            trajectory.append(current)
        
        return trajectory
    
    def mixing_time(self, epsilon=0.01, max_steps=10000):
        """
        Estimate mixing time: steps needed for total variation
        distance from stationary to be < epsilon.
        """
        pi = self.stationary_distribution()
        
        # Start from worst case (point mass)
        dist = np.zeros(self.n)
        dist[0] = 1
        
        for t in range(max_steps):
            dist = self.step(dist)
            tv_distance = 0.5 * np.sum(np.abs(dist - pi))
            if tv_distance < epsilon:
                return t
        
        return max_steps

Problem: AIME 2023

Let \(P\) be a point inside triangle \(ABC\) such that \(\angle PAB = \angle PBC = \angle PCA = \omega\). If \(AB = 13\), \(BC = 14\), and \(CA = 15\), find \(\tan \omega\) expressed as a reduced fraction \(\frac{m}{n}\), and compute \(m + n\).

💡 Hint

The point \(P\) is known as the Brocard point of the triangle. Use the fact that for the Brocard point, \(\cot \omega = \cot A + \cot B + \cot C\). First compute the area of the triangle using Heron's formula, then find the cotangents of the angles.

✅ Solution

Step 1: Compute the semiperimeter and area.

\(s = \frac{13 + 14 + 15}{2} = 21\)

By Heron's formula: \(\text{Area} = \sqrt{21 \cdot 8 \cdot 7 \cdot 6} = \sqrt{7056} = 84\)

Step 2: Compute \(\cot A\), \(\cot B\), \(\cot C\).

Using \(\cot A = \frac{b^2 + c^2 - a^2}{4 \cdot \text{Area}}\):

\(\cot A = \frac{15^2 + 13^2 - 14^2}{4 \cdot 84} = \frac{225 + 169 - 196}{336} = \frac{198}{336} = \frac{33}{56}\)

\(\cot B = \frac{14^2 + 15^2 - 13^2}{4 \cdot 84} = \frac{196 + 225 - 169}{336} = \frac{252}{336} = \frac{3}{4}\)

\(\cot C = \frac{13^2 + 14^2 - 15^2}{4 \cdot 84} = \frac{169 + 196 - 225}{336} = \frac{140}{336} = \frac{5}{12}\)

Step 3: Apply the Brocard point formula.

\(\cot \omega = \cot A + \cot B + \cot C = \frac{33}{56} + \frac{3}{4} + \frac{5}{12}\)

Finding common denominator (168):

\(\cot \omega = \frac{99 + 126 + 70}{168} = \frac{295}{168}\)

Step 4: Find \(\tan \omega\).

\(\tan \omega = \frac{168}{295}\)

Since \(\gcd(168, 295) = 1\), we have \(m = 168\) and \(n = 295\).

Answer: \(m + n = 168 + 295 = \boxed{463}\)

📊 Visualization of the Day: The Lorenz Attractor

The Lorenz attractor is a set of chaotic solutions to the Lorenz system, a simplified mathematical model for atmospheric convection. Discovered by Edward Lorenz in 1963, it was one of the first examples of deterministic chaos—a system whose future states are highly sensitive to initial conditions.

The system is defined by three ordinary differential equations:

\[\frac{dx}{dt} = \sigma(y - x)\] \[\frac{dy}{dt} = x(\rho - z) - y\] \[\frac{dz}{dt} = xy - \beta z\]

With parameters \(\sigma = 10\), \(\rho = 28\), and \(\beta = 8/3\), the system exhibits chaotic behavior, with trajectories forming the iconic butterfly-shaped attractor. The Lorenz attractor has become a symbol of chaos theory and the "butterfly effect"—the idea that small changes can have large consequences.

import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import solve_ivp
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from mpl_toolkits.mplot3d import Axes3D

def lorenz_system(t, state, sigma=10, rho=28, beta=8/3):
    """
    Lorenz system of ODEs.
    dx/dt = sigma * (y - x)
    dy/dt = x * (rho - z) - y
    dz/dt = x * y - beta * z
    """
    x, y, z = state
    dxdt = sigma * (y - x)
    dydt = x * (rho - z) - y
    dzdt = x * y - beta * z
    return [dxdt, dydt, dzdt]

def simulate_lorenz(initial_state, t_span, t_eval, **params):
    """
    Simulate Lorenz system.
    """
    solution = solve_ivp(
        lorenz_system, 
        t_span, 
        initial_state, 
        t_eval=t_eval,
        args=(params.get('sigma', 10), 
              params.get('rho', 28), 
              params.get('beta', 8/3)),
        method='RK45'
    )
    return solution

def plot_lorenz_attractor(solution, title="Lorenz Attractor"):
    """
    Create 3D plot of Lorenz attractor.
    """
    fig = plt.figure(figsize=(12, 9))
    ax = fig.add_subplot(111, projection='3d')
    
    x, y, z = solution.y
    
    # Color by time
    colors = np.linspace(0, 1, len(x))
    scatter = ax.scatter(x, y, z, c=colors, cmap='viridis', s=0.5, alpha=0.8)
    
    ax.set_xlabel('X')
    ax.set_ylabel('Y')
    ax.set_zlabel('Z')
    ax.set_title(title)
    
    plt.colorbar(scatter, label='Time')
    plt.tight_layout()
    return fig

def demonstrate_sensitivity(initial1, initial2, t_span, t_eval, **params):
    """
    Demonstrate sensitive dependence on initial conditions
    (the butterfly effect).
    """
    sol1 = simulate_lorenz(initial1, t_span, t_eval, **params)
    sol2 = simulate_lorenz(initial2, t_span, t_eval, **params)
    
    # Compute distance between trajectories
    diff = np.sqrt(np.sum((sol1.y - sol2.y)**2, axis=0))
    
    return sol1, sol2, diff
