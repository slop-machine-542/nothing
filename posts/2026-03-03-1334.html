1. Algebraic Number Theory: The Unit Theorem

Motivation

Dirichlet's unit theorem describes the structure of the group of units in the ring of integers of a number field. This fundamental result, proved by Dirichlet in 1846, reveals that the units form a finitely generated abelian group with a precise rank determined by the number of real and complex embeddings. Understanding units is essential for computing class numbers and solving Diophantine equations.

Definitions

Let \(K\) be a number field of degree \(n = [K : \mathbb{Q}]\) with \(r_1\) real embeddings and \(2r_2\) complex embeddings (so \(n = r_1 + 2r_2\)).

  • Ring of Integers: \(\mathcal{O}_K\) is the integral closure of \(\mathbb{Z}\) in \(K\).
  • Unit: An element \(\alpha \in \mathcal{O}_K\) such that \(\alpha^{-1} \in \mathcal{O}_K\).
  • Group of Units: \(\mathcal{O}_K^\times = \{\alpha \in \mathcal{O}_K : \alpha \text{ is a unit}\}\).
  • Roots of Unity: \(\mu_K = \{\zeta \in K : \zeta^n = 1 \text{ for some } n \geq 1\}\).
  • Regulator: A measure of the "size" of the unit group, defined via the logarithmic embedding.

Theorem: Dirichlet's Unit Theorem

Theorem: Let \(K\) be a number field with \(r_1\) real embeddings and \(r_2\) pairs of complex conjugate embeddings. Then:

\[\mathcal{O}_K^\times \cong \mu_K \times \mathbb{Z}^{r_1 + r_2 - 1}\]

where \(\mu_K\) is the finite cyclic group of roots of unity in \(K\). The rank of the free part is \(r = r_1 + r_2 - 1\).

Proof Sketch

Step 1: Define the logarithmic embedding \(\lambda: K^\times \to \mathbb{R}^{r_1 + r_2}\) by:

\[\lambda(\alpha) = (\log|\sigma_1(\alpha)|, \ldots, \log|\sigma_{r_1}(\alpha)|, 2\log|\sigma_{r_1+1}(\alpha)|, \ldots, 2\log|\sigma_{r_1+r_2}(\alpha)|)\]

where \(\sigma_i\) are the embeddings.

Step 2: Show that \(\mathcal{O}_K^\times = \ker(\lambda) \cap \mathcal{O}_K^\times\) consists exactly of roots of unity. This uses the fact that elements of bounded norm lie in a finite set.

Step 3: Prove that \(\lambda(\mathcal{O}_K^\times)\) is a discrete subgroup of the hyperplane \(H = \{x \in \mathbb{R}^{r_1+r_2} : \sum x_i = 0\}\).

Step 4: Show that \(\lambda(\mathcal{O}_K^\times)\) spans \(H\) as an \(\mathbb{R}\)-vector space. This requires Minkowski's geometry of numbers to construct units with prescribed logarithmic embeddings.

Step 5: Conclude that \(\lambda(\mathcal{O}_K^\times) \cong \mathbb{Z}^{r_1+r_2-1}\) and therefore \(\mathcal{O}_K^\times \cong \mu_K \times \mathbb{Z}^{r_1+r_2-1}\). ∎

Notation Guide

  • \(\mathcal{O}_K\): Ring of integers of \(K\)
  • \(\mathcal{O}_K^\times\): Unit group
  • \(r_1\): Number of real embeddings
  • \(r_2\): Number of pairs of complex embeddings
  • \(N_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha)\): Field norm of \(\alpha\)
  • \(R_K\): Regulator of \(K\)

2. Class Field Theory: The Artin Reciprocity Law

Motivation

Class field theory, one of the crowning achievements of 20th-century mathematics, provides a complete description of all abelian extensions of a number field. The Artin reciprocity law establishes an isomorphism between the Galois group of an abelian extension and a generalized ideal class group, unifying quadratic reciprocity and generalizing it to all number fields.

Definitions

  • Ray Class Group: For a modulus \(\mathfrak{m}\), \(Cl_{\mathfrak{m}}(K) = I_{\mathfrak{m}} / P_{\mathfrak{m}}\) where \(I_{\mathfrak{m}}\) is the group of fractional ideals coprime to \(\mathfrak{m}\) and \(P_{\mathfrak{m}}\) is the subgroup of principal ideals generated by elements congruent to 1 modulo \(\mathfrak{m}\).
  • Artin Symbol: For a prime \(\mathfrak{p}\) unramified in \(L/K\), \(\left(\frac{L/K}{\mathfrak{p}}\right) \in \text{Gal}(L/K)\) is the Frobenius automorphism.
  • Conductor: The smallest modulus \(\mathfrak{f}\) such that \(L/K\) is contained in the ray class field modulo \(\mathfrak{f}\).

Theorem: Artin Reciprocity

Theorem: Let \(L/K\) be an abelian extension of number fields. There exists a modulus \(\mathfrak{m}\) divisible by all ramified primes such that the Artin map:

\[\psi_{L/K}: I_{\mathfrak{m}} \to \text{Gal}(L/K)\]

given by \(\psi_{L/K}(\mathfrak{p}) = \left(\frac{L/K}{\mathfrak{p}}\right)\) induces an isomorphism:

\[I_{\mathfrak{m}} / P_{\mathfrak{m}} \cdot N_{L/K}(I_{L,\mathfrak{m}}) \cong \text{Gal}(L/K)\]

Key Consequences

1. Existence Theorem: Every finite abelian extension of \(K\) is contained in a ray class field.

2. Quadratic Reciprocity: For distinct odd primes \(p, q\):

\[\left(\frac{p}{q}\right)\left(\frac{q}{p}\right) = (-1)^{\frac{p-1}{2} \cdot \frac{q-1}{2}}\]

This follows from Artin reciprocity applied to \(\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{p^*}) / \mathbb{Q}\) where \(p^* = (-1)^{(p-1)/2}p\).

3. Kronecker-Weber Theorem: Every finite abelian extension of \(\mathbb{Q}\) is contained in a cyclotomic field \(\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_n)\). ∎

Notation Guide

  • \(I_{\mathfrak{m}}\): Group of fractional ideals coprime to \(\mathfrak{m}\)
  • \(P_{\mathfrak{m}}\): Subgroup of principal ideals \((\alpha)\) with \(\alpha \equiv 1 \pmod{\mathfrak{m}}\)
  • \(\left(\frac{L/K}{\mathfrak{p}}\right)\): Artin symbol (Frobenius)
  • \(L^{ab}\): Maximal abelian extension

def quadratic_reciprocity(p, q):
    """
    Demonstrate quadratic reciprocity: (p/q)(q/p) = (-1)^((p-1)/2 * (q-1)/2)

    if not (isprime(p) and isprime(q)):
        return None

        return None

3. Arithmetic Dynamics: Heights on Projective Space

Motivation

Arithmetic dynamics combines number theory and dynamical systems, studying the behavior of rational maps under iteration over number fields. The theory of heights, introduced by Weil and developed by Néron and Tate, provides a crucial tool for understanding the arithmetic of dynamical systems, including the distribution of periodic points and the dynamics of rational maps.

Definitions

  • Weil Height: For \(P = [x_0 : \cdots : x_n] \in \mathbb{P}^n(\mathbb{Q})\) with coprime integer coordinates:

\[h(P) = \log \max_i |x_i|\]

  • Canonical Height: For a morphism \(\phi: \mathbb{P}^n \to \mathbb{P}^n\) of degree \(d \geq 2\):

\[\hat{h}_{\phi}(P) = \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{h(\phi^n(P))}{d^n}\]

  • Preperiodic Point: A point \(P\) such that \(\phi^{n+m}(P) = \phi^n(P)\) for some \(n, m \geq 1\).

Theorem: Northcott's Theorem

Theorem: Let \(K\) be a number field and \(D > 0\). The set:

\[\{P \in \mathbb{P}^n(K) : h(P) \leq D\}\]

is finite. Consequently, for any morphism \(\phi: \mathbb{P}^n \to \mathbb{P}^n\) of degree \(d \geq 2\), the set of preperiodic points \(\text{PrePer}(\phi, K)\) is finite.

Proof Sketch

Step 1: For \(\mathbb{P}^1(\mathbb{Q})\), if \(P = [x : y]\) with \(\gcd(x, y) = 1\), then \(h(P) = \log \max(|x|, |y|)\). The condition \(h(P) \leq D\) implies \(|x|, |y| \leq e^D\), giving finitely many possibilities.

Step 2: For general number fields, use the fact that there are finitely many elements of bounded height and degree.

Step 3: For preperiodic points, use the key property of canonical heights: \(\hat{h}_{\phi}(\phi(P)) = d \cdot \hat{h}_{\phi}(P)\).

Step 4: If \(P\) is preperiodic, then \(\hat{h}_{\phi}(P) = 0\) (since the orbit is finite). By Northcott's theorem applied to \(\hat{h}_{\phi}\), there are finitely many such points. ∎

Notation Guide

  • \(h(P)\): Weil height of point \(P\)
  • \(\hat{h}_{\phi}(P)\): Canonical height with respect to \(\phi\)
  • \(\phi^n\): n-th iterate of \(\phi\)
  • \(H_K(P)\): Multiplicative height over field \(K\)

import math
from fractions import Fraction

def weil_height_projective(P):
    """
    Compute the Weil height of a point in P^1(Q).
    P = [x, y] where x, y are integers (coprime).
    """
    x, y = P
    # Ensure coprime
    g = math.gcd(abs(x), abs(y))
    x, y = x // g, y // g
    
    return math.log(max(abs(x), abs(y)) + 1e-10)

def rational_map_iterates(phi, P, n):
    """
    Compute iterates of a rational map phi on P.
    phi is a function taking [x, y] to [f(x,y), g(x,y)].
    """
    orbit = [P]
    current = P
    
    for _ in range(n):
        current = phi(current)
        # Normalize to coprime integers
        x, y = current
        g = math.gcd(abs(x), abs(y))
        current = (x // g, y // g)
        orbit.append(current)
    
    return orbit

def doubling_map(P):
    """
    The doubling map on P^1: [x:y] -> [x^2 + y^2 : 2xy]
    This corresponds to z -> z^2 in affine coordinates (z = x/y).
    """
    x, y = P
    return (x**2 + y**2, 2*x*y)

def canonical_height_estimate(phi, P, d, n_iterations=10):
    """
    Estimate the canonical height using the limit definition.
    d is the degree of phi.
    """
    current = P
    heights = []
    
    for n in range(n_iterations):
        h = weil_height_projective(current)
        heights.append(h / (d ** n))
        
        # Apply phi
        x, y = phi(current)
        g = math.gcd(abs(x), abs(y))
        current = (x // g, y // g)
    
    return heights

def find_preperiodic_points(phi, bound, max_iter=10):
    """
    Find preperiodic points with coordinates bounded by 'bound'.
    """
    preperiodic = []
    
    for x in range(-bound, bound + 1):
        for y in range(-bound, bound + 1):
            if x == 0 and y == 0:
                continue
            if math.gcd(abs(x), abs(y)) != 1:
                continue
            
            P = (x, y)
            orbit = set()
            current = P
            
            is_preperiodic = False
            for _ in range(max_iter):
                # Normalize
                x_c, y_c = current
                g = math.gcd(abs(x_c), abs(y_c))
                normalized = (x_c // g, y_c // g)
                
                if normalized in orbit:
                    is_preperiodic = True
                    break
                
                orbit.add(normalized)
                current = phi(current)
            
            if is_preperiodic:
                preperiodic.append(P)
    
    return preperiodic

1. Numerical Linear Algebra: The QR Algorithm

Motivation

The QR algorithm, developed independently by Francis and Kublanovskaya in the early 1960s, is one of the most important algorithms in numerical linear algebra. It computes all eigenvalues of a matrix through an elegant iterative process based on QR factorization. Despite its simplicity, the algorithm achieves remarkable convergence properties and remains the foundation for modern eigenvalue computations.

Definitions

  • QR Factorization: For matrix \(A\), factorization \(A = QR\) where \(Q\) is orthogonal and \(R\) is upper triangular.
  • Similarity Transformation: \(A\) and \(B\) are similar if \(B = X^{-1}AX\) for some invertible \(X\).
  • Hessenberg Form: A matrix is upper Hessenberg if \(a_{ij} = 0\) for \(i > j + 1\).
  • Rayleigh Quotient: For vector \(x\), \(r(x) = \frac{x^*Ax}{x^*x}\).

Theorem: Convergence of the QR Algorithm

Theorem: Let \(A\) be a real matrix with eigenvalues satisfying \(|\lambda_1| > |\lambda_2| > \cdots > |\lambda_n| > 0\). The basic QR iteration:

\[A_k = Q_k R_k, \quad A_{k+1} = R_k Q_k\]

converges to an upper triangular matrix with eigenvalues on the diagonal. Specifically:

\[\lim_{k \to \infty} A_k = \Lambda\]

where \(\Lambda\) is upper triangular with \(\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_n\) on the diagonal.

Proof Sketch

Step 1: Show that all iterates \(A_k\) are similar to \(A\):

\[A_{k+1} = R_k Q_k = Q_k^* A_k Q_k\]

so \(A_k = Q^{(k)*} A Q^{(k)}\) where \(Q^{(k)} = Q_1 Q_2 \cdots Q_k\).

Step 2: Relate to power iteration. The first column of \(Q^{(k)}\) converges to the dominant eigenvector of \(A\).

Step 3: Use the fact that \(A^k = Q^{(k)} R^{(k)}\) where \(R^{(k)} = R_k R_{k-1} \cdots R_1\).

Step 4: The subdiagonal entries decay as \((|\lambda_{i+1}|/|\lambda_i|)^k\), giving linear convergence. ∎

Notation Guide

  • \(A = QR\): QR factorization
  • \(A_k\): k-th iterate of QR algorithm
  • \(\kappa(A)\): Condition number of \(A\)
  • \(H\): Upper Hessenberg matrix

import numpy as np
from scipy.linalg import qr, hessenberg

def basic_qr_iteration(A, max_iter=100, tol=1e-10):
    """
    Basic QR iteration for computing eigenvalues.
    """
    n = A.shape[0]
    Ak = A.copy().astype(float)
    
    # First reduce to Hessenberg form for efficiency
    Ak, _ = hessenberg(Ak, calc_q=True)
    
    eigenvalues_history = []
    
    for k in range(max_iter):
        # QR factorization
        Q, R = qr(Ak)
        
        # Reverse multiplication
        Ak = R @ Q
        
        # Store diagonal (eigenvalue approximations)
        eigenvalues_history.append(np.diag(Ak).copy())
        
        # Check convergence (subdiagonal elements)
        subdiag_norm = np.sqrt(np.sum(Ak[np.arange(1,n), np.arange(0,n-1)]**2))
        if subdiag_norm < tol:
            print(f"Converged after {k+1} iterations")
            break
    
    return np.diag(Ak), eigenvalues_history, Ak

def qr_with_shifts(A, max_iter=100, tol=1e-10):
    """
    QR algorithm with Wilkinson shift for faster convergence.
    """
    n = A.shape[0]
    Ak = A.copy().astype(float)
    
    # Reduce to Hessenberg form
    Ak, _ = hessenberg(Ak, calc_q=True)
    
    eigenvalues = []
    
    while n > 1:
        for k in range(max_iter):
            # Wilkinson shift: eigenvalue of bottom 2x2 closest to a_nn
            submatrix = Ak[n-2:n, n-2:n]
            eigenvals_2x2 = np.linalg.eigvals(submatrix)
            shift = eigenvals_2x2[np.argmin(np.abs(eigenvals_2x2 - Ak[n-1, n-1]))]
            
            # QR with shift
            Q, R = qr(Ak - shift * np.eye(n))
            Ak = R @ Q + shift * np.eye(n)
            
            # Check for convergence
            if abs(Ak[n-1, n-2]) < tol:
                eigenvalues.append(Ak[n-1, n-1])
                n -= 1
                Ak = Ak[:n, :n]
                break
        else:
            # No convergence, extract anyway
            eigenvalues.append(Ak[n-1, n-1])
            n -= 1
            Ak = Ak[:n, :n]
    
    eigenvalues.append(Ak[0, 0])
    
    return np.array(eigenvalues)

2. Inverse Problems: Tikhonov Regularization

Motivation

Inverse problems arise when we seek to determine causes from observed effects—reconstructing an image from blurred data, finding a source from measured field values, or determining material properties from boundary measurements. These problems are typically ill-posed: small errors in data can lead to large errors in solutions. Tikhonov regularization, introduced by Andrey Tikhonov in the 1940s, provides a principled framework for obtaining stable solutions.

Definitions

  • Forward Problem: Given model parameters \(x\), compute observable data \(b = Ax\).
  • Inverse Problem: Given data \(b\), find \(x\) such that \(Ax = b\).
  • Ill-Posed Problem: (Hadamard) A problem where solution does not exist, is not unique, or does not depend continuously on data.
  • Moore-Penrose Pseudoinverse: \(A^\dagger = (A^*A)^{-1}A^*\) for full-rank matrices.

Theorem: Tikhonov Regularization

Theorem: Let \(A \in \mathbb{C}^{m \times n}\) with \(m \geq n\) and \(\text{rank}(A) = n\). For \(\lambda > 0\), the Tikhonov-regularized solution:

\[x_{\lambda} = \arg\min_x \|Ax - b\|_2^2 + \lambda^2\|x\|_2^2\]

is given by:

\[x_{\lambda} = (A^*A + \lambda^2 I)^{-1}A^*b\]

As \(\lambda \to 0\), \(x_{\lambda} \to A^\dagger b\) (the minimum-norm least squares solution). As \(\lambda \to \infty\), \(x_{\lambda} \to 0\).

Proof

Step 1: The objective function is:

\[J(x) = (Ax - b)^*(Ax - b) + \lambda^2 x^*x\]

Step 2: Take the gradient and set to zero:

\[\nabla J = 2A^*(Ax - b) + 2\lambda^2 x = 0\]

Step 3: Rearrange:

\[(A^*A + \lambda^2 I)x = A^*b\]

Step 4: Since \(A^*A + \lambda^2 I\) is positive definite (hence invertible) for \(\lambda > 0\):

\[x_{\lambda} = (A^*A + \lambda^2 I)^{-1}A^*b\]

Step 5: The limiting behavior follows from the singular value decomposition of \(A\). ∎

Notation Guide

  • \(\|x\|_2\): Euclidean norm
  • \(\lambda\): Regularization parameter
  • \(A^*\): Conjugate transpose (adjoint)
  • \(A^\dagger\): Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse
  • \(\sigma_i\): Singular values of \(A\)

from scipy.linalg import svd
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def tikhonov_solve(A, b, lambda_reg):
    """
    Solve Tikhonov regularization problem.
    """
    n = A.shape[1]
    
    # Using normal equations
    AtA = A.T @ A
    Atb = A.T @ b
    
    x_lambda = np.linalg.solve(AtA + lambda_reg**2 * np.eye(n), Atb)
    
    return x_lambda

def tikhonov_svd(A, b, lambda_reg):
    """
    Solve using SVD for better numerical stability.
    """
    U, s, Vh = svd(A, full_matrices=False)
    
    # Filter factors
    filter_factors = s**2 / (s**2 + lambda_reg**2)
    
    # Solution
    x_lambda = Vh.T @ ((U.T @ b) * filter_factors / s)
    
    return x_lambda

def l_curve(A, b, lambda_values):
    """
    Compute L-curve for choosing regularization parameter.
    """
    residuals = []
    norms = []
    
    for lam in lambda_values:
        x = tikhonov_solve(A, b, lam)
        residuals.append(np.linalg.norm(A @ x - b))
        norms.append(np.linalg.norm(x))
    
    return np.array(residuals), np.array(norms)

3. Uncertainty Quantification: Polynomial Chaos

Motivation

Uncertainty quantification (UQ) addresses how uncertainties in model inputs propagate to outputs. Polynomial chaos, introduced by Norbert Wiener in 1938 and revitalized by Roger Ghanem in the 1990s, represents random quantities as expansions in orthogonal polynomials. This spectral approach enables efficient computation of statistics and sensitivity analysis for complex systems with uncertain parameters.

Definitions

  • Polynomial Chaos Expansion: For random variable \(Y = f(\xi)\) where \(\xi\) is a standard random variable:

\[Y = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} y_k \Phi_k(\xi)\]

  • Orthogonal Polynomials: \(\{\Phi_k\}\) satisfy \(\mathbb{E}[\Phi_i(\xi)\Phi_j(\xi)] = \delta_{ij}\gamma_j\).
  • Gaussian-Hermite: For \(\xi \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)\), \(\Phi_k\) are Hermite polynomials.
  • Uniform-Legendre: For \(\xi \sim \mathcal{U}(-1,1)\), \(\Phi_k\) are Legendre polynomials.

Theorem: Convergence of Polynomial Chaos

Theorem: Let \(f: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}\) be such that \(\mathbb{E}[f(\xi)^2] < \infty\) where \(\xi\) has density \(\rho\). Then:

\[f(\xi) = \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} c_k \Phi_k(\xi)\]

where \(\{\Phi_k\}\) are orthogonal polynomials with respect to \(\rho\), and the series converges in \(L^2(\rho)\). The coefficients are:

\[c_k = \frac{\mathbb{E}[f(\xi)\Phi_k(\xi)]}{\mathbb{E}[\Phi_k(\xi)^2]}\]

Key Properties

Mean: \(\mathbb{E}[Y] = y_0\) (since \(\Phi_0 = 1\) and \(\mathbb{E}[\Phi_k] = 0\) for \(k \geq 1\)).

Variance: \(\text{Var}(Y) = \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} y_k^2 \gamma_k\) where \(\gamma_k = \mathbb{E}[\Phi_k^2]\).

Sobol Indices: For multivariate expansions, partial variances decompose as:

\[\text{Var}(Y) = \sum_{\alpha \neq 0} y_{\alpha}^2 \gamma_{\alpha}\]

enabling global sensitivity analysis. ∎

Notation Guide

  • \(\Phi_k\): Orthogonal polynomial of degree \(k\)
  • \(y_k\): Polynomial chaos coefficient
  • \(\gamma_k = \mathbb{E}[\Phi_k^2]\): Normalization constant
  • \(S_i\): Sobol sensitivity index

def hermite_probabilist(n):
    """
    Probabilist's Hermite polynomials (orthogonal w.r.t. N(0,1)).
    He_n(x) = (-1)^n e^{x^2/2} d^n/dx^n e^{-x^2/2}
    """
    # Coefficients for probabilist's Hermite
    if n == 0:
        return np.array([1])
    elif n == 1:
        return np.array([0, 1])
    else:
        # Recurrence: He_{n+1}(x) = x He_n(x) - n He_{n-1}(x)
        coeffs = [np.array([1]), np.array([0, 1])]
        for i in range(1, n):
            c_new = np.convolve([0, 1], coeffs[i]) - i * np.pad(coeffs[i-1], (1, 0), constant_values=0)
            coeffs.append(c_new)
        return coeffs[n]

def polynomial_chaos_coefficients(f, poly_type='hermite', degree=10):
    """
    Compute polynomial chaos coefficients for function f.
    """
    coeffs = []
    
    if poly_type == 'hermite':
        # Gaussian: integrate against N(0,1)
        for k in range(degree + 1):
            poly = hermite_probabilist(k)
            
            # Integral of f(x) * He_k(x) * exp(-x^2/2) / sqrt(2*pi)
            integrand = lambda x: f(x) * np.polyval(poly[::-1], x) * np.exp(-x**2/2) / np.sqrt(2*np.pi)

            # Normalization: E[He_k^2] = k!
            denominator = factorial(k)
            
            coeffs.append(numerator / denominator)
    
    return np.array(coeffs)

1. Perfect Numbers and the Euclid-Euler Theorem

Historical Context

Perfect numbers have fascinated mathematicians since antiquity. A perfect number equals the sum of its proper divisors—6 = 1 + 2 + 3, 28 = 1 + 2 + 4 + 7 + 14. Euclid proved that if \(2^p - 1\) is prime (a Mersenne prime), then \(2^{p-1}(2^p - 1)\) is perfect. Euler proved the converse: every even perfect number has this form. Whether odd perfect numbers exist remains one of mathematics' oldest unsolved problems.

Definitions

  • Perfect Number: A positive integer \(n\) such that \(\sigma(n) = 2n\), where \(\sigma(n)\) is the sum of all divisors of \(n\).
  • Mersenne Prime: A prime of the form \(M_p = 2^p - 1\).
  • Deficient/Abundant: \(n\) is deficient if \(\sigma(n) < 2n\), abundant if \(\sigma(n) > 2n\).

Theorem: Euclid-Euler Theorem

Theorem: An even number \(n\) is perfect if and only if:

\[n = 2^{p-1}(2^p - 1)\]

where \(2^p - 1\) is a Mersenne prime.

Proof

(Euclid's direction): Let \(q = 2^p - 1\) be prime. For \(n = 2^{p-1}q\):

\[\sigma(n) = \sigma(2^{p-1})\sigma(q) = (2^p - 1)(q + 1) = q \cdot 2^p = 2n\]

(Euler's direction): Let \(n\) be an even perfect number. Write \(n = 2^{k}m\) where \(m\) is odd. Then:

\[\sigma(n) = \sigma(2^k)\sigma(m) = (2^{k+1} - 1)\sigma(m) = 2^{k+1}m\]

So \(\sigma(m) = \frac{2^{k+1}m}{2^{k+1} - 1}\). Since \(\gcd(2^{k+1} - 1, 2^{k+1}) = 1\), we have \((2^{k+1} - 1) \mid m\).

Write \(m = (2^{k+1} - 1)j\). Then \(\sigma(m) = 2^{k+1}j\). If \(j > 1\), then \(m\) has divisors 1, \(j\), \(m\), so:

\[\sigma(m) \geq 1 + j + m = 1 + j + (2^{k+1} - 1)j = 1 + 2^{k+1}j > 2^{k+1}j\]

Contradiction. Thus \(j = 1\), so \(m = 2^{k+1} - 1\) is prime. ∎

Notation Guide

  • \(\sigma(n)\): Sum of divisors function
  • \(\omega(n)\): Number of distinct prime factors
  • \(\Omega(n)\): Total number of prime factors
  • \(\phi(n)\): Euler's totient function

2. The Mordell Conjecture (Faltings' Theorem)

Historical Context

Louis Mordell conjectured in 1922 that curves of genus \(g \geq 2\) over number fields have finitely many rational points. This profound statement, proved by Gerd Faltings in 1983, earned Faltings a Fields Medal and revolutionized arithmetic geometry. The theorem settled several famous problems, including the conjecture that the Fermat equation has only finitely many solutions for each exponent \(n \geq 4\).

Definitions

  • Algebraic Curve: A one-dimensional variety defined by polynomial equations.
  • Genus: The number of "holes" in the Riemann surface; for a smooth plane curve of degree \(d\), \(g = (d-1)(d-2)/2\).
  • Rational Point: A point with coordinates in the base field (e.g., \(\mathbb{Q}\)).
  • Abelian Variety: A projective algebraic variety that is also an algebraic group (generalization of elliptic curves).

Theorem: Faltings' Theorem

Theorem: Let \(C\) be a curve of genus \(g \geq 2\) defined over a number field \(K\). Then the set of \(K\)-rational points \(C(K)\) is finite.

Key Ideas in the Proof

1. Parshin's Trick: Reduce to showing that the set of curves of fixed genus with good reduction outside a fixed set of places is finite (Shafarevich conjecture).

2. Arakelov Theory: Develop intersection theory on arithmetic surfaces, combining algebraic geometry over finite fields with complex geometry.

3. Semistability: Use the theory of semistable reduction to control the behavior of curves at bad primes.

4. Height Bounds: Prove that heights of rational points are bounded, using the geometry of the Jacobian variety.

5. Diagonal Argument: Show that infinitely many rational points would lead to a contradiction via properties of the Faltings height on moduli spaces. ∎

Notation Guide

  • \(C(K)\): Set of \(K\)-rational points on \(C\)
  • \(g(C)\): Genus of curve \(C\)
  • \(\text{Jac}(C)\): Jacobian variety of \(C\)
  • \(h_{Fal}(A)\): Faltings height of abelian variety \(A\)

3. The ABC Conjecture

Historical Context

Proposed by Joseph Oesterlé and David Masser in 1985, the ABC conjecture connects the additive and multiplicative structure of integers in a profound way. It states that for any \(\epsilon > 0\), there are only finitely many triples of coprime positive integers \(a + b = c\) where \(c\) is significantly larger than the product of the distinct prime factors of \(abc\). The conjecture implies many famous results including Fermat's Last Theorem for sufficiently large exponents.

Definitions

  • Radical: For positive integer \(n\), \(\text{rad}(n) = \prod_{p|n} p\) (product of distinct prime factors).
  • ABC Triple: A triple \((a, b, c)\) of coprime positive integers with \(a + b = c\).
  • Quality: \(q(a, b, c) = \frac{\log c}{\log \text{rad}(abc)}\).

The ABC Conjecture

Conjecture: For every \(\epsilon > 0\), there exist only finitely many ABC triples \((a, b, c)\) with:

\[c > \text{rad}(abc)^{1+\epsilon}\]

Equivalently, \(\limsup q(a, b, c) = 1\).

Key Consequences

1. Fermat's Last Theorem (asymptotic): For \(n\) sufficiently large, \(x^n + y^n = z^n\) has no nontrivial solutions.

Proof sketch: If \(a^n + b^n = c^n\), then \(\text{rad}(abc) \leq abc = c^{3/n}\). For large \(n\), this contradicts ABC.

2. Roth's Theorem on Diophantine Approximation: ABC implies bounds on approximating algebraic numbers by rationals.

3. Mordell Conjecture: ABC implies Faltings' theorem on rational points of curves.

4. Wieferich Primes: Infinitude of non-Wieferich primes (primes \(p\) with \(2^{p-1} \not\equiv 1 \pmod{p^2}\)). ∎

Notation Guide

  • \(\text{rad}(n)\): Radical of \(n\)
  • \(q(a, b, c)\): Quality of ABC triple
  • \(\omega(n)\): Number of distinct prime factors

4. The Sato-Tate Conjecture

Historical Context

The Sato-Tate conjecture, formulated independently by Mikio Sato and John Tate around 1960, predicts the statistical distribution of Frobenius traces for elliptic curves without complex multiplication. The conjecture was proved by Laurent Clozel, Michael Harris, Nick Shepherd-Barron, and Richard Taylor in 2006-2008, representing a triumph of the Langlands program and establishing deep connections between number theory and representation theory.

Definitions

  • Frobenius Trace: For an elliptic curve \(E\) over \(\mathbb{F}_p\), \(a_p = p + 1 - |E(\mathbb{F}_p)|\).
  • Sato-Tate Measure: The probability measure on \([-2, 2]\) with density \(\frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{4 - x^2}\).
  • Complex Multiplication (CM): The endomorphism ring of \(E\) is larger than \(\mathbb{Z}\).
  • Hasse Bound: \(|a_p| \leq 2\sqrt{p}\).

Theorem: Sato-Tate Conjecture

Theorem: Let \(E\) be an elliptic curve over \(\mathbb{Q}\) without complex multiplication. For any interval \([\alpha, \beta] \subset [-2, 2]\):

\[\lim_{N \to \infty} \frac{\#\{p \leq N : a_p/\sqrt{p} \in [\alpha, \beta]\}}{\pi(N)} = \int_{\alpha}^{\beta} \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{4 - x^2} \, dx\]

where \(\pi(N)\) is the prime counting function.

Key Ideas in the Proof

1. Galois Representations: Associate to \(E\) the family of \(\ell\)-adic representations \(\rho_{E,\ell}: G_{\mathbb{Q}} \to \text{GL}_2(\mathbb{Z}_\ell)\).

2. Potential Automorphy: Prove that symmetric powers of these representations are potentially automorphic, meaning they correspond to automorphic forms.

3. Analytic Properties: Establish analytic continuation and functional equations for associated L-functions.

4. Equidistribution: Apply the Weyl criterion for equidistribution, reducing to vanishing of certain L-function coefficients. ∎

Notation Guide

  • \(a_p\): Frobenius trace at \(p\)
  • \(\theta_p = \arccos(a_p / 2\sqrt{p})\): Sato-Tate angle
  • \(\mu_{ST}\): Sato-Tate measure
  • \(\text{Sym}^n\): n-th symmetric power representation

📜 Math History: The Development of Algebraic Number Theory

The 19th century witnessed a profound transformation in mathematics, as the study of numbers evolved from elementary arithmetic into the sophisticated discipline of algebraic number theory. This revolution began with Carl Friedrich Gauss's Disquisitiones Arithmeticae (1801), which established number theory as a rigorous mathematical discipline and introduced the concept of congruence. Gauss's study of quadratic forms and cyclotomic equations laid the groundwork for what would become a vast edifice of algebraic number theory.

The true birth of algebraic number theory, however, is often attributed to Ernst Kummer's work on Fermat's Last Theorem in the 1840s. Attempting to prove that the equation \(x^n + y^n = z^n\) has no nontrivial integer solutions for \(n > 2\), Kummer discovered that the ring of integers in cyclotomic fields lacks unique factorization. Rather than abandoning his approach, Kummer invented ideal numbers—what we now call ideals—to restore unique factorization. This breakthrough transformed number theory, introducing algebraic structures that would become fundamental to modern mathematics.

Richard Dedekind, in the 1870s, refined Kummer's ideas into the modern theory of ideals. Dedekind's approach emphasized conceptual clarity over computational methods, defining ideals as subsets of rings satisfying certain properties rather than as "ideal numbers." His theory of Dedekind domains established the foundation for algebraic number theory, proving that every nonzero ideal in the ring of integers of a number field factors uniquely into prime ideals. This theorem, the unique factorization of ideals, remains one of the cornerstones of the subject.

The late 19th and early 20th centuries saw algebraic number theory expand in multiple directions. Leopold Kronecker developed his theory of divisors, while Dedekind and Heinrich Weber applied algebraic methods to function fields, revealing deep analogies between number fields and function fields of curves. David Hilbert's Zahlbericht (1897) synthesized these developments and posed fundamental questions that would guide research for decades, including his 12th problem on constructing abelian extensions of number fields.

The 20th century brought even greater abstraction and generality. Emmy Noether's work in the 1920s emphasized the importance of ascending chain conditions and abstract algebraic structures. Her approach, which influenced all of modern algebra, provided the conceptual framework for understanding factorization properties in general rings. The development of class field theory by Teiji Takagi, Emil Artin, and Helmut Hasse established a complete description of abelian extensions of number fields, realizing a program that had begun with Gauss's study of quadratic reciprocity.

Today, algebraic number theory stands at the intersection of multiple mathematical disciplines. The Langlands program, initiated in the 1960s, seeks to unify number theory with representation theory and harmonic analysis through the study of automorphic forms and L-functions. Andrew Wiles's proof of Fermat's Last Theorem in 1994, building on the modularity conjecture for elliptic curves, demonstrated the power of these modern methods. The subject continues to evolve, with connections to algebraic geometry, mathematical physics, and computer science ensuring that the study of algebraic numbers remains as vibrant as ever.

Problem: A Competition Problem on Sums of Powers

Problem (IMO Shortlist 2005): Let \(n\) be a positive integer, and let \(a_1, a_2, \ldots, a_n\) be distinct positive integers. Prove that:

\[\sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i^3 \geq \left(\sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i\right)^2\]

and determine when equality holds.

💡 Hint

Consider the case where the \(a_i\) are consecutive integers starting from 1. Use induction or consider the differences when replacing the sequence with a "smaller" one. The key observation is that for distinct positive integers, the sum of cubes grows faster than the square of the sum.

📖 Solution

Solution: We prove this by induction on \(n\).

Base case (n = 1): We need \(a_1^3 \geq a_1^2\), which holds for all positive integers \(a_1\) with equality when \(a_1 = 1\).

Inductive step: Assume the result holds for all sequences of length \(n-1\). For a sequence of length \(n\), assume without loss of generality that \(a_1 < a_2 < \cdots < a_n\). Since these are distinct positive integers, we have \(a_i \geq i\) for all \(i\).

Consider the function:

\[f(a_1, \ldots, a_n) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i^3 - \left(\sum_{i=1}^{n} a_i\right)^2\]

We claim that the minimum of \(f\) subject to \(1 \leq a_1 < a_2 < \cdots < a_n\) occurs when \(a_i = i\) for all \(i\).

To verify this, note that if we replace \(a_n\) by \(a_n - 1\) (assuming \(a_n > a_{n-1} + 1\)), the change in \(f\) is:

\[\Delta f = [(a_n-1)^3 - a_n^3] - [(S-1)^2 - S^2]\]

where \(S = \sum a_i\). This simplifies to:

\[\Delta f = -3a_n^2 + 3a_n - 1 + 2S - 1 = -3a_n^2 + 3a_n + 2S - 2\]

For \(a_n \geq n\) and \(S \geq n(n+1)/2\), we have \(\Delta f < 0\), meaning decreasing \(a_n\) decreases \(f\). By similar arguments for other variables, the minimum occurs at \(a_i = i\).

For \(a_i = i\):

\[\sum_{i=1}^{n} i^3 = \left(\frac{n(n+1)}{2}\right)^2 = \left(\sum_{i=1}^{n} i\right)^2\]

Thus \(f \geq 0\) with equality if and only if \(a_i = i\) for all \(i\). ∎

🎨 Visualization of the Day: The Modular Group and Farey Tessellation

The modular group \(\text{PSL}(2, \mathbb{Z})\) acts on the upper half-plane by Möbius transformations, producing a beautiful tessellation that reveals deep connections between number theory and geometry. This visualization demonstrates the Farey tessellation, where ideal triangles tile the hyperbolic plane.

Mathematical Background

The modular group consists of matrices \(\begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix}\) with \(ad - bc = 1\), acting on the complex upper half-plane by \(z \mapsto \frac{az + b}{cz + d}\). The fundamental domain is a hyperbolic triangle with vertices at infinity. The tiling generated by this action connects to continued fractions, with geodesics corresponding to Farey sequences.

    """

def draw_geodesic(ax, z1, z2, color='blue', linewidth=1, alpha=0.6):
    """
    Draw geodesic in upper half-plane model.
    Geodesics are either vertical lines or semicircles orthogonal to real axis.
    """
    x1, y1 = z1.real, z1.imag
    x2, y2 = z2.real, z2.imag
    
    if abs(x1 - x2) < 1e-10:
        # Vertical line
        ax.plot([x1, x1], [max(y1, y2, 0.01), 5], color=color, 
                linewidth=linewidth, alpha=alpha)
    else:
        # Semicircle orthogonal to real axis
        # Center is at (c, 0) where c = (|z1|^2 - |z2|^2)/(2*(x1-x2))
        c = (x1**2 + y1**2 - x2**2 - y2**2) / (2*(x1 - x2))
        r = np.sqrt((x1 - c)**2 + y1**2)

        x = c + r * np.cos(theta)

def farey_sequence(n):
    """
