The Riemann Mapping Theorem

Tags: complex-analysis conformal-maps geometric-function-theory

Motivation

The Riemann mapping theorem is one of the most profound results in complex analysis, establishing that any simply connected proper subset of the complex plane (other than the plane itself) is conformally equivalent to the unit disk. This means complex analysis on arbitrary simply connected domains reduces to analysis on the unit disk—a dramatic simplification with far-reaching consequences in fluid dynamics, electrostatics, and geometric function theory.

Definitions

A domain \( \Omega \subseteq \mathbb{C} \) is simply connected if every closed curve in \( \Omega \) can be continuously contracted to a point within \( \Omega \). Equivalently, \( \mathbb{C} \setminus \Omega \) is connected.

A function \( f: \Omega \to \mathbb{C} \) is conformal if it is holomorphic and injective. Conformal maps preserve angles between curves and locally preserve shapes (up to scaling).

The unit disk is \( \mathbb{D} = \{z \in \mathbb{C} : |z| < 1\} \).

Theorem and Proof

Theorem (Riemann Mapping Theorem): Let \( \Omega \subsetneq \mathbb{C} \) be a simply connected domain with \( \Omega \neq \mathbb{C} \). Then for any \( z_0 \in \Omega \), there exists a unique conformal map \( f: \Omega \to \mathbb{D} \) such that \( f(z_0) = 0 \) and \( f'(z_0) > 0 \).

Proof Sketch: The proof proceeds in three stages:

Step 1 (Normalization): First show we can assume \( 0 \notin \Omega \) and \( \Omega \subseteq \mathbb{D} \) by using a Möbius transformation. Since \( \Omega \neq \mathbb{C} \), pick \( a \notin \Omega \). By simple connectivity, we can define a branch of \( \sqrt{z-a} \) on \( \Omega \). This maps \( \Omega \) to a region avoiding some disk, which can be mapped into \( \mathbb{D} \).

Step 2 (Extremal Problem): Consider the family \(\mathcal{F}\) of injective holomorphic functions \( f: \Omega \to \mathbb{D} \) with \( f(z_0) = 0 \). Define \( M = \sup\{|f'(z_0)| : f \in \mathcal{F}\} \). Show \( M < \infty \) using Cauchy estimates.

Step 3 (Existence via Normal Families): By Montel's theorem, \(\mathcal{F}\) is a normal family. Take a sequence \( f_n \in \mathcal{F} \) with \( |f_n'(z_0)| \to M \). A subsequence converges uniformly on compact sets to some \( f \). By Hurwitz's theorem, \( f \) is either constant or injective. Since \( |f'(z_0)| = M > 0 \), \( f \) is injective. If \( f \) were not surjective onto \( \mathbb{D} \), we could construct a function with larger derivative at \( z_0 \), contradicting maximality. Thus \( f \) is the desired conformal map.

Uniqueness: If \( f, g \) both satisfy the conditions, then \( h = f \circ g^{-1} \) is an automorphism of \( \mathbb{D} \) fixing 0 with \( h'(0) > 0 \). By Schwarz lemma, \( h(z) = z \), so \( f = g \). ∎

Notation Guide

  • \( \mathbb{D} \) — unit disk \( \{z : |z| < 1\} \)
  • \( f'(z_0) \) — complex derivative at \( z_0 \)
  • \( \mathcal{F} \) — family of injective holomorphic maps
  • Aut(\( \mathbb{D} \)) — group of conformal automorphisms of the disk
  • \( \text{Aut}(\mathbb{D}) = \{e^{i\theta}\frac{z-a}{1-\bar{a}z} : |a| < 1, \theta \in \mathbb{R}\} \)

Noether Normalization and Dimension Theory

Tags: commutative-algebra dimension-theory integral-extensions

Motivation

Noether normalization is a fundamental result in commutative algebra that provides a geometric perspective on finitely generated algebras over fields. It states that any such algebra can be viewed as a finite extension of a polynomial ring. This theorem is the algebraic foundation for the concept of dimension in algebraic geometry.

Definitions

Let \( k \) be a field. A finitely generated \( k \)-algebra is a ring of the form \( A = k[x_1, \ldots, x_n]/I \) for some \( n \) and ideal \( I \).

An element \( a \in A \) is integral over a subring \( R \subseteq A \) if it satisfies a monic polynomial equation with coefficients in \( R \).

The Krull dimension of a ring \( A \), denoted \( \dim(A) \), is the supremum of lengths of chains of prime ideals.

Theorem and Proof

Theorem (Noether Normalization): Let \( k \) be a field and \( A \) a finitely generated \( k \)-algebra. Then there exist algebraically independent elements \( y_1, \ldots, y_d \in A \) such that \( A \) is integral over \( k[y_1, \ldots, y_d] \). The integer \( d \) equals \( \dim(A) \).

Proof Sketch: We proceed by induction on the number of generators.

Base case: If \( A = k[x_1, \ldots, x_n] \) is a polynomial ring, take \( y_i = x_i \) and \( d = n \).

Inductive step: Suppose \( A = k[x_1, \ldots, x_n]/I \) with \( I \neq 0 \). Pick a nonzero polynomial \( f \in I \). The key is to make a change of variables so that \( f \) becomes monic in \( x_n \). Define new variables: \( x_i' = x_i - x_n^{m_i} \) for \( i = 1, \ldots, n-1 \), where the \( m_i \) are chosen sufficiently large. Then \( x_n \) is integral over \( k[x_1', \ldots, x_{n-1}'] \). By induction, this subring has a Noether normalization. Composing gives the result for \( A \).

Dimension: Since integral extensions preserve dimension, \( \dim(A) = \dim(k[y_1, \ldots, y_d]) = d \). ∎

Notation Guide

  • \( k[y_1, \ldots, y_d] \) — polynomial ring in \( d \) variables
  • \( A/R \) integral — every element of \( A \) satisfies a monic polynomial over \( R \)
  • \( \dim(A) \) — Krull dimension
  • \( \text{tr.deg}_k(A) \) — transcendence degree of fraction field

The Sturm-Liouville Theory

Tags: differential-equations spectral-theory eigenvalue-problems

Motivation

Sturm-Liouville theory provides a comprehensive framework for understanding second-order linear differential equations with boundary conditions. It generalizes the eigenvalue problems of linear algebra to infinite-dimensional function spaces, with applications in quantum mechanics (Schrödinger equation), heat conduction, vibrating strings, and orthogonal polynomials.

Definitions

A Sturm-Liouville problem on an interval \( [a, b] \) consists of: \[-\frac{d}{dx}\left(p(x)\frac{dy}{dx}\right) + q(x)y = \lambda w(x)y\] with boundary conditions at \( a \) and \( b \) that make the operator self-adjoint.

The Sturm-Liouville operator is \( L[y] = -\frac{d}{dx}(p(x)y') + q(x)y \).

Theorem and Proof

Theorem (Sturm-Liouville Spectral Theorem): For a regular Sturm-Liouville problem on \( [a, b] \):

  1. The eigenvalues \( \lambda_1 < \lambda_2 < \cdots \) form an increasing sequence with \( \lambda_n \to \infty \).
  2. Eigenfunctions \( \phi_n \) corresponding to distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal with respect to the weight \( w \).
  3. The eigenfunctions form a complete orthogonal set in \( L^2([a,b], w\,dx) \).
  4. The \( n \)-th eigenfunction \( \phi_n \) has exactly \( n-1 \) zeros in \( (a, b) \).

Proof Sketch of Orthogonality: Let \( \phi_m, \phi_n \) be eigenfunctions with eigenvalues \( \lambda_m \neq \lambda_n \).

Consider the Lagrange identity: \[\phi_n L[\phi_m] - \phi_m L[\phi_n] = \frac{d}{dx}\left[p(x)(\phi_n\phi_m' - \phi_m\phi_n')\right]\]

Since \( L[\phi_m] = \lambda_m w \phi_m \) and \( L[\phi_n] = \lambda_n w \phi_n \), the left side equals \((\lambda_m - \lambda_n)w\phi_m\phi_n\).

Integrating from \( a \) to \( b \): \[(\lambda_m - \lambda_n)\int_a^b \phi_m\phi_n w\,dx = \left[p(x)(\phi_n\phi_m' - \phi_m\phi_n')\right]_a^b\]

The boundary conditions ensure the right side vanishes. Thus: \[(\lambda_m - \lambda_n)\int_a^b \phi_m\phi_n w\,dx = 0\]

Since \( \lambda_m \neq \lambda_n \), we have orthogonality. ∎

Notation Guide

  • \( L[y] \) — Sturm-Liouville differential operator
  • \( \lambda_n \) — \( n \)-th eigenvalue
  • \( \phi_n(x) \) — \( n \)-th eigenfunction
  • \( w(x) \) — weight function
  • \( \langle f, g \rangle_w = \int_a^b f(x)g(x)w(x)dx \) — weighted inner product

The PageRank Algorithm and Random Walks on Graphs

Tags: graph-algorithms random-walks network-analysis

Real-World Motivation

When Google launched in 1998, the web contained millions of pages with no clear organization. Traditional search engines ranked results by counting keyword occurrences, making them vulnerable to manipulation. Larry Page and Sergey Brin's breakthrough was recognizing that the link structure of the web itself contains valuable information: a page is important if important pages link to it. This recursive definition led to the PageRank algorithm, which revolutionized web search.

Definitions

A directed graph \( G = (V, E) \) consists of vertices \( V \) (web pages) and edges \( E \subseteq V \times V \) (hyperlinks).

The adjacency matrix \( A \) has entries \( A_{ij} = 1 \) if there is a link from page \( j \) to page \( i \).

The transition matrix \( P \) is defined by \( P_{ij} = A_{ij}/d_j \) where \( d_j \) is the out-degree of page \( j \).

The Google matrix is \( G = \alpha P + (1-\alpha)\frac{1}{n}J \) where \( \alpha \approx 0.85 \) is the damping factor.

Theorem and Proof

Theorem (PageRank Convergence): The Google matrix \( G \) is stochastic, irreducible, and aperiodic. Therefore, it has a unique stationary distribution \( \pi \) satisfying \( G\pi = \pi \), and for any initial distribution \( \pi_0 \), we have \( G^k \pi_0 \to \pi \) as \( k \to \infty \).

Proof:

Stochastic: Each column of \( P \) sums to 1. Each column of \( J/n \) sums to 1. Therefore each column of \( G \) sums to \( \alpha \cdot 1 + (1-\alpha) \cdot 1 = 1 \).

Irreducible: The term \( (1-\alpha)J/n \) adds positive probability to every entry of \( G \), making the underlying graph strongly connected.

Aperiodic: Since all entries of \( G \) are positive, the chain can return to any state in one step, so the period is 1.

Existence and Uniqueness: By the Perron-Frobenius theorem, a stochastic matrix with all positive entries has a unique stationary distribution. ∎

Notation Guide

  • \( n = |V| \) — number of web pages
  • \( A \) — adjacency matrix
  • \( d_j \) — out-degree of page \( j \)
  • \( P \) — transition matrix
  • \( \alpha \) — damping factor
  • \( \pi \) — PageRank vector

Python Code

import numpy as np
import networkx as nx
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def pagerank_power_iteration(adj_matrix, alpha=0.85, tol=1e-10, max_iter=1000):
    """Compute PageRank using power iteration."""
    n = adj_matrix.shape[0]
    
    # Build transition matrix P
    out_degrees = np.sum(adj_matrix, axis=0)
    dangling = (out_degrees == 0)
    out_degrees[dangling] = 1
    P = adj_matrix / out_degrees
    P[:, dangling] = 1.0 / n
    
    # Initialize and iterate
    pi = np.ones(n) / n
    for iteration in range(max_iter):
        pi_new = alpha * P @ pi + (1 - alpha) / n
        if np.linalg.norm(pi_new - pi, 1) < tol:
            return pi_new, iteration + 1
        pi = pi_new
    return pi, max_iter

# Create sample web graph
edges = [
    (0, 1), (0, 2), (0, 4),
    (1, 0), (1, 3),
    (2, 0), (2, 3), (2, 5),
    (3, 0),
    (4, 0), (4, 4), (4, 5),
    (5, 2), (5, 4),
]
n = 6
A = np.zeros((n, n))
for i, j in edges:
    A[i, j] = 1

page_names = ['Home', 'About', 'Products', 'Contact', 'Blog', 'FAQ']

print("=== PageRank Algorithm Demo ===")

print("\nPageRank scores:")

    print(f"  {name:12s}: {pr:.4f}")

print(f"\nSum: {np.sum(pagerank):.6f} (should be 1)")

Support Vector Machines and Kernel Methods

Tags: statistical-learning optimization kernel-methods

Real-World Motivation

Classification problems abound in modern applications: spam detection, medical diagnosis, image recognition, credit scoring. Support Vector Machines (SVMs), introduced by Vapnik and Chervonenkis, address this by maximizing the margin—the distance from the decision boundary to the nearest data points. This maximum-margin principle, combined with the kernel trick for handling non-linear boundaries, made SVMs the dominant classification method before the deep learning revolution.

Definitions

Given training data \( \{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^n \) with \( y_i \in \{-1, +1\} \), a linear classifier has the form \( f(x) = \text{sign}(w^T x + b) \).

The margin is \( \gamma = \min_i \frac{y_i(w^T x_i + b)}{\|w\|} \).

A kernel is a function \( K: \mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{X} \to \mathbb{R} \) that is symmetric and positive semi-definite.

Theorem and Proof

Theorem (Hard-Margin SVM): For linearly separable data, the maximum-margin classifier is given by: \[\min_{w,b} \frac{1}{2}\|w\|^2 \quad \text{s.t.} \quad y_i(w^T x_i + b) \geq 1 \text{ for all } i\] The optimal hyperplane has margin \( \gamma = 1/\|w^*\| \).

Proof: The distance from point \( x_i \) to the hyperplane is \( |w^T x_i + b|/\|w\| \). For correct classification, the distance is \( y_i(w^T x_i + b)/\|w\| \).

The margin is \( \gamma = \min_i y_i(w^T x_i + b)/\|w\| \). Maximizing \( \gamma \) subject to \( y_i(w^T x_i + b) \geq \gamma\|w\| \).

Since scaling doesn't change the hyperplane, impose \( \gamma\|w\| = 1 \). Maximizing \( \gamma = 1/\|w\| \) is equivalent to minimizing \( \frac{1}{2}\|w\|^2 \) subject to \( y_i(w^T x_i + b) \geq 1 \). ∎

Notation Guide

  • \( w \in \mathbb{R}^d \) — weight vector
  • \( b \in \mathbb{R} \) — bias term
  • \( \gamma \) — margin
  • \( \alpha_i \) — Lagrange multipliers
  • \( K(x, x') \) — kernel function

Python Code

import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import minimize
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from sklearn import datasets

def linear_svm_dual(X, y, C=1e6):
    """Solve linear SVM in dual form."""
    n = len(y)
    K = np.dot(X, X.T)
    
    def objective(alpha):
        return 0.5 * np.sum(alpha[:, None] * alpha[None, :] * 
                           y[:, None] * y[None, :] * K) - np.sum(alpha)
    
    constraints = {'type': 'eq', 'fun': lambda a: np.dot(a, y)}
    bounds = [(0, C) for _ in range(n)]
    
    result = minimize(objective, np.ones(n) * 0.1, 
                     method='SLSQP', bounds=bounds, constraints=constraints)
    
    alpha = result.x
    w = np.sum(alpha[:, None] * y[:, None] * X, axis=0)
    sv_mask = (alpha > 1e-5) & (alpha < C - 1e-5)
    b = np.mean(y[sv_mask] - np.dot(X[sv_mask], w)) if np.any(sv_mask) else 0
    
    return w, b, alpha

                                     n_informative=2, n_clusters_per_class=1,

y = 2 * y - 1

Zero-Knowledge Proofs and the Schnorr Protocol

Tags: cryptographic-protocols zero-knowledge discrete-logarithm

Real-World Motivation

How can you prove you know a secret without revealing it? This seemingly paradoxical question has profound practical applications: proving identity without exposing credentials, verifying computation without re-executing it, and maintaining privacy in blockchain transactions. Zero-knowledge proofs, first conceived by Goldwasser, Micali, and Rackoff in 1985, enable exactly this.

Definitions

A zero-knowledge proof is an interactive protocol where a prover \( P \) convinces a verifier \( V \) that a statement is true, with three properties:

  • Completeness: If the statement is true, \( V \) accepts.
  • Soundness: If the statement is false, no cheating prover can convince \( V \).
  • Zero-knowledge: \( V \) learns nothing beyond the truth of the statement.

The discrete logarithm problem: Given a cyclic group \( G = \langle g \rangle \) of prime order \( q \), and \( h = g^x \), find \( x \).

Theorem and Proof

Theorem (Schnorr Protocol is Honest-Verifier Zero-Knowledge): The Schnorr protocol for proving knowledge of \( x \) such that \( h = g^x \) is a zero-knowledge proof of knowledge with soundness error \( 1/q \).

Protocol:

  1. Prover chooses random \( r \in \mathbb{Z}_q \), computes \( t = g^r \), sends \( t \) to verifier.
  2. Verifier chooses random challenge \( c \in \mathbb{Z}_q \), sends \( c \) to prover.
  3. Prover computes \( s = r + cx \pmod{q} \), sends \( s \) to verifier.
  4. Verifier accepts if \( g^s = t \cdot h^c \).

Completeness: If prover knows \( x \), then \( g^s = g^{r+cx} = g^r \cdot (g^x)^c = t \cdot h^c \). ✓

Proof of Knowledge: If prover answers two challenges \( c_1, c_2 \) with \( s_1, s_2 \) for the same \( t \), then: \[g^{s_1-s_2} = h^{c_1-c_2} = g^{x(c_1-c_2)}\] So \( x = (s_1-s_2)(c_1-c_2)^{-1} \pmod{q} \).

Zero-Knowledge: The simulator, given \( h \) and \( c \), picks random \( s \), sets \( t = g^s \cdot h^{-c} \). The distribution is identical to the real protocol. ∎

Notation Guide

  • \( G \) — cyclic group of prime order \( q \)
  • \( g \) — generator
  • \( x \in \mathbb{Z}_q \) — secret (discrete logarithm)
  • \( h = g^x \) — public value
  • \( r \) — prover's random nonce
  • \( t = g^r \) — commitment
  • \( c \) — challenge
  • \( s = r + cx \) — response

Python Code

import random
import hashlib

Q = 0xFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFFEBAAEDCE6AF48A03BBFD25E8CD0364141
G = 2

def mod_exp(base, exp, mod):
    return pow(base, exp, mod)

def mod_inv(a, m):
    def extended_gcd(a, b):
        if a == 0:
            return b, 0, 1
        gcd, x1, y1 = extended_gcd(b % a, a)
        x = y1 - (b // a) * x1
        return gcd, x, 0
    _, x, _ = extended_gcd(a % m, m)
    return (x % m + m) % m

class SchnorrProof:
    def __init__(self, t, s):
        self.t = t
        self.s = s
    
    def verify(self, h, g, q):
        c = self._hash_challenge(g, h, self.t, q)
        left = mod_exp(g, self.s, q)
        right = (self.t * mod_exp(h, c, q)) % q
        return left == right
    
    @staticmethod
    def _hash_challenge(g, h, t, q):
        data = f"{g},{h},{t}".encode()
        return int(hashlib.sha256(data).hexdigest(), 16) % q

def schnorr_prove(x, h, g, q):
    r = random.randint(1, q - 1)
    t = mod_exp(g, r, q)
    c = SchnorrProof._hash_challenge(g, h, t, q)
    s = (r + c * x) % q
    return SchnorrProof(t, s)

print(f"Public value: h = g^x = ...{h % 10000}")

Algebraic Surfaces: The Enriques-Kodaira Classification

Tags: algebraic-surfaces birational-geometry classification

Historical Context

The classification of algebraic surfaces represents one of the crowning achievements of the Italian school of algebraic geometry in the early 20th century. Guided by Federigo Enriques and Guido Castelnuovo, mathematicians sought to extend Riemann's classification of curves to higher dimensions.

Definitions

An algebraic surface is a two-dimensional algebraic variety over \( \mathbb{C} \).

The geometric genus is \( p_g = \dim H^0(S, K_S) \).

The irregularity is \( q = \dim H^1(S, \mathcal{O}_S) \).

The Kodaira dimension \( \kappa(S) \in \{-\infty, 0, 1, 2\} \) measures the growth rate of pluricanonical maps.

Theorem and Proof

Theorem (Enriques-Kodaira Classification): Minimal algebraic surfaces over \( \mathbb{C} \) are classified by their Kodaira dimension:

  • \( \kappa = -\infty \): Rational or ruled surfaces
  • \( \kappa = 0 \): K3, Enriques, abelian, or bielliptic surfaces
  • \( \kappa = 1 \): Elliptic surfaces with \( K^2 = 0 \)
  • \( \kappa = 2 \): Surfaces of general type with \( K^2 > 0 \)

Proof Outline:

Case \( \kappa = -\infty \): If \( h^0(nK) = 0 \) for all \( n > 0 \), by the Castelnuovo criterion, \( S \) is birational to \( \mathbb{P}^2 \) or a ruled surface.

Case \( \kappa = 0 \): If \( h^0(nK) \) is bounded: K3 (\( q=0, p_g=1 \)), Enriques (\( q=0, p_g=0 \)), Abelian (\( q=2, p_g=1 \)), or Bielliptic.

Case \( \kappa = 1 \): The Iitaka fibration gives a map to a curve with elliptic fibers. The canonical bundle formula gives \( K^2 = 0 \).

Case \( \kappa = 2 \): For surfaces of general type, pluricanonical maps are birational. The BMY inequality gives \( K^2 \leq 9\chi \). ∎

Notation Guide

  • \( p_g \) — geometric genus
  • \( q \) — irregularity
  • \( \chi = 1 - q + p_g \) — holomorphic Euler characteristic
  • \( K_S \) — canonical divisor
  • \( K^2 \) — self-intersection of canonical divisor
  • \( \kappa \) — Kodaira dimension

Topological K-Theory and Bott Periodicity

Tags: topological-k-theory bott-periodicity vector-bundles

Historical Context

Topological K-theory emerged from the work of Alexander Grothendieck in algebraic geometry. In 1959, Michael Atiyah and Friedrich Hirzebruch adapted this construction to topology. The crowning achievement came with Raoul Bott's discovery of periodicity in the homotopy groups of classical groups.

Definitions

A complex vector bundle over a topological space \( X \) is a map \( \pi: E \to X \) where each fiber \( E_x \) is a complex vector space, locally trivial.

The Grothendieck group \( K(X) \) is constructed from the semiring of isomorphism classes of vector bundles.

Theorem and Proof

Theorem (Bott Periodicity): For any compact Hausdorff space \( X \): \[\tilde{K}(X) \cong \tilde{K}(S^2 \wedge X)\] Complex K-theory is a 2-periodic generalized cohomology theory.

Proof Sketch: Let \( H \) be the tautological line bundle over \( S^2 = \mathbb{C}P^1 \). External tensor product with \( H - 1 \in \tilde{K}(S^2) \) gives a map \( \beta: \tilde{K}(X) \to \tilde{K}(S^2 \wedge X) \).

The clutching construction identifies vector bundles over \( S^2 \wedge X \) with families of clutching functions \( f: S^1 \to GL_n(\mathbb{C}) \). Using that \( GL_n(\mathbb{C}) \) retracts onto \( U(n) \), and \( \pi_1(U(n)) \cong \mathbb{Z} \), every clutching function is homotopic to \( z \mapsto z^k \cdot I \).

The Bott element \( \beta = [H] - 1 \) generates \( \tilde{K}(S^2) \cong \mathbb{Z} \). The external product with \( \beta \) induces the periodicity isomorphism. ∎

Notation Guide

  • \( K(X) \) — complex K-theory
  • \( \tilde{K}(X) \) — reduced K-theory
  • \( [E] \) — class of vector bundle in K-theory
  • \( H \) — Hopf line bundle over \( \mathbb{C}P^1 \)
  • \( \beta = [H] - 1 \) — Bott element

Matroid Theory and the Greedy Algorithm

Tags: combinatorial-optimization matroids greedy-algorithms

Historical Context

Matroid theory was introduced by Hassler Whitney in 1935 as a simultaneous generalization of linear independence in vector spaces and forests in graphs. The characterization of matroids as exactly those structures where the greedy algorithm works optimally—discovered by Jack Edmonds in 1971—established matroid theory as fundamental to combinatorial optimization.

Definitions

A matroid \( M = (E, \mathcal{I}) \) consists of a finite ground set \( E \) and a collection \( \mathcal{I} \subseteq 2^E \) of independent sets satisfying:

  1. \( \emptyset \in \mathcal{I} \)
  2. If \( A \in \mathcal{I} \) and \( B \subseteq A \), then \( B \in \mathcal{I} \) (hereditary)
  3. If \( A, B \in \mathcal{I} \) and \( |A| < |B| \), then \( \exists e \in B \setminus A \) with \( A \cup \{e\} \in \mathcal{I} \) (exchange)

A basis is a maximal independent set. All bases have the same cardinality, called the rank.

Theorem and Proof

Theorem (Matroid-Greedy Equivalence): Let \( (E, \mathcal{I}) \) be a hereditary system. The following are equivalent:

  1. \( (E, \mathcal{I}) \) is a matroid.
  2. For every weight function \( w: E \to \mathbb{R} \), the greedy algorithm produces a maximum-weight basis.

(1) ⇒ (2): Let \( B = \{e_1, \ldots, e_r\} \) be the greedy basis, ordered by decreasing weight. Let \( B^* \) be an optimal basis. Suppose \( w(B) < w(B^*) \) and let \( k \) be the first index where \( w(e_k) < w(f_k) \).

Consider \( A = \{e_1, \ldots, e_{k-1}\} \) and \( C = \{f_1, \ldots, f_k\} \). By the exchange property, there exists \( f_j \in C \setminus A \) with \( A \cup \{f_j\} \in \mathcal{I} \). Since \( w(f_j) \geq w(f_k) > w(e_k) \), the greedy algorithm would have chosen \( f_j \) before \( e_k \), contradiction.

(2) ⇒ (1): Suppose the exchange property fails. Define weights so that greedy picks \( A \) but cannot add elements from \( B \setminus A \), yielding a suboptimal solution, contradiction. ∎

Notation Guide

  • \( E \) — ground set
  • \( \mathcal{I} \) — independent sets
  • \( r(A) \) — rank of set \( A \)
  • \( \mathcal{B} \) — bases
  • \( \mathcal{C} \) — circuits (minimal dependent sets)

Python Code

class Matroid:
    """Abstract matroid defined by its independent sets."""
    def __init__(self, ground_set, independent_sets):
        self.ground_set = ground_set
        self.independent_sets = independent_sets
    
    def is_independent(self, subset):
        return frozenset(subset) in self.independent_sets
    
    def greedy_max_weight_basis(self, weights):
        """Find maximum weight basis using greedy algorithm."""
        sorted_elements = sorted(self.ground_set, key=lambda e: -weights[e])
        basis = set()
        for e in sorted_elements:
            trial = basis | {e}
            if self.is_independent(trial):
                basis = trial
        return basis

def uniform_matroid(n, r):
    """Create uniform matroid U_{n,r}."""
    from itertools import combinations
    ground_set = set(range(n))
    independent_sets = set()
    for size in range(r + 1):
        for subset in combinations(range(n), size):
            independent_sets.add(frozenset(subset))
    return Matroid(ground_set, independent_sets)

Georg Cantor and the Infinite

Tags: cantor set-theory history-of-mathematics

Historical Context

Georg Cantor (1845–1918) revolutionized mathematics by creating set theory and establishing a rigorous foundation for the study of infinity. Before Cantor, mathematicians generally avoided the infinite or treated it as a potential rather than actual concept. His work met fierce resistance from Leopold Kronecker, who declared "God made the integers; all else is the work of man."

Definitions

Two sets have the same cardinality (written \( |A| = |B| \)) if there exists a bijection between them.

A set is countable if it has the same cardinality as \( \mathbb{N} \). Otherwise, it is uncountable.

The power set \( \mathcal{P}(A) \) is the set of all subsets of \( A \).

Theorem and Proof

Theorem (Cantor's Diagonal Argument): The real numbers are uncountable. For any set \( A \), there is no surjection from \( A \) onto \( \mathcal{P}(A) \). Hence \( |A| < |\mathcal{P}(A)| \).

Proof: Suppose \( f: A \to \mathcal{P}(A) \) is any function. Define \( D = \{x \in A : x \notin f(x)\} \).

We claim \( D \) is not in the image of \( f \). Suppose \( D = f(y) \) for some \( y \in A \).

If \( y \in D \), then \( y \notin f(y) = D \), contradiction.

If \( y \notin D \), then \( y \in f(y) = D \), contradiction.

Therefore \( D \neq f(y) \) for any \( y \), so \( f \) is not surjective. ∎

Notation Guide

  • \( \aleph_0 = |\mathbb{N}| \) — cardinality of natural numbers
  • \( \mathfrak{c} = |\mathbb{R}| = 2^{\aleph_0} \) — cardinality of continuum
  • \( \mathcal{P}(A) \) — power set of \( A \)
  • CH asserts: \( 2^{\aleph_0} = \aleph_1 \)

📜 Math History: The Development of Abstract Algebra

The emergence of abstract algebra in the late 19th and early 20th centuries represents one of the most profound shifts in mathematical thinking. Before this transformation, algebra was primarily concerned with solving equations and manipulating symbolic expressions. The modern conception of algebra as the study of algebraic structures—groups, rings, fields, and modules—emerged gradually through the work of several visionary mathematicians.

The story begins with Évariste Galois (1811–1832), who, in his short life, revolutionized the theory of equations. Galois discovered that the solvability of polynomial equations by radicals depends not on the coefficients themselves but on the structure of a group associated with the equation—its Galois group. Though his work was nearly lost and only published posthumously, it planted the seed of the group concept.

The term "group" was coined by Galois, but the concept was developed systematically by Arthur Cayley and Augustin-Louis Cauchy. Cayley, in 1854, gave the first abstract definition of a group. Meanwhile, the study of algebraic integers by Ernst Kummer, Richard Dedekind, and Leopold Kronecker led to the concept of ideals.

Dedekind's work was particularly influential. His 1871 supplement to Dirichlet's lectures introduced the modern definition of a field, the concept of modules, and the lattice structure of ideals.

The unification of these ideas came through the work of Emmy Noether (1882–1935), often called the "mother of modern algebra." Noether's 1921 paper "Idealtheorie in Ringbereichen" established the general theory of ideals in commutative rings. Her approach was radically abstract: "numbers, functions, operations do not matter; only relations matter."

Noether's influence extended through her students and collaborators, including Bartel van der Waerden, whose 1930 textbook "Moderne Algebra" codified the abstract approach and became the standard reference for generations of mathematicians.

IMO 2023 Problem 2

Let \( \mathbb{R}^+ \) denote the set of positive real numbers. Find all functions \( f: \mathbb{R}^+ \to \mathbb{R}^+ \) such that for all \( x, y \in \mathbb{R}^+ \): \[f(xy + f(x)) = xf(y) + 2\]

Hint

First, try to find \( f(1) \) by making a clever substitution. Then investigate what happens when you set \( y = 1 \). Look for a pattern that might suggest \( f \) is linear.

Solution

Step 1: Let \( P(x, y) \) denote the assertion \( f(xy + f(x)) = xf(y) + 2 \).

Step 2: We claim \( f \) is injective. Suppose \( f(a) = f(b) \). Then comparing \( P(a, y) \) and \( P(b, y) \): \[af(y) + 2 = f(ay + f(a)) = f(by + f(b)) = bf(y) + 2\] This gives \( af(y) = bf(y) \) for all \( y \), so \( a = b \).

Step 3: Let \( f(1) = c \). From \( P(1, y) \): \( f(y + c) = f(y) + 2 \).

Step 4: From \( P(x, 1) \): \( f(x + f(x)) = cx + 2 \).

Step 5: We guess \( f(x) = ax + b \). Substituting and comparing coefficients: \( a^2 = b \) and \( ab + b = 2 \). This gives \( a = 1 \), \( b = 1 \).

Step 6: Verify \( f(x) = x + 1 \): LHS = \( f(xy + x + 1) = xy + x + 2 \), RHS = \( x(y + 1) + 2 = xy + x + 2 \). ✓

Answer: The unique solution is \( f(x) = x + 1 \). ∎

🎨 Visualization of the Day: The Mandelbrot Set

The Mandelbrot set is one of the most famous examples of a fractal—a mathematical object that exhibits infinite complexity at every scale. It is defined as the set of complex numbers \( c \) for which the iteration \( z_{n+1} = z_n^2 + c \) (starting with \( z_0 = 0 \)) remains bounded.

Mathematically, the Mandelbrot set is: \[\mathcal{M} = \{c \in \mathbb{C} : \sup_{n \geq 0} |z_n| < \infty\}\] where \( z_{n+1} = z_n^2 + c \) and \( z_0 = 0 \).

The boundary of the Mandelbrot set is infinitely complex—zooming in reveals ever more intricate patterns, including miniature copies of the entire set. The set is connected (proved by Douady and Hubbard) and its boundary has Hausdorff dimension 2.

Python Code

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def mandelbrot_set(width=800, height=600, max_iter=100):
    """Generate the Mandelbrot set image."""
    x = np.linspace(-2.5, 1.5, width)
    y = np.linspace(-1.5, 1.5, height)
    X, Y = np.meshgrid(x, y)
    C = X + 1j*Y
    
    Z = np.zeros_like(C)
    M = np.zeros(C.shape, dtype=int)
    
    for i in range(max_iter):
        mask = np.abs(Z) <= 2
        Z[mask] = Z[mask]**2 + C[mask]
        M[mask] = i
    
    return M

print("Generating Mandelbrot set...")
M = mandelbrot_set(width=800, height=600, max_iter=100)

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(12, 9))
im = ax.imshow(M, extent=[-2.5, 1.5, -1.5, 1.5], 
               cmap='hot', origin='lower', interpolation='bilinear')
ax.set_xlabel('Re(c)', fontsize=12)
ax.set_ylabel('Im(c)', fontsize=12)
ax.set_title('The Mandelbrot Set', fontsize=14)
plt.colorbar(im, ax=ax, label='Iterations to escape')
plt.tight_layout()
plt.savefig('mandelbrot.png', dpi=150)
plt.close()

print("Visualization saved to mandelbrot.png")
