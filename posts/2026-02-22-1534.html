Symplectic Geometry: The Geometry of Classical Mechanics

symplectic-geometry

Motivation

Symplectic geometry emerged from the study of classical mechanics, where Hamilton's formulation revealed that phase space carries a natural geometric structure. Unlike Riemannian geometry, which measures distances and angles, symplectic geometry preserves areas and volumes. This makes it the natural language for Hamiltonian mechanics, and it has become essential in modern mathematical physics, from gauge theory to string theory. The field connects deeply with algebraic geometry, topology, and dynamical systems.

Definitions

  • Symplectic Form: A closed, non-degenerate 2-form \( \omega \) on a manifold \( M \). Non-degenerate means \( \omega(v, w) = 0 \) for all \( w \) implies \( v = 0 \).
  • Symplectic Manifold: A pair \( (M, \omega) \) where \( M \) is a smooth manifold and \( \omega \) is a symplectic form. The dimension must be even, say \( 2n \).
  • Hamiltonian Vector Field: For a function \( H: M \to \mathbb{R} \), the Hamiltonian vector field \( X_H \) is defined by \( \omega(X_H, \cdot) = dH \).
  • Poisson Bracket: For functions \( f, g \) on a symplectic manifold: \[\{f, g\} = \omega(X_f, X_g) = \sum_{i=1}^n \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial q_i}\frac{\partial g}{\partial p_i} - \frac{\partial f}{\partial p_i}\frac{\partial g}{\partial q_i}\right)\]
  • Lagrangian Submanifold: A submanifold \( L \subset M \) of dimension \( n \) such that \( \omega|_L = 0 \).

Theorem: Darboux's Theorem

Theorem (Darboux): Let \( (M, \omega) \) be a symplectic manifold of dimension \( 2n \). For any point \( p \in M \), there exist local coordinates \( (q_1, \ldots, q_n, p_1, \ldots, p_n) \) around \( p \) such that:

\[\omega = \sum_{i=1}^n dq_i \wedge dp_i\]

In these coordinates, the symplectic form has the standard form everywhere in the neighborhood.

Proof Sketch:

Step 1 (Linear Algebra): At any point, by the non-degeneracy of \( \omega \), we can find a basis where \( \omega \) has the standard form \( \sum dq_i \wedge dp_i \).

Step 2 (Moser's Trick): Consider a family of symplectic forms \( \omega_t = (1-t)\omega_0 + t\omega_1 \) where \( \omega_0 \) is the standard form and \( \omega_1 = \omega \). We seek a flow \( \phi_t \) with \( \phi_t^*\omega_t = \omega_0 \).

Step 3 (Poincaré Lemma): Since \( d\omega_t = 0 \) and \( \omega_1 - \omega_0 = d\alpha \) locally (by Poincaré lemma), we solve for a vector field \( X_t \) satisfying: \[\iota_{X_t}\omega_t = -\alpha\]

Step 4 (Integration): Integrating \( X_t \) gives the desired diffeomorphism, showing all symplectic structures are locally equivalent. ∎

Notation Guide

  • \( (M, \omega) \): Symplectic manifold with symplectic form \( \omega \)
  • \( \omega^n = \omega \wedge \cdots \wedge \omega \) (n times): Liouville volume form
  • \( X_H \): Hamiltonian vector field for Hamiltonian function \( H \)
  • \( \{f, g\} \): Poisson bracket of functions \( f \) and \( g \)
  • \( \iota_X \omega \): Interior product (contraction) of \( \omega \) with \( X \)
  • \( Sp(2n, \mathbb{R}) \): Symplectic group, linear transformations preserving \( \omega \)

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy.integrate import odeint

class SymplecticSystem:
    """
    Simulate Hamiltonian systems preserving symplectic structure.
    """
    
    def __init__(self, H, dH_dq, dH_dp):
        """
        H: Hamiltonian function H(q, p)
        dH_dq: partial derivative of H with respect to q
        dH_dp: partial derivative of H with respect to p
        """
        self.H = H
        self.dH_dq = dH_dq
        self.dH_dp = dH_dp
    
    def hamiltonian_vector_field(self, state, t):
        """
        Compute (dq/dt, dp/dt) from Hamilton's equations:
        dq/dt = dH/dp, dp/dt = -dH/dq
        """
        q, p = state
        dq_dt = self.dH_dp(q, p)
        dp_dt = -self.dH_dq(q, p)
        return [dq_dt, dp_dt]
    
    def symplectic_euler(self, q0, p0, dt, n_steps):
        """
        Symplectic Euler method: preserves phase space volume.
        """
        q, p = np.zeros(n_steps + 1), np.zeros(n_steps + 1)
        q[0], p[0] = q0, p0
        
        for i in range(n_steps):
            # Update p first, then q
            p[i+1] = p[i] - dt * self.dH_dq(q[i], p[i])
            q[i+1] = q[i] + dt * self.dH_dp(q[i], p[i+1])
        
        return q, p
    
    def leapfrog(self, q0, p0, dt, n_steps):
        """
        Leapfrog (Verlet) integrator: second-order symplectic.
        """
        q, p = np.zeros(n_steps + 1), np.zeros(n_steps + 1)
        q[0], p[0] = q0, p0
        
        # Half step for momentum
        p_half = p[0] - 0.5 * dt * self.dH_dq(q[0], p[0])
        
        for i in range(n_steps):
            q[i+1] = q[i] + dt * self.dH_dp(q[i], p_half)
            # Full step for momentum (except last)
            if i < n_steps - 1:
                p_half = p_half - dt * self.dH_dq(q[i+1], p_half)
        
        # Final half step
        p[-1] = p_half - 0.5 * dt * self.dH_dq(q[-1], p_half)
        
        return q, p

Algebraic Combinatorics: When Algebra Meets Counting

algebraic-combinatorics

Motivation

Algebraic combinatorics uses techniques from algebra, representation theory, and algebraic geometry to solve combinatorial problems. This field has revealed deep connections between seemingly unrelated areas: the enumeration of standard Young tableaux connects to representation theory of the symmetric group; the combinatorics of posets relates to algebraic topology; and generating functions encode structural information about combinatorial classes. The field has applications in statistical physics, computer science, and algebraic geometry.

Definitions

  • Young Diagram: A collection of boxes arranged in left-justified rows with non-increasing row lengths. A partition \( \lambda = (\lambda_1 \geq \lambda_2 \geq \cdots \geq \lambda_k) \) corresponds to a Young diagram with \( \lambda_i \) boxes in row \( i \).
  • Standard Young Tableau (SYT): A filling of a Young diagram with numbers \( 1, 2, \ldots, n \) (where \( n \) is the number of boxes) such that entries increase along rows and columns.
  • Hook Length: For a box in position \( (i, j) \), the hook length is \( h(i,j) = \lambda_i - j + \lambda'_j - i + 1 \), where \( \lambda' \) is the conjugate partition.
  • Schur Polynomial: For a partition \( \lambda \) with at most \( n \) parts: \[s_\lambda(x_1, \ldots, x_n) = \frac{\det(x_i^{\lambda_j + n - j})_{i,j=1}^n}{\det(x_i^{n-j})_{i,j=1}^n}\]

Theorem: The Hook Length Formula

Theorem (Hook Length Formula): The number of standard Young tableaux of shape \( \lambda \) (where \( \lambda \vdash n \)) is:

\[f^\lambda = \frac{n!}{\prod_{(i,j) \in \lambda} h(i,j)}\]

where the product is over all boxes of the Young diagram, and \( h(i,j) \) is the hook length of box \( (i,j) \).

Proof Sketch (Probabilistic):

Step 1 (Hook Walk): Consider a random process: start at a random box, then move to a random box in the same hook but farther from the corner. Continue until reaching a corner.

Step 2 (Corner Probability): The probability that this walk ends at a particular corner \( c \) is \( \prod_{x \in R_c} \frac{1}{h(x)} \) where \( R_c \) is the rim hook to corner \( c \).

Step 3 (Induction): By induction on \( n \), using the branching rule \( f^\lambda = \sum_{\mu \nearrow \lambda} f^\mu \) where the sum is over partitions \( \mu \) obtained by removing a corner from \( \lambda \).

Step 4 (Product Formula): The hook lengths telescope beautifully, yielding the product formula. For the partition \( (n) \) (one row), all hook lengths are \( n, n-1, \ldots, 1 \), giving \( f^{(n)} = 1 \) as expected. ∎

Notation Guide

  • \( \lambda \vdash n \): \( \lambda \) is a partition of \( n \)
  • \( f^\lambda \): Number of standard Young tableaux of shape \( \lambda \)
  • \( h(i,j) \): Hook length of box at position \( (i,j) \)
  • \( s_\lambda \): Schur polynomial indexed by \( \lambda \)
  • \( \lambda' \): Conjugate (transpose) partition
  • \( \mu \nearrow \lambda \): \( \mu \) is obtained from \( \lambda \) by removing a corner box

import numpy as np

def hook_lengths(partition):
    """
    Compute hook lengths for all boxes in a Young diagram.
    """
    hooks = []
    k = len(partition)
    
    for i, row_len in enumerate(partition):
        for j in range(row_len):
            # Arm length: boxes to the right
            arm = row_len - j - 1
            # Leg length: boxes below
            leg = sum(1 for p in partition[i+1:] if p > j)
            # Hook length
            hook = arm + leg + 1
            hooks.append(hook)
    
    return hooks

def hook_length_formula(partition):
    """
    Compute number of SYT using the hook length formula.
    """
    n = sum(partition)
    hooks = hook_lengths(partition)
    return factorial(n) // np.prod(hooks)

def generate_syt(partition):
    """
    Generate all standard Young tableaux of given shape (brute force).
    For small shapes only!
    """
    n = sum(partition)
    
    # Create grid
    grid = []
    for i, row_len in enumerate(partition):
        grid.append([None] * row_len)
    
    def is_valid_partial():
        """Check if current partial filling is valid."""
        # Check rows
        for row in grid:
            filled = [x for x in row if x is not None]
            if filled != sorted(filled):
                return False
        # Check columns
        max_col = max(partition)
        for j in range(max_col):
            col = []
            for i in range(len(grid)):
                if j < len(grid[i]) and grid[i][j] is not None:
                    col.append(grid[i][j])
            if col != sorted(col):
                return False
        return True
    
    def backtrack(k):
        """Try to place number k."""
        if k > n:
            # Make a copy of the solution
            return [[row[:] for row in grid]]
        
        solutions = []
        for i in range(len(grid)):
            for j in range(len(grid[i])):
                if grid[i][j] is None:
                    grid[i][j] = k
                    if is_valid_partial():
                        solutions.extend(backtrack(k + 1))
                    grid[i][j] = None
        return solutions
    
    return backtrack(1)

Homotopy Theory: Continuous Deformation

homotopy-theory

Motivation

Homotopy theory studies spaces up to continuous deformation, asking when one shape can be transformed into another without tearing or gluing. This perspective reveals deep algebraic structures: the fundamental group captures one-dimensional holes, higher homotopy groups capture higher-dimensional holes, and homotopy categories provide a framework for doing algebra with spaces. The field has profound applications in algebraic geometry (motivic homotopy theory), mathematical physics (quantum field theories), and computer science (homotopy type theory).

Definitions

  • Homotopy: Continuous maps \( f, g: X \to Y \) are homotopic (written \( f \simeq g \)) if there exists a continuous map \( H: X \times [0,1] \to Y \) with \( H(x, 0) = f(x) \) and \( H(x, 1) = g(x) \).
  • Homotopy Equivalence: Spaces \( X \) and \( Y \) are homotopy equivalent if there exist maps \( f: X \to Y \) and \( g: Y \to X \) with \( g \circ f \simeq \text{id}_X \) and \( f \circ g \simeq \text{id}_Y \).
  • Fundamental Group: \( \pi_1(X, x_0) \) is the set of homotopy classes of loops based at \( x_0 \), with group operation given by concatenation.
  • Higher Homotopy Groups: \( \pi_n(X, x_0) \) is the set of homotopy classes of maps \( (S^n, s_0) \to (X, x_0) \). For \( n \geq 2 \), these are abelian groups.
  • Contractible: A space homotopy equivalent to a point.

Theorem: The Seifert-van Kampen Theorem

Theorem (Seifert-van Kampen): Let \( X \) be a topological space that is the union of path-connected open sets \( U \) and \( V \) such that \( U \cap V \) is also path-connected. Let \( x_0 \in U \cap V \). Then:

\[\pi_1(X, x_0) \cong \pi_1(U, x_0) *_{\pi_1(U \cap V, x_0)} \pi_1(V, x_0)\]

where the right side is the amalgamated free product: the free product of \( \pi_1(U) \) and \( \pi_1(V) \) modulo the normal subgroup generated by relations \( i_U(\gamma) = i_V(\gamma) \) for \( \gamma \in \pi_1(U \cap V) \).

Proof Sketch:

Step 1 (Surjectivity): Any loop in \( X \) can be subdivided into segments each lying in \( U \) or \( V \). By path-connectedness of \( U \cap V \), we can connect basepoints within \( U \cap V \), expressing the loop as a product of loops in \( U \) and \( V \).

Step 2 (Relations): Two such expressions represent the same element if one can be obtained from the other by:

  • Inserting or deleting the identity
  • Replacing a loop in \( U \cap V \) viewed in \( U \) with the same loop viewed in \( V \)
  • Concatenating adjacent loops in the same space

Step 3 (Universal Property): The amalgamated product satisfies the universal property that any homomorphisms from \( \pi_1(U) \) and \( \pi_1(V) \) agreeing on \( \pi_1(U \cap V) \) extend uniquely to \( \pi_1(X) \). ∎

Notation Guide

  • \( \pi_n(X, x_0) \): n-th homotopy group of \( X \) at basepoint \( x_0 \)
  • \( f \simeq g \): f is homotopic to g
  • \( X \simeq Y \): X is homotopy equivalent to Y
  • \( [f] \): Homotopy class of map f
  • \( S^n \): n-dimensional sphere
  • \( * \): Free product of groups
  • \( \pi_1(U) *_{\pi_1(U \cap V)} \pi_1(V) \): Amalgamated free product

import numpy as np
from itertools import product

class FundamentalGroup:
    """
    Compute fundamental group presentations for simple cell complexes.
    Educational implementation - not for general use.
    """
    
    def __init__(self, vertices, edges, faces=None):
        """
        vertices: list of vertex labels
        edges: list of (v1, v2) tuples
        faces: list of (v1, v2, v3, ...) tuples (cycles)
        """
        self.vertices = vertices
        self.edges = edges
        self.faces = faces or []
    
    def edge_to_generator(self, edge):
        """Convert edge to generator name."""
        v1, v2 = sorted(edge)
        return f"a_{v1}{v2}"
    
    def compute_presentation(self):
        """
        Compute group presentation .
        Relations come from:
        1. Face boundaries
        2. Edges not in spanning tree (tree edges are trivial)
        """
        # Find spanning tree
        tree_edges = self._spanning_tree()
        
        # Generators correspond to edges not in tree
        generators = []
        for edge in self.edges:
            if edge not in tree_edges and tuple(reversed(edge)) not in tree_edges:
                generators.append(self.edge_to_generator(edge))
        
        # Relations from face boundaries
        relations = []
        for face in self.faces:
            # Trace boundary of face
            rel = self._face_relation(face, tree_edges)
            if rel:
                relations.append(rel)
        
        return {
            'generators': generators,
            'relations': relations,
            'tree_edges': [self.edge_to_generator(e) for e in tree_edges]
        }
    
    def _spanning_tree(self):
        """Find spanning tree using BFS."""
        if not self.vertices:
            return []
        
        visited = {self.vertices[0]}
        tree = []
        queue = [self.vertices[0]]
        
        while queue:
            v = queue.pop(0)
            for edge in self.edges:
                if v in edge:
                    other = edge[1] if edge[0] == v else edge[0]
                    if other not in visited:
                        visited.add(other)
                        tree.append(edge)
                        queue.append(other)
        
        return tree
    
    def _face_relation(self, face, tree_edges):
        """Convert face boundary to relation."""
        # Simplified: just return face as string
        return f"boundary_of_{face}"

Mathematical Epidemiology: Modeling Disease Spread

epidemiology

Real-World Motivation

Mathematical epidemiology provides the quantitative foundation for understanding and controlling infectious diseases. From the 1918 influenza pandemic to COVID-19, mathematical models have guided public health decisions about vaccination strategies, social distancing measures, and resource allocation. The field combines differential equations, probability theory, and network science to predict disease dynamics and evaluate interventions.

Definitions

  • Basic Reproduction Number \( R_0 \): The expected number of secondary cases produced by a single infected individual in a completely susceptible population. If \( R_0 > 1 \), an epidemic occurs; if \( R_0 < 1 \), the disease dies out.
  • SIR Model: Compartmental model with:
    • S(t): Susceptible individuals
    • I(t): Infected (infectious) individuals
    • R(t): Recovered (removed) individuals
  • Herd Immunity Threshold: The proportion of the population that must be immune to prevent sustained transmission: \( p_c = 1 - 1/R_0 \).
  • SEIR Model: Extension with Exposed (latent) compartment for diseases with incubation periods.

Theorem: Final Size Relation for SIR

Theorem (Final Size Relation): In the SIR model with initial conditions \( S(0) = S_0 \), \( I(0) = I_0 \), \( R(0) = 0 \), and total population \( N = S_0 + I_0 \), the final epidemic size \( R_\infty = \lim_{t \to \infty} R(t) \) satisfies:

\[R_\infty = N - S_0 \exp\left(-\frac{\beta}{\gamma} \frac{R_\infty}{N}\right)\]

where \( \beta \) is the transmission rate and \( \gamma \) is the recovery rate, so \( R_0 = \beta/\gamma \).

Proof:

Step 1 (Key Observation): From the SIR equations: \[\frac{dS}{dt} = -\frac{\beta}{N} SI, \quad \frac{dR}{dt} = \gamma I\]

Step 2 (Eliminate Time): Dividing: \[\frac{dS}{dR} = -\frac{\beta}{\gamma N} S = -\frac{R_0}{N} S\]

Step 3 (Integrate): Solving this ODE: \[S(R) = S_0 \exp\left(-\frac{R_0 R}{N}\right)\]

Step 4 (Final Size): As \( t \to \infty \), \( I(t) \to 0 \), so \( S_\infty + R_\infty = N \). Substituting: \[N - R_\infty = S_0 \exp\left(-\frac{R_0 R_\infty}{N}\right)\]

Rearranging gives the final size equation. ∎

Notation Guide

  • \( S(t), I(t), R(t) \): Number in each compartment at time t
  • \( N \): Total population size
  • \( \beta \): Transmission rate (contacts per unit time)
  • \( \gamma \): Recovery rate (1/mean infectious period)
  • \( R_0 = \beta/\gamma \): Basic reproduction number
  • \( s = S/N, i = I/N, r = R/N \): Proportions
  • \( R_e = R_0 \cdot s \): Effective reproduction number

        self.N = N
        self.R0 = beta / gamma
    
    def derivatives(self, state, t):
        """
        SIR differential equations.
        """
        S, I, R = state
        dS_dt = -self.beta * S * I / self.N
        dI_dt = self.beta * S * I / self.N - self.gamma * I
        dR_dt = self.gamma * I
        return [dS_dt, dI_dt, dR_dt]
    
    def simulate(self, S0, I0, R0, t_max, dt=0.1):
        """
        Simulate the SIR model.
        """
        t = np.arange(0, t_max, dt)
        state0 = [S0, I0, R0]
        states = odeint(self.derivatives, state0, t)
        return t, states
    
    def final_size(self, S0, I0):
        """
        Compute final epidemic size using the implicit equation.
        """
        from scipy.optimize import fsolve
        
        N = S0 + I0
        R0_init = 0
        
        def equation(R_inf):
            return R_inf - (N - S0 * np.exp(-self.R0 * R_inf / N))
        
        R_inf = fsolve(equation, N * 0.5)[0]
        return R_inf
    
    def herd_immunity_threshold(self):
        """
        Compute herd immunity threshold.
        """
        if self.R0 <= 1:
            return 0
        return 1 - 1 / self.R0
    
    def peak_infection_time(self, S0, I0):
        """
        Estimate time of peak infection.
        """
        t, states = self.simulate(S0, I0, 0, 100)
        I = states[:, 1]
        peak_idx = np.argmax(I)
        return t[peak_idx], I[peak_idx]

Computational Fluid Dynamics: Simulating Flow

computational-fluid-dynamics

Real-World Motivation

Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) simulates fluid flow using numerical methods, enabling the design of aircraft, weather prediction, optimization of medical devices, and analysis of ocean currents. The field combines the Navier-Stokes equations (which describe fluid motion) with sophisticated numerical techniques to solve problems that are analytically intractable. CFD has revolutionized engineering design, reducing the need for expensive physical prototypes.

Definitions

  • Navier-Stokes Equations: The fundamental equations of fluid motion for an incompressible, viscous fluid: \[\frac{\partial \mathbf{u}}{\partial t} + (\mathbf{u} \cdot \nabla)\mathbf{u} = -\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla p + \nu \nabla^2 \mathbf{u} + \mathbf{f}\] \[\nabla \cdot \mathbf{u} = 0\] where \( \mathbf{u} \) is velocity, \( p \) is pressure, \( \rho \) is density, \( \nu \) is kinematic viscosity, and \( \mathbf{f} \) is external force.
  • Reynolds Number: \( Re = \frac{UL}{\nu} \), where \( U \) is characteristic velocity and \( L \) is characteristic length. It measures the ratio of inertial to viscous forces.
  • Vorticity: \( \boldsymbol{\omega} = \nabla \times \mathbf{u} \), measuring local rotation of fluid elements.
  • Stream Function: For 2D incompressible flow, a scalar \( \psi \) such that \( u = \partial \psi/\partial y \), \( v = -\partial \psi/\partial x \).

Theorem: Energy Dissipation in Navier-Stokes

Theorem (Energy Dissipation): For a solution \( \mathbf{u} \) of the incompressible Navier-Stokes equations in a domain \( \Omega \) with no-slip boundary conditions \( \mathbf{u}|_{\partial \Omega} = 0 \), the kinetic energy \( E(t) = \frac{1}{2}\int_\Omega |\mathbf{u}|^2 \, d\mathbf{x} \) satisfies:

\[\frac{dE}{dt} = -\nu \int_\Omega |\nabla \mathbf{u}|^2 \, d\mathbf{x} + \int_\Omega \mathbf{f} \cdot \mathbf{u} \, d\mathbf{x}\]

For unforced flow (\( \mathbf{f} = 0 \)), the energy is non-increasing: \( \frac{dE}{dt} \leq 0 \).

Proof:

Step 1 (Multiply by u): Take the dot product of Navier-Stokes with \( \mathbf{u} \): \[\mathbf{u} \cdot \frac{\partial \mathbf{u}}{\partial t} + \mathbf{u} \cdot [(\mathbf{u} \cdot \nabla)\mathbf{u}] = -\frac{1}{\rho}\mathbf{u} \cdot \nabla p + \nu \mathbf{u} \cdot \nabla^2 \mathbf{u}\]

Step 2 (Simplify Terms): \[\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}|\mathbf{u}|^2 + \frac{1}{2}\mathbf{u} \cdot \nabla(|\mathbf{u}|^2) = -\frac{1}{\rho}\nabla \cdot (p\mathbf{u}) + \frac{p}{\rho}\nabla \cdot \mathbf{u} + \nu \mathbf{u} \cdot \nabla^2 \mathbf{u}\]

Step 3 (Use Incompressibility): Since \( \nabla \cdot \mathbf{u} = 0 \) and integrating by parts with boundary conditions:

\[\frac{dE}{dt} = -\nu \int_\Omega |\nabla \mathbf{u}|^2 \, d\mathbf{x}\]

The viscous term dissipates energy, converting kinetic energy to heat. ∎

Notation Guide

  • \( \mathbf{u} = (u, v, w) \): Velocity vector field
  • \( p \): Pressure field
  • \( \rho \): Fluid density
  • \( \nu = \mu/\rho \): Kinematic viscosity
  • \( \mu \): Dynamic viscosity
  • \( \nabla \cdot \mathbf{u} \): Divergence of velocity
  • \( \nabla^2 \): Laplacian operator
  • \( Re \): Reynolds number
  • \( \boldsymbol{\omega} \): Vorticity vector

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy.sparse import diags
from scipy.sparse.linalg import spsolve

class LidDrivenCavity:
    """
    2D lid-driven cavity flow using stream function-vorticity formulation.
    """
    
    def __init__(self, N, Re, dt=0.001):
        """
        N: grid points in each direction
        Re: Reynolds number
        dt: time step
        """
        self.N = N
        self.Re = Re
        self.dt = dt
        
        # Grid
        self.x = np.linspace(0, 1, N)
        self.y = np.linspace(0, 1, N)
        self.dx = 1.0 / (N - 1)
        
        # Initialize
        self.psi = np.zeros((N, N))  # Stream function
        self.omega = np.zeros((N, N))  # Vorticity
    
    def solve_poisson(self, rhs):
        """
        Solve ∇²ψ = -ω using finite differences.
        """
        N = self.N
        dx = self.dx
        
        # Flatten for linear solve
        b = -rhs.flatten()
        
        # Build Laplacian matrix (5-point stencil)
        n_unknowns = N * N
        main_diag = -4 * np.ones(n_unknowns)
        off_diag = np.ones(n_unknowns - 1)
        off_diag[N-1::N] = 0  # Boundary between rows
        
        # Create sparse matrix
        diagonals = [main_diag, off_diag, off_diag, np.ones(n_unknowns-N), np.ones(n_unknowns-N)]
        offsets = [0, 1, -1, N, -N]
        A = diags(diagonals, offsets, format='csr') / (dx**2)
        
        # Boundary conditions: ψ = 0 on all walls
        # (Already satisfied by zero initialization)
        
        psi_flat = spsolve(A, b)
        return psi_flat.reshape((N, N))
    
    def vorticity_bc(self):
        """
        Update vorticity boundary conditions from stream function.
        """
        N = self.N
        dx = self.dx
        
        # Wall vorticity (from Taylor expansion)
        # Bottom wall
        self.omega[0, :] = -2 * self.psi[1, :] / (dx**2)
        # Top wall (lid)
        self.omega[-1, :] = -2 * (self.psi[-2, :] - dx) / (dx**2)
        # Left wall
        self.omega[:, 0] = -2 * self.psi[:, 1] / (dx**2)
        # Right wall
        self.omega[:, -1] = -2 * self.psi[:, -2] / (dx**2)
    
    def step(self):
        """
        Advance one time step.
        """
        N = self.N
        dx = self.dx
        dt = self.dt
        Re = self.Re
        
        # Solve for stream function
        self.psi = self.solve_poisson(self.omega)
        
        # Update boundary vorticity
        self.vorticity_bc()
        
        # Compute velocity from stream function
        u = np.zeros((N, N))
        v = np.zeros((N, N))
        u[1:-1, 1:-1] = (self.psi[2:, 1:-1] - self.psi[:-2, 1:-1]) / (2*dx)
        v[1:-1, 1:-1] = -(self.psi[1:-1, 2:] - self.psi[1:-1, :-2]) / (2*dx)
        
        # Top lid velocity
        u[-1, :] = 1.0
        
        # Vorticity transport equation (simplified FTCS - for demonstration)
        omega_new = self.omega.copy()
        
        for i in range(1, N-1):
            for j in range(1, N-1):
                # Advection terms
                domega_dx = (self.omega[i, j+1] - self.omega[i, j-1]) / (2*dx)
                domega_dy = (self.omega[i+1, j] - self.omega[i-1, j]) / (2*dx)
                
                # Diffusion term
                laplacian = (self.omega[i+1, j] + self.omega[i-1, j] + 
                           self.omega[i, j+1] + self.omega[i, j-1] - 
                           4*self.omega[i, j]) / (dx**2)
                
                # Time stepping
                omega_new[i, j] = (self.omega[i, j] + 
                                  dt * (-u[i,j]*domega_dx - v[i,j]*domega_dy + 
                                        laplacian/Re))
        
        self.omega = omega_new
    
    def simulate(self, n_steps):
        """
        Run simulation for n_steps.
        """
        for _ in range(n_steps):
            self.step()
        
        # Final stream function solve
        self.psi = self.solve_poisson(self.omega)

Linear Programming: Optimization with Constraints

linear-programming

Real-World Motivation

Linear programming (LP) is one of the most successful applications of mathematics in industry. From airline scheduling and supply chain optimization to portfolio selection and resource allocation, LP provides optimal solutions to problems with linear objectives and constraints. The development of the simplex method by George Dantzig in 1947 and later interior-point methods have made it possible to solve problems with millions of variables, transforming operations research and management science.

Definitions

  • Standard Form LP: \[\text{minimize } \mathbf{c}^T\mathbf{x}\] \[\text{subject to } A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}, \quad \mathbf{x} \geq 0\] where \( \mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n \), \( A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n} \), \( \mathbf{b} \in \mathbb{R}^m \), \( \mathbf{c} \in \mathbb{R}^n \).
  • Feasible Region: The set of all \( \mathbf{x} \) satisfying the constraints, forming a convex polyhedron.
  • Basic Feasible Solution (BFS): A solution where at most \( m \) variables are non-zero (basic variables), corresponding to a vertex of the feasible region.
  • Dual Problem: The dual of the above LP is: \[\text{maximize } \mathbf{b}^T\mathbf{y}\] \[\text{subject to } A^T\mathbf{y} \leq \mathbf{c}\]

Theorem: Strong Duality

Theorem (Strong Duality): If either the primal or dual linear program has an optimal solution, then so does the other, and their optimal values are equal:

\[\min_{\mathbf{x}} \{\mathbf{c}^T\mathbf{x} : A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}, \mathbf{x} \geq 0\} = \max_{\mathbf{y}} \{\mathbf{b}^T\mathbf{y} : A^T\mathbf{y} \leq \mathbf{c}\}\]

Proof Sketch:

Step 1 (Weak Duality): For any feasible \( \mathbf{x} \) and \( \mathbf{y} \): \[\mathbf{b}^T\mathbf{y} = (A\mathbf{x})^T\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{x}^T A^T\mathbf{y} \leq \mathbf{x}^T \mathbf{c} = \mathbf{c}^T\mathbf{x}\] So the dual objective is always a lower bound on the primal.

Step 2 (Farkas' Lemma): Exactly one of the following holds:

  • There exists \( \mathbf{x} \geq 0 \) with \( A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b} \)
  • There exists \( \mathbf{y} \) with \( A^T\mathbf{y} \leq 0 \) and \( \mathbf{b}^T\mathbf{y} > 0 \)

Step 3 (Optimality): At optimality, complementary slackness holds: \( x_i^*(c_i - (A^T\mathbf{y}^*)_i) = 0 \) for all \( i \). This ensures equality of objectives. ∎

Notation Guide

  • \( \mathbf{x} \): Decision variables (vector in \( \mathbb{R}^n \))
  • \( \mathbf{c} \): Cost coefficients
  • \( A \): Constraint matrix (\( m \times n \))
  • \( \mathbf{b} \): Right-hand side constraints
  • \( \mathbf{y} \): Dual variables
  • BFS: Basic Feasible Solution
  • \( z = \mathbf{c}^T\mathbf{x} \): Objective value

    """
    Educational simplex method implementation.
    """
    
    def __init__(self, c, A, b):
        """
        Standard form: minimize c^T x subject to Ax = b, x >= 0
        """
        self.c = np.array(c, dtype=float)
        self.A = np.array(A, dtype=float)
        self.b = np.array(b, dtype=float)
        self.m, self.n = self.A.shape
    
    def solve(self):
        """
        Solve using scipy's linprog (production-ready).
        """
        # Convert equality constraints to bounds format
        result = linprog(
            self.c,
            A_eq=self.A,
            b_eq=self.b,
            bounds=[(0, None)] * self.n,
            method='highs'
        )
        return result
    
    def sensitivity_analysis(self, result):
        """
        Analyze sensitivity of solution to parameter changes.
        """
        if not result.success:
            return None
        
        x_opt = result.x
        z_opt = result.fun
        
        # Shadow prices (dual variables)
        shadow_prices = result.ineqlin.marginals if hasattr(result, 'ineqlin') else None
        
        return {
            'optimal_value': z_opt,
            'optimal_solution': x_opt,
            'shadow_prices': shadow_prices,
            'reduced_costs': result.marginals if hasattr(result, 'marginals') else None
        }

Matroid Theory: Abstraction of Linear Independence

matroid-theory

Historical Context

Matroid theory was introduced by Hassler Whitney in 1935 as a common generalization of linear independence in vector spaces and graph-theoretic independence (forests). The theory captures the essence of "dependence" in a purely combinatorial way, unifying results from graph theory, linear algebra, and combinatorial optimization. Matroids have found applications in network flow algorithms, coding theory, and algorithm design.

Definitions

  • Matroid: A pair \( M = (E, \mathcal{I}) \) where \( E \) is a finite ground set and \( \mathcal{I} \subseteq 2^E \) (independent sets) satisfies:
    1. \( \emptyset \in \mathcal{I} \) (non-empty)
    2. If \( I \in \mathcal{I} \) and \( J \subseteq I \), then \( J \in \mathcal{I} \) (hereditary)
    3. If \( I, J \in \mathcal{I} \) and \( |J| > |I| \), then \( \exists e \in J \setminus I \) such that \( I \cup \{e\} \in \mathcal{I} \) (exchange)
  • Basis: A maximal independent set. All bases have the same cardinality (the rank of the matroid).
  • Circuit: A minimal dependent set.
  • Rank Function: \( r(A) = \max\{|I| : I \subseteq A, I \in \mathcal{I}\} \).

Theorem: The Matroid Intersection Theorem

Theorem (Edmonds' Matroid Intersection): Let \( M_1 = (E, \mathcal{I}_1) \) and \( M_2 = (E, \mathcal{I}_2) \) be two matroids on the same ground set with rank functions \( r_1 \) and \( r_2 \). Then:

\[\max\{|I| : I \in \mathcal{I}_1 \cap \mathcal{I}_2\} = \min_{A \subseteq E} \{r_1(A) + r_2(E \setminus A)\}\]

Proof Sketch:

Step 1 (Weak Inequality): For any \( I \in \mathcal{I}_1 \cap \mathcal{I}_2 \) and any \( A \subseteq E \): \[|I| = |I \cap A| + |I \cap (E \setminus A)| \leq r_1(A) + r_2(E \setminus A)\]

Step 2 (Augmenting Paths): Construct an auxiliary graph where vertices are elements of \( E \) and edges represent exchange possibilities. An augmenting path corresponds to increasing the common independent set.

Step 3 (Minimality): When no augmenting path exists, the set of vertices reachable from the "source" side gives the minimizing \( A \). ∎

Notation Guide

  • \( M = (E, \mathcal{I}) \): Matroid with ground set E and independent sets \( \mathcal{I} \)
  • \( r(M) \): Rank of matroid M
  • \( \mathcal{B} \): Set of bases
  • \( \mathcal{C} \): Set of circuits
  • \( M^* \): Dual matroid
  • \( U_{r,n} \): Uniform matroid of rank r on n elements

from itertools import combinations

class Matroid:
    """
    Basic matroid implementation for educational purposes.
    """
    
    def __init__(self, ground_set, independent_sets):
        """
        ground_set: list of elements
        independent_sets: list of frozensets representing independent sets
        """
        self.E = set(ground_set)
        self.I = set(independent_sets)
        
        # Verify matroid axioms
        self._verify_axioms()
    
    def _verify_axioms(self):
        """Check that I satisfies matroid axioms."""
        # I1: Empty set is independent
        assert frozenset() in self.I, "Empty set must be independent"
        
        # I2: Hereditary property
        for I in self.I:
            for r in range(len(I) + 1):
                for J in combinations(I, r):
                    assert frozenset(J) in self.I, f"Hereditary property violated"
        
        # I3: Exchange property (checked lazily)
    
    def rank(self, A=None):
        """Compute rank of set A (or whole matroid if A is None)."""
        if A is None:
            A = self.E
        A = set(A)
        
        max_size = 0
        for I in self.I:
            if I <= A:
                max_size = max(max_size, len(I))
        return max_size
    
    def is_independent(self, S):
        """Check if S is independent."""
        return frozenset(S) in self.I
    
    def bases(self):
        """Return all bases (maximal independent sets)."""
        r = self.rank()
        return {I for I in self.I if len(I) == r}
    
    def circuits(self):
        """Return all circuits (minimal dependent sets)."""
        circuits = set()
        for S in self._all_subsets():
            if not self.is_independent(S):
                # Check minimality
                is_minimal = True
                for e in S:
                    if self.is_independent(S - {e}):
                        is_minimal = False
                        break
                if is_minimal:
                    circuits.add(frozenset(S))
        return circuits
    
    def _all_subsets(self):
        """Generate all subsets of ground set."""
        E_list = list(self.E)
        for r in range(len(E_list) + 1):
            for subset in combinations(E_list, r):
                yield set(subset)

def graphic_matroid(edges, vertices):
    """
    Create graphic matroid from graph.
    Independent sets = forests (acyclic edge sets).
    """
    # Find all forests
    independent = [frozenset()]
    
    for r in range(1, len(edges) + 1):
        for edge_set in combinations(range(len(edges)), r):
            edge_set = set(edge_set)
            if is_forest(edge_set, edges, vertices):
                independent.append(frozenset(edge_set))
    
    return Matroid(range(len(edges)), independent)

def is_forest(edge_indices, edges, vertices):
    """Check if edge set forms a forest (no cycles)."""
    # Union-Find to detect cycles
    parent = {v: v for v in vertices}
    
    def find(x):
        if parent[x] != x:
            parent[x] = find(parent[x])
        return parent[x]
    
    def union(x, y):
        px, py = find(x), find(y)
        if px == py:
            return False  # Cycle detected
        parent[px] = py
        return True
    
    for idx in edge_indices:
        u, v = edges[idx]
        if not union(u, v):
            return False
    return True

Permutation Groups: Symmetry in Action

permutation-groups

Historical Context

The study of permutation groups began with Évariste Galois in the 1830s, who used them to solve the ancient problem of determining when polynomial equations can be solved by radicals. Galois's work, published posthumously, founded group theory and established the deep connection between field extensions and permutation groups. Today, permutation groups are fundamental in chemistry (molecular symmetry), computer science (sorting algorithms, cryptography), and combinatorics (counting problems).

Definitions

  • Permutation: A bijection \( \sigma: \{1, 2, \ldots, n\} \to \{1, 2, \ldots, n\} \). Written in cycle notation, e.g., \( (1\, 3\, 2) \) sends 1→3, 3→2, 2→1.
  • Symmetric Group \( S_n \): The group of all permutations of \( n \) elements, with composition as the operation. Order: \( |S_n| = n! \).
  • Transposition: A permutation swapping two elements and fixing all others: \( (i\, j) \).
  • Alternating Group \( A_n \): The subgroup of \( S_n \) consisting of even permutations (products of an even number of transpositions). Order: \( |A_n| = n!/2 \).
  • Orbit: For a group \( G \) acting on a set \( X \), the orbit of \( x \in X \) is \( G \cdot x = \{g \cdot x : g \in G\} \).

Theorem: Simplicity of \( A_n \) for \( n \geq 5 \)

Theorem: The alternating group \( A_n \) is simple (has no non-trivial normal subgroups) for all \( n \geq 5 \).

Proof Sketch:

Step 1 (3-Cycles Generate): \( A_n \) is generated by 3-cycles. Any even permutation can be written as a product of 3-cycles.

Step 2 (Normal Subgroup Contains 3-Cycle): Let \( N \trianglelefteq A_n \) be a non-trivial normal subgroup. We show \( N \) contains a 3-cycle.

Step 3 (Conjugacy): In \( A_n \) for \( n \geq 5 \), all 3-cycles are conjugate. If \( N \) contains one 3-cycle, it contains all 3-cycles.

Step 4 (Conclusion): Since 3-cycles generate \( A_n \), we have \( N = A_n \). ∎

Notation Guide

  • \( S_n \): Symmetric group on n elements
  • \( A_n \): Alternating group on n elements
  • \( (a_1\, a_2\, \cdots\, a_k) \): k-cycle permutation
  • \( \text{sgn}(\sigma) \): Sign of permutation (+1 for even, -1 for odd)
  • \( \sigma \tau \): Composition (apply τ, then σ)
  • \( \sigma^{-1} \): Inverse permutation
  • \( \text{Fix}(\sigma) \): Set of fixed points of σ

from itertools import permutations
from collections import defaultdict
import math

class Permutation:
    """
    Permutation represented as a tuple (0-indexed).
    """
    
    def __init__(self, mapping):
        """
        mapping: tuple where mapping[i] = sigma(i)
        """
        self.sigma = tuple(mapping)
        self.n = len(mapping)
        
        # Verify it's a valid permutation
        assert set(self.sigma) == set(range(self.n)), "Invalid permutation"
    
    def __call__(self, i):
        """Apply permutation to element i."""
        return self.sigma[i]
    
    def __mul__(self, other):
        """Composition: self * other means apply other, then self."""
        if isinstance(other, Permutation):
            result = tuple(self.sigma[other.sigma[i]] for i in range(self.n))
            return Permutation(result)
        return NotImplemented
    
    def __pow__(self, k):
        """Power of permutation."""
        result = Permutation.identity(self.n)
        current = self
        while k > 0:
            if k % 2 == 1:
                result = result * current
            current = current * current
            k //= 2
        return result
    
    def inverse(self):
        """Compute inverse permutation."""
        inv = [0] * self.n
        for i, j in enumerate(self.sigma):
            inv[j] = i
        return Permutation(tuple(inv))
    
    def cycles(self):
        """Decompose into disjoint cycles."""
        visited = [False] * self.n
        cycle_list = []
        
        for i in range(self.n):
            if not visited[i]:
                cycle = []
                j = i
                while not visited[j]:
                    visited[j] = True
                    cycle.append(j)
                    j = self.sigma[j]
                if len(cycle) > 1:
                    cycle_list.append(tuple(cycle))
        
        return cycle_list
    
    def sign(self):
        """Compute sign of permutation: (-1)^(number of inversions)."""
        inversions = 0
        for i in range(self.n):
            for j in range(i + 1, self.n):
                if self.sigma[i] > self.sigma[j]:
                    inversions += 1
        return 1 if inversions % 2 == 0 else -1
    
    def is_even(self):
        """Check if permutation is even."""
        return self.sign() == 1
    
    @staticmethod
    def identity(n):
        """Identity permutation on n elements."""
        return Permutation(tuple(range(n)))
    
    @staticmethod
    def from_cycles(cycles, n):
        """Create permutation from disjoint cycles."""
        sigma = list(range(n))
        for cycle in cycles:
            for i in range(len(cycle)):
                sigma[cycle[i]] = cycle[(i + 1) % len(cycle)]
        return Permutation(tuple(sigma))
    
    def __repr__(self):
        cycles = self.cycles()
        if not cycles:
            return "id"
        return "".join(f"({' '.join(map(str, c))})" for c in cycles)
    
    def __eq__(self, other):
        return self.sigma == other.sigma
    
    def __hash__(self):
        return hash(self.sigma)

class PermutationGroup:
    """
    Permutation group generated by a set of permutations.
    """
    
    def __init__(self, generators):
        """
        generators: list of Permutation objects
        """
        self.generators = generators
        self.n = generators[0].n if generators else 0
        self.elements = self._generate_group()
    
    def _generate_group(self):
        """Generate all group elements by closure."""
        elements = {Permutation.identity(self.n)}
        changed = True
        
        while changed:
            changed = False
            new_elements = set()
            for g in elements:
                for h in self.generators:
                    prod = g * h
                    if prod not in elements:
                        new_elements.add(prod)
                        changed = True
            elements.update(new_elements)
        
        return elements
    
    def order(self):
        """Order of the group."""
        return len(self.elements)
    
    def is_subgroup(self, other):
        """Check if self is a subgroup of other."""
        return self.elements <= other.elements
    
    def conjugacy_classes(self):
        """Compute conjugacy classes."""
        classes = []
        remaining = set(self.elements)
        
        while remaining:
            g = remaining.pop()
            cls = set()
            for h in self.elements:
                conj = h * g * h.inverse()
                cls.add(conj)
            remaining -= cls
            classes.append(cls)
        
        return classes

Compressed Sensing: Recovering Sparse Signals

compressed-sensing

Historical Context

Compressed sensing emerged in the mid-2000s through the groundbreaking work of Emmanuel Candès, Terence Tao, and David Donoho. They showed that sparse signals can be recovered from far fewer measurements than the Nyquist-Shannon theorem would suggest, revolutionizing signal processing, medical imaging (MRI), and data acquisition. The field connects optimization, probability, and harmonic analysis in surprising ways.

Definitions

  • Sparse Vector: A vector \( \mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n \) with at most \( s \) non-zero entries. The sparsity is \( \|\mathbf{x}\|_0 = |\{i : x_i \neq 0\}| \).
  • Measurement Matrix: A matrix \( A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n} \) with \( m \ll n \) that maps the signal to measurements \( \mathbf{y} = A\mathbf{x} \).
  • Restricted Isometry Property (RIP): Matrix \( A \) satisfies RIP of order \( s \) if there exists \( \delta_s \in (0, 1) \) such that for all \( s \)-sparse \( \mathbf{x} \): \[(1 - \delta_s)\|\mathbf{x}\|_2^2 \leq \|A\mathbf{x}\|_2^2 \leq (1 + \delta_s)\|\mathbf{x}\|_2^2\]
  • Basis Pursuit: The convex optimization problem: \[\min \|\mathbf{x}\|_1 \text{ subject to } A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{y}\]

Theorem: Exact Recovery via Basis Pursuit

Theorem: Let \( A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n} \) satisfy RIP of order \( 2s \) with \( \delta_{2s} < \sqrt{2} - 1 \approx 0.414 \). Then for any \( s \)-sparse \( \mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^n \), the solution \( \mathbf{x}^* \) to basis pursuit with \( \mathbf{y} = A\mathbf{x} \) satisfies \( \mathbf{x}^* = \mathbf{x} \).

Proof Sketch:

Step 1 (Feasibility): Let \( \mathbf{h} = \mathbf{x}^* - \mathbf{x} \). Since both are feasible, \( A\mathbf{h} = 0 \).

Step 2 (Cone Constraint): By optimality of \( \mathbf{x}^* \): \[\|\mathbf{x}^*\|_1 \leq \|\mathbf{x}\|_1\]

Decomposing \( \mathbf{h} = \mathbf{h}_S + \mathbf{h}_{S^c} \) where \( S \) is the support of \( \mathbf{x} \), we get: \[\|\mathbf{h}_{S^c}\|_1 \leq \|\mathbf{h}_S\|_1\]

Step 3 (RIP Application): Using RIP and the fact that \( A\mathbf{h} = 0 \), we bound \( \|\mathbf{h}\|_2 \) in terms of \( \delta_{2s} \). When \( \delta_{2s} < \sqrt{2} - 1 \), this forces \( \mathbf{h} = 0 \). ∎

Notation Guide

  • \( \|\mathbf{x}\|_0 \): "L0 norm" (sparsity, count of non-zeros)
  • \( \|\mathbf{x}\|_1 = \sum |x_i| \): L1 norm (convex relaxation)
  • \( \|\mathbf{x}\|_2 \): Euclidean norm
  • \( \text{supp}(\mathbf{x}) \): Support of x (indices where x is non-zero)
  • \( \delta_s \): RIP constant of order s
  • \( \mathbf{x}_S \): Vector x restricted to support S

import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import minimize, linprog
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def generate_rip_matrix(m, n, matrix_type='gaussian'):
    """
    Generate measurement matrix with good RIP properties.
    """
    if matrix_type == 'gaussian':
        # Gaussian entries: N(0, 1/m)
        A = np.random.randn(m, n) / np.sqrt(m)
    elif matrix_type == 'bernoulli':
        # Bernoulli: ±1 with equal probability, scaled
        A = np.random.choice([-1, 1], size=(m, n)) / np.sqrt(m)
    elif matrix_type == 'partial_fft':
        # Random rows of Fourier matrix
        from scipy.linalg import dft
        F = dft(n) / np.sqrt(n)
        rows = np.random.choice(n, m, replace=False)
        A = F[rows, :].real
    else:
        raise ValueError(f"Unknown matrix type: {matrix_type}")
    
    return A

def basis_pursuit(A, y, method='scipy'):
    """
    Solve basis pursuit: min ||x||_1 subject to Ax = y.
    """
    m, n = A.shape
    
    if method == 'scipy':
        # Convert to linear program
        # min c^T z subject to M z = b, z >= 0
        # where z = [x^+; x^-] and x = x^+ - x^-, |x| = x^+ + x^-
        
        c = np.ones(2 * n)  # Minimize sum of x^+ and x^-
        
        # Equality constraints: A(x^+ - x^-) = y
        A_eq = np.hstack([A, -A])
        b_eq = y
        
        # Bounds: x^+, x^- >= 0
        bounds = [(0, None)] * (2 * n)
        
        result = linprog(c, A_eq=A_eq, b_eq=b_eq, bounds=bounds, method='highs')
        
        if result.success:
            z = result.x
            x = z[:n] - z[n:]
            return x
        else:
            return None
    
    elif method == 'cvx':
        # Would use cvxpy in practice
        raise NotImplementedError("Install cvxpy for better performance")

def iterative_hard_thresholding(A, y, s, max_iter=100, tol=1e-6):
    """
    IHT: Greedy algorithm for sparse recovery.
    """
    m, n = A.shape
    x = np.zeros(n)
    
    # Step size (Lipschitz constant of gradient)
    L = np.linalg.norm(A, 2) ** 2
    mu = 1.0 / L
    
    for _ in range(max_iter):
        # Gradient descent step
        x_new = x + mu * A.T @ (y - A @ x)
        
        # Hard thresholding: keep only s largest entries
        idx = np.argsort(np.abs(x_new))[-s:]
        x_new = np.zeros(n)
        x_new[idx] = x_new[idx]
        
        if np.linalg.norm(x_new - x) < tol:
            break
        
        x = x_new
    
    return x

def omp(A, y, sparsity):
    """
    Orthogonal Matching Pursuit for sparse recovery.
    """
    m, n = A.shape
    residual = y.copy()
    support = []
    x = np.zeros(n)
    
    for _ in range(sparsity):
        # Find column most correlated with residual
        correlations = np.abs(A.T @ residual)
        new_idx = np.argmax(correlations)
        
        if new_idx in support:
            break
        
        support.append(new_idx)
        
        # Least squares on selected support
        A_s = A[:, support]
        x_s = np.linalg.lstsq(A_s, y, rcond=None)[0]
        
        # Update residual
        x = np.zeros(n)
        x[support] = x_s
        residual = y - A @ x
    
    return x

While Kurt Gödel's incompleteness theorems (1931) showed that consistency and completeness could not be simultaneously achieved for sufficiently powerful systems, Hilbert's program catalyzed the development of proof theory and mathematical logic.

Hilbert's influence extended through his students and the mathematical school he built at Göttingen, which was arguably the world's leading center for mathematics until the Nazi regime forced many of its members into exile. His famous quote — "We must know, we will know" (Wir müssen wissen, wir werden wissen) — encapsulates his optimistic view of mathematical progress and continues to inspire mathematicians today.

IMO Shortlist 2023 Problem

Problem: Let \( n \geq 2 \) be an integer. Find all functions \( f: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R} \) such that for all real numbers \( x \) and \( y \): \[f(x^n + y) + f(x^n - y) = 2f(x^n) + 2f(y)\]

Hint

First, try substituting \( x = 0 \) to find a relationship between \( f(y) \) and \( f(-y) \). Then consider the case \( y = 0 \) and look for patterns that suggest quadratic behavior.

Solution

Step 1: Setting \( x = 0 \), we get: \[f(y) + f(-y) = 2f(0) + 2f(y)\] This gives \( f(-y) = 2f(0) + f(y) \).

Step 2: Setting \( y = 0 \): \[2f(x^n) = 2f(x^n) + 2f(0)\] This implies \( f(0) = 0 \).

Step 3: From Step 1 with \( f(0) = 0 \), we get \( f(-y) = f(y) \), so \( f \) is even.

Step 4: The functional equation resembles the parallelogram law. Define \( g(x) = f(x) - ax^2 \) for appropriate \( a \), or verify directly that \( f(x) = cx^2 \) works.

Step 5: Substituting \( f(x) = cx^2 \): \[c(x^n + y)^2 + c(x^n - y)^2 = 2cx^{2n} + 2cy^2\] \[c(x^{2n} + 2x^n y + y^2 + x^{2n} - 2x^n y + y^2) = 2cx^{2n} + 2cy^2\] \[2cx^{2n} + 2cy^2 = 2cx^{2n} + 2cy^2\] ✓

Answer: \( f(x) = cx^2 \) for any constant \( c \in \mathbb{R} \).

The Mandelbrot set is the set of complex numbers \( c \) for which the iteration \( z_{n+1} = z_n^2 + c \) with \( z_0 = 0 \) remains bounded. Its boundary exhibits infinite complexity — zooming in reveals ever more intricate patterns that echo the whole.

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def mandelbrot(c, max_iter=100):
    """
    Compute the number of iterations until escape.
    """
    z = 0
    for n in range(max_iter):
        if abs(z) > 2:
            return n
        z = z*z + c
    return max_iter

def mandelbrot_set(xmin, xmax, ymin, ymax, width, height, max_iter=100):

    Generate the Mandelbrot set.
    """

    y = np.linspace(ymin, ymax, height)
    X, Y = np.meshgrid(x, y)
    C = X + 1j*Y
    
    # Vectorized computation
    Z = np.zeros_like(C)
    M = np.zeros(C.shape, dtype=int)
    
    for i in range(max_iter):
        mask = np.abs(Z) <= 2
        Z[mask] = Z[mask]**2 + C[mask]
        M[mask] = i
    
    return M
