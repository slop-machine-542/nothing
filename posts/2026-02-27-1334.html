Galois Theory: The Symmetry of Equations

Motivation

For thousands of years, mathematicians sought formulas for solving polynomial equations. The quadratic formula was known to Babylonians, and Italian mathematicians discovered formulas for cubic and quartic equations in the 16th century. But for centuries, the quintic equation resisted all attempts. Galois theory, developed by Évariste Galois in the 1830s, finally explained why: it provides a deep connection between field extensions and group theory, showing that a polynomial is solvable by radicals if and only if its Galois group is solvable.

Definitions

Field Extension: If \(F\) and \(E\) are fields with \(F \subseteq E\), we call \(E/F\) a field extension. The degree \([E:F]\) is the dimension of \(E\) as a vector space over \(F\).

Splitting Field: For a polynomial \(f(x) \in F[x]\), the splitting field is the smallest extension \(E/F\) such that \(f\) factors completely into linear factors in \(E[x]\).

Galois Group: For a field extension \(E/F\), the Galois group \(\text{Gal}(E/F)\) is the group of field automorphisms of \(E\) that fix \(F\) pointwise.

Galois Extension: An extension \(E/F\) is Galois if it is normal (every irreducible polynomial with a root in \(E\) splits completely) and separable (every element has distinct minimal polynomial roots).

Theorem and Proof

The Fundamental Theorem of Galois Theory: Let \(E/F\) be a finite Galois extension with Galois group \(G = \text{Gal}(E/F)\). There is a bijective correspondence between:

\[\{\text{subgroups } H \leq G\} \longleftrightarrow \{\text{intermediate fields } F \subseteq K \subseteq E\}\]

given by \(H \mapsto E^H\) (the fixed field of \(H\)) and \(K \mapsto \text{Gal}(E/K)\). This correspondence reverses inclusion, and \([E:E^H] = |H|\).

Proof Sketch: The key observation is that for any subgroup \(H\), the fixed field \(E^H\) satisfies \([E:E^H] = |H|\) by Artin's theorem. Conversely, for any intermediate field \(K\), we have \(E^{\text{Gal}(E/K)} = K\) by the characterization of Galois extensions. The maps are mutually inverse, establishing the correspondence. The reversal of inclusion follows from the definitions: larger subgroups fix smaller subfields.

Notation Guide

  • \(E/F\) — field extension (read "E over F")
  • \([E:F]\) — degree of extension (dimension as vector space)
  • \(\text{Gal}(E/F)\) — Galois group of the extension
  • \(E^H\) — fixed field of subgroup \(H\)
  • \(F(\alpha)\) — smallest field containing \(F\) and \(\alpha\)

Complex Analysis: The Residue Theorem

Motivation

Complex analysis reveals a profound difference between real and complex differentiability. While real differentiable functions can be quite wild, complex differentiable (holomorphic) functions are remarkably rigid—they are infinitely differentiable, admit power series expansions, and satisfy powerful integral theorems. The residue theorem stands as the crown jewel: it allows evaluation of complex integrals by examining only local behavior at singularities, with applications ranging from evaluating real integrals to counting zeros of polynomials.

Definitions

Holomorphic Function: A function \(f: U \to \mathbb{C}\) is holomorphic on an open set \(U\) if the complex derivative \(f'(z) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(z+h) - f(z)}{h}\) exists at every point in \(U\).

Isolated Singularity: A point \(z_0\) is an isolated singularity of \(f\) if \(f\) is holomorphic on a punctured disk \(0 < |z - z_0| < r\) but not at \(z_0\) itself.

Laurent Series: Near an isolated singularity \(z_0\), \(f\) admits an expansion \(f(z) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} a_n (z - z_0)^n\). The coefficient \(a_{-1}\) is called the residue of \(f\) at \(z_0\), denoted \(\text{Res}(f, z_0)\).

Types of Singularities: Removable (no negative powers), pole (finite negative powers), essential (infinitely many negative powers).

Theorem and Proof

The Residue Theorem: Let \(f\) be holomorphic inside and on a simple closed contour \(\gamma\), except for isolated singularities \(z_1, z_2, \ldots, z_n\) inside \(\gamma\). Then: \[\oint_\gamma f(z)\, dz = 2\pi i \sum_{k=1}^n \text{Res}(f, z_k)\]

Proof: By Cauchy's theorem, we can deform \(\gamma\) into small circles around each singularity. For a circle \(C_k\) around \(z_k\), parameterize \(z = z_k + re^{i\theta}\) and use the Laurent series: \[\oint_{C_k} f(z)\, dz = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} a_n \oint_{C_k} (z-z_k)^n\, dz = 2\pi i \cdot a_{-1}\] since \(\oint (z-z_k)^n\, dz = 0\) for \(n \neq -1\) and equals \(2\pi i\) for \(n = -1\). Summing over all singularities gives the result.

Notation Guide

  • \(\mathbb{C}\) — complex numbers
  • \(f'(z)\) — complex derivative
  • \(\oint_\gamma\) — contour integral
  • \(\text{Res}(f, z_0)\) — residue of \(f\) at \(z_0\)
  • \(\text{wind}(\gamma, z)\) — winding number of \(\gamma\) around \(z\)

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def compute_residue_numerical(f, z0, radius=0.01, n_points=1000):
    """
    Numerically compute the residue of f at z0 using contour integration.
    Res(f, z0) = (1/2πi) ∮ f(z) dz around z0.
    """
    theta = np.linspace(0, 2*np.pi, n_points, endpoint=False)
    z = z0 + radius * np.exp(1j * theta)
    dz = 1j * radius * np.exp(1j * theta) * (2*np.pi / n_points)
    
    integral = np.sum(f(z) * dz)
    residue = integral / (2j * np.pi)
    return residue

Differential Geometry: Curvature and the Shape of Space

Motivation

How do we understand the shape of a curved surface? Gauss's Theorema Egregium ("remarkable theorem") revealed that certain aspects of curvature are intrinsic—they can be determined by measurements within the surface itself, without reference to the ambient space. This insight, that a surface "knows" its own curvature, became foundational for Riemann's generalization to higher dimensions and ultimately for Einstein's theory of general relativity, where gravity is the curvature of spacetime.

Definitions

First Fundamental Form: For a parametrized surface \(\mathbf{r}(u,v)\), the metric \(I = E\, du^2 + 2F\, du\, dv + G\, dv^2\) where \(E = \mathbf{r}_u \cdot \mathbf{r}_u\), \(F = \mathbf{r}_u \cdot \mathbf{r}_v\), \(G = \mathbf{r}_v \cdot \mathbf{r}_v\).

Second Fundamental Form: \(II = L\, du^2 + 2M\, du\, dv + N\, dv^2\) where \(L = \mathbf{r}_{uu} \cdot \mathbf{n}\), \(M = \mathbf{r}_{uv} \cdot \mathbf{n}\), \(N = \mathbf{r}_{vv} \cdot \mathbf{n}\), and \(\mathbf{n}\) is the unit normal.

Gaussian Curvature: \(K = \frac{LN - M^2}{EG - F^2} = \kappa_1 \kappa_2\), the product of principal curvatures.

Mean Curvature: \(H = \frac{EN + GL - 2FM}{2(EG - F^2)} = \frac{\kappa_1 + \kappa_2}{2}\).

Theorem and Proof

Theorema Egregium (Gauss): The Gaussian curvature \(K\) of a surface is determined entirely by the first fundamental form (the metric) and its derivatives. Specifically: \[K = -\frac{1}{2\sqrt{EG}}\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial u}\left(\frac{G_u}{\sqrt{EG}}\right) + \frac{\partial}{\partial v}\left(\frac{E_v}{\sqrt{EG}}\right)\right]\] in orthogonal coordinates where \(F = 0\).

Proof Sketch: The proof proceeds by expressing the Christoffel symbols purely in terms of the metric coefficients and their derivatives. The Gauss equations (relating the second derivatives of the surface parametrization) yield the Gauss-Codazzi equations. Remarkably, one combination of these equations gives an expression for \(K\) depending only on the metric and its first two derivatives, independent of the second fundamental form. This shows that \(K\) is intrinsic—two surfaces with the same metric (like a cylinder and a plane locally) have the same Gaussian curvature, even if embedded differently in \(\mathbb{R}^3\).

Notation Guide

  • \(E, F, G\) — coefficients of first fundamental form
  • \(L, M, N\) — coefficients of second fundamental form
  • \(K\) — Gaussian curvature (intrinsic)
  • \(H\) — mean curvature (extrinsic)
  • \(\kappa_1, \kappa_2\) — principal curvatures
  • \(\Gamma^k_{ij}\) — Christoffel symbols

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from mpl_toolkits.mplot3d import Axes3D

def compute_curvatures_surface(X, Y, Z):
    """
    Compute Gaussian and mean curvature from discrete surface data.
    Uses discrete differential geometry approximations.
    """
    # Compute first derivatives
    Xu, Xv = np.gradient(X)
    Yu, Yv = np.gradient(Y)
    Zu, Zv = np.gradient(Z)
    
    # First fundamental form coefficients
    E = Xu**2 + Yu**2 + Zu**2
    F = Xu*Xv + Yu*Yv + Zu*Zv
    G = Xv**2 + Yv**2 + Zv**2
    
    # Compute normal vector
    ru = np.stack([Xu, Yu, Zu], axis=-1)
    rv = np.stack([Xv, Yv, Zv], axis=-1)
    n = np.cross(ru, rv)
    n_norm = np.linalg.norm(n, axis=-1, keepdims=True)
    n_norm = np.where(n_norm == 0, 1, n_norm)
    n = n / n_norm
    
    # Second derivatives
    Xuu, Xuv = np.gradient(Xu)
    Yuu, Yuv = np.gradient(Yu)
    Zuu, Zuv = np.gradient(Zu)
    Xvu, Xvv = np.gradient(Xv)
    Yvu, Yvv = np.gradient(Yv)
    Zvu, Zvv = np.gradient(Zv)
    
    # Second fundamental form coefficients
    ruu = np.stack([Xuu, Yuu, Zuu], axis=-1)
    ruv = np.stack([Xuv, Yuv, Zuv], axis=-1)
    rvv = np.stack([Xvv, Yvv, Zvv], axis=-1)
    
    L = np.sum(ruu * n, axis=-1)
    M = np.sum(ruv * n, axis=-1)
    N = np.sum(rvv * n, axis=-1)
    
    # Gaussian and mean curvature
    denom = E*G - F**2
    denom = np.where(denom == 0, 1, denom)
    
    K = (L*N - M**2) / denom
    H = (E*N + G*L - 2*F*M) / (2*denom)
    
    return K, H, E, F, G

RSA Cryptography: The Mathematics of Secure Communication

Real-World Motivation

Every time you visit a secure website (HTTPS), send an encrypted email, or verify a digital signature, you're likely using RSA encryption. Invented in 1977 by Rivest, Shamir, and Adleman, RSA was the first practical public-key cryptosystem. Its security rests on a simple asymmetry: multiplying two large primes is easy, but factoring their product is computationally infeasible. This "one-way function" enables anyone to encrypt messages using a public key, while only the holder of the private key can decrypt them.

Definitions

Public Key: A pair \((n, e)\) where \(n = pq\) is the product of two large primes, and \(e\) is coprime to \(\varphi(n) = (p-1)(q-1)\).

Private Key: The pair \((n, d)\) where \(d\) is the modular inverse of \(e\) modulo \(\varphi(n)\), i.e., \(ed \equiv 1 \pmod{\varphi(n)}\).

Euler's Totient Function: \(\varphi(n)\) counts integers up to \(n\) coprime to \(n\). For \(n = pq\), \(\varphi(n) = (p-1)(q-1)\).

Modular Exponentiation: Computing \(a^b \pmod n\) efficiently using repeated squaring.

Theorem and Proof

RSA Correctness: For any message \(m\) with \(0 \leq m < n\): \[(m^e)^d \equiv m \pmod n\]

Proof: Since \(ed \equiv 1 \pmod{\varphi(n)}\), we have \(ed = 1 + k\varphi(n)\) for some integer \(k\). By Euler's theorem, \(m^{\varphi(n)} \equiv 1 \pmod n\) when \(\gcd(m,n) = 1\). Thus: \[(m^e)^d = m^{ed} = m^{1 + k\varphi(n)} = m \cdot (m^{\varphi(n)})^k \equiv m \cdot 1^k = m \pmod n\] The case \(\gcd(m,n) \neq 1\) requires a more careful analysis using the Chinese Remainder Theorem but yields the same result.

Notation Guide

  • \(n\) — modulus (public, product of two primes)
  • \(e\) — public exponent (typically 65537 = \(2^{16} + 1\))
  • \(d\) — private exponent (kept secret)
  • \(\varphi(n)\) — Euler's totient function
  • \(a \equiv b \pmod n\) — congruence modulo \(n\)

import random
from math import gcd

def mod_exp(base, exp, mod):
    """Compute (base^exp) % mod using fast exponentiation."""
    result = 1
    base = base % mod
    while exp > 0:
        if exp % 2 == 1:
            result = (result * base) % mod
        exp = exp >> 1
        base = (base * base) % mod
    return result

def extended_gcd(a, b):
    """Extended Euclidean algorithm. Returns (g, x, y) such that ax + by = g = gcd(a,b)."""
    if b == 0:
        return (a, 1, 0)
    g, x1, y1 = extended_gcd(b, a % b)
    x = y1
    y = x1 - (a // b) * y1
    return (g, x, y)

def mod_inverse(a, m):
    """Compute modular inverse of a modulo m."""
    g, x, _ = extended_gcd(a, m)
    if g != 1:
        raise ValueError("Modular inverse does not exist")
    return (x % m + m) % m

def is_prime(n, k=10):
    """Miller-Rabin primality test."""
    if n < 2:
        return False
    if n in (2, 3):
        return True
    if n % 2 == 0:
        return False
    
    # Write n-1 as 2^r * d
    r, d = 0, n - 1
    while d % 2 == 0:
        r += 1
        d //= 2
    
    # Witness loop
    for _ in range(k):
        a = random.randrange(2, n - 1)
        x = mod_exp(a, d, n)
        if x == 1 or x == n - 1:
            continue
        for _ in range(r - 1):
            x = mod_exp(x, 2, n)
            if x == n - 1:
                break
        else:
            return False
    return True

def generate_prime(bits):
    """Generate a random prime with specified bit length."""
    while True:
        n = random.getrandbits(bits) | 1 | (1 << (bits - 1))
        if is_prime(n):
            return n

def generate_rsa_keys(bits=512):
    """Generate RSA key pair."""
    # Generate two distinct primes
    p = generate_prime(bits // 2)
    q = generate_prime(bits // 2)
    while q == p:
        q = generate_prime(bits // 2)
    
    n = p * q
    phi = (p - 1) * (q - 1)
    
    # Common choice: e = 65537
    e = 65537
    while gcd(e, phi) != 1:
        e += 2
    
    d = mod_inverse(e, phi)
    
    return ((n, e), (n, d), p, q)

The Fast Fourier Transform: From \(O(n^2)\) to \(O(n \log n)\)

Real-World Motivation

The Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) is one of the most important algorithms ever devised. It underlies digital audio compression (MP3), image processing (JPEG), wireless communication (WiFi, 5G), medical imaging (MRI, CT), and countless scientific computations. The discrete Fourier transform (DFT) converts a signal from time domain to frequency domain, but a naive implementation requires \(O(n^2)\) operations. The FFT, discovered by Cooley and Tukey in 1965 (though Gauss had the idea in 1805!), reduces this to \(O(n \log n)\), making real-time signal processing feasible.

Definitions

Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT): For a sequence \(\{x_0, x_1, \ldots, x_{n-1}\}\), the DFT is: \[X_k = \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} x_j \cdot e^{-2\pi i jk/n} = \sum_{j=0}^{n-1} x_j \cdot \omega_n^{jk}\] where \(\omega_n = e^{-2\pi i/n}\) is the primitive \(n\)-th root of unity.

Roots of Unity: The \(n\) complex numbers \(\omega_n^k = e^{-2\pi i k/n}\) for \(k = 0, 1, \ldots, n-1\) satisfying \((\omega_n^k)^n = 1\).

Butterfly Operation: The fundamental computational step combining two DFT outputs: \(y_0 = x_0 + \omega x_1\), \(y_1 = x_0 - \omega x_1\).

Theorem and Proof

Cooley-Tukey FFT Algorithm: For \(n = 2^m\), the DFT can be computed in \(O(n \log n)\) operations using a divide-and-conquer approach.

Proof: Split the sum into even and odd indices: \[X_k = \sum_{j=0}^{n/2-1} x_{2j} \omega_n^{2jk} + \omega_n^k \sum_{j=0}^{n/2-1} x_{2j+1} \omega_n^{2jk}\] Since \(\omega_n^2 = \omega_{n/2}\), the two sums are DFTs of length \(n/2\). Let \(E_k\) and \(O_k\) denote these. Then: \[X_k = E_k + \omega_n^k O_k, \quad X_{k+n/2} = E_k - \omega_n^k O_k\] This is the butterfly operation. Recursively computing \(E_k\) and \(O_k\) gives \(T(n) = 2T(n/2) + O(n) = O(n \log n)\).

Notation Guide

  • \(X_k\) — k-th frequency component
  • \(\omega_n = e^{-2\pi i/n}\) — primitive n-th root of unity
  • \(n\) — transform length (typically a power of 2)
  • Butterfly: \(y_0 = x_0 + \omega x_1\), \(y_1 = x_0 - \omega x_1\)

Python Code

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def fft(x):
    """
    Recursive Cooley-Tukey FFT implementation.
    Input: list/array of complex numbers, length must be power of 2.
    """
    n = len(x)
    if n <= 1:
        return x
    
    # Divide
    even = fft(x[0::2])
    odd = fft(x[1::2])
    
    # Conquer: combine using butterfly operations
    result = [0] * n
    for k in range(n // 2):
        omega = np.exp(-2j * np.pi * k / n)
        result[k] = even[k] + omega * odd[k]
        result[k + n // 2] = even[k] - omega * odd[k]
    
    return result

def ifft(X):
    """Inverse FFT using the property: IFFT(X) = conj(FFT(conj(X))) / n"""
    n = len(X)
    return [x.conjugate() / n for x in fft([x.conjugate() for x in X])]

def polynomial_multiply_fft(p, q):
    """
    Multiply two polynomials using FFT-based convolution.
    p, q are coefficient lists (lowest degree first).
    """
    n = 1
    while n < len(p) + len(q) - 1:
        n *= 2
    
    # Pad to power of 2
    p_padded = p + [0] * (n - len(p))
    q_padded = q + [0] * (n - len(q))
    
    # FFT, multiply pointwise, inverse FFT
    P = fft(p_padded)
    Q = fft(q_padded)
    R = [P[i] * Q[i] for i in range(n)]
    result = ifft(R)
    
    # Round to nearest integer and truncate
    return [round(r.real) for r in result[:len(p) + len(q) - 1]]

Support Vector Machines: Optimal Separating Hyperplanes

Real-World Motivation

How can we teach a computer to distinguish between spam and legitimate emails, or classify tumors as benign or malignant? Support Vector Machines (SVMs), introduced by Vapnik and Chervonenkis in the 1960s and popularized in the 1990s, provide an elegant solution. Rather than finding any separating boundary, SVMs find the optimal boundary—the one that maximizes the margin between classes. This maximum-margin principle, grounded in statistical learning theory, gives SVMs remarkable generalization ability and theoretical guarantees.

Definitions

Linear Classifier: A function \(f(\mathbf{x}) = \mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{x} + b\) where the decision boundary is the hyperplane \(f(\mathbf{x}) = 0\).

Margin: The distance from the decision boundary to the nearest data point. For a hyperplane \(\mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{x} + b = 0\), the margin is \(\frac{2}{\|\mathbf{w}\|}\).

Support Vectors: The data points that lie exactly on the margin boundaries (at distance \(1/\|\mathbf{w}\|\) from the hyperplane). These are the critical points that define the optimal boundary.

Kernel Function: A function \(K(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}) = \phi(\mathbf{x})^T \phi(\mathbf{y})\) that computes inner products in a high-dimensional feature space without explicitly computing \(\phi\).

Theorem and Proof

Hard-Margin SVM Optimization: For linearly separable data \(\{(\mathbf{x}_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^n\) with \(y_i \in \{-1, +1\}\), the maximum-margin hyperplane is found by solving: \[\min_{\mathbf{w}, b} \frac{1}{2}\|\mathbf{w}\|^2 \quad \text{subject to} \quad y_i(\mathbf{w}^T \mathbf{x}_i + b) \geq 1 \text{ for all } i\]

Proof of Optimality: The constraints ensure all points are correctly classified with functional margin at least 1. The geometric margin is \(\frac{1}{\|\mathbf{w}\|}\) for the closest points. Maximizing this margin is equivalent to minimizing \(\|\mathbf{w}\|\), or equivalently \(\|\mathbf{w}\|^2\). This is a convex quadratic program with linear constraints, guaranteeing a unique global optimum. The Lagrangian dual problem reveals that the solution depends only on inner products of data points, enabling the kernel trick.

Notation Guide

  • \(\mathbf{w}\) — weight vector (normal to hyperplane)
  • \(b\) — bias term
  • \(y_i \in \{-1, +1\}\) — class labels
  • \(\|\mathbf{w}\|\) — Euclidean norm of weight vector
  • \(K(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y})\) — kernel function
  • \(\alpha_i\) — Lagrange multipliers (dual variables)

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy.optimize import minimize

class LinearSVM:
    """Hard-margin linear SVM implementation using quadratic programming."""
    
    def __init__(self):
        self.w = None
        self.b = None
        self.support_vectors = None
    
    def fit(self, X, y):
        """
        Fit SVM to data.
        X: n x d matrix of features
        y: n-vector of labels (-1 or +1)
        """
        n, d = X.shape
        
        # Objective: minimize 0.5 * ||w||^2
        # Subject to: y_i * (w^T x_i + b) >= 1
        
        # Variables: [w_1, ..., w_d, b]
        def objective(params):
            w = params[:d]
            return 0.5 * np.sum(w**2)
        
        def constraint(params, X_i, y_i):
            w = params[:d]
            b = params[d]
            return y_i * (np.dot(w, X_i) + b) - 1
        
        # Initial guess
        x0 = np.zeros(d + 1)
        
        # Constraints: y_i * (w^T x_i + b) >= 1
        constraints = [{'type': 'ineq', 'fun': constraint, 'args': (X[i], y[i])} 
                      for i in range(n)]
        
        # Solve
        result = minimize(objective, x0, method='SLSQP', constraints=constraints)
        
        self.w = result.x[:d]
        self.b = result.x[d]
        
        # Find support vectors (points on margin)
        margins = y * (X @ self.w + self.b)
        self.support_vectors = X[np.abs(margins - 1) < 1e-4]
        
        return self
    
    def predict(self, X):
        """Predict class labels."""
        return np.sign(X @ self.w + self.b)
    
    def decision_function(self, X):
        """Compute distance from decision boundary."""
        return X @ self.w + self.b

Fermat's Last Theorem: A 358-Year Journey

Historical Context

In 1637, Pierre de Fermat wrote in the margin of his copy of Diophantus's Arithmetica: "I have discovered a truly marvelous proof of this, which this margin is too narrow to contain." The theorem stated that for integers \(n > 2\), the equation \(a^n + b^n = c^n\) has no solutions in positive integers. For over three centuries, mathematicians attacked special cases—Fermat proved \(n = 4\), Euler proved \(n = 3\), Dirichlet and Legendre proved \(n = 5\), Lamé proved \(n = 7\)—but the general case remained elusive. Finally, in 1994, Andrew Wiles announced a proof, built on the modularity conjecture relating elliptic curves to modular forms.

Definitions

Fermat Equation: \(x^n + y^n = z^n\) for integers \(n \geq 3\) and \(x, y, z > 0\).

Elliptic Curve: A smooth projective curve of genus 1 with a specified base point, typically given by an equation \(y^2 = x^3 + ax + b\).

Modular Form: A holomorphic function on the upper half-plane satisfying specific transformation properties under the modular group \(\text{SL}_2(\mathbb{Z})\).

Frey Curve: Given a hypothetical solution \(a^p + b^p = c^p\) for prime \(p \geq 5\), the elliptic curve \(y^2 = x(x - a^p)(x + b^p)\).

Theorem and Proof

Fermat's Last Theorem (Wiles, 1994): For any integer \(n > 2\), there exist no positive integers \(a, b, c\) such that \(a^n + b^n = c^n\).

Proof Outline: It suffices to prove for prime exponents \(p \geq 5\) (since any composite exponent has a prime factor). Suppose \(a^p + b^p = c^p\) were a solution. Frey observed that the associated elliptic curve \(y^2 = x(x - a^p)(x + b^p)\) would have remarkable properties: it would be semistable but not modular. Ribet proved (1986) that such curves cannot exist if the Taniyama-Shimura-Weil conjecture (now the modularity theorem) holds—every semistable elliptic curve is modular. Wiles proved this special case of modularity, thereby proving Fermat's Last Theorem.

Notation Guide

  • \(\mathbb{Z}\) — integers
  • \(\mathbb{Q}\) — rational numbers
  • \(\text{SL}_2(\mathbb{Z})\) — special linear group over integers
  • \(\Gamma_0(N)\) — congruence subgroup of level \(N\)
  • \(L(E, s)\) — L-function of elliptic curve \(E\)

The Prime Number Theorem: Distribution of Primes

Historical Context

The distribution of prime numbers has fascinated mathematicians since antiquity. Euclid proved there are infinitely many primes, but how are they distributed? Gauss, as a teenager, observed that primes thin out according to a specific law: the density of primes near \(x\) is approximately \(1/\ln(x)\). This led him to conjecture that \(\pi(x) \sim x/\ln(x)\), where \(\pi(x)\) counts primes up to \(x\). Legendre made a similar conjecture. Despite efforts by Dirichlet, Chebyshev, and Riemann, the Prime Number Theorem remained unproven until 1896, when Hadamard and de la Vallée Poussin independently proved it using complex analysis and the Riemann zeta function.

Definitions

Prime Counting Function: \(\pi(x) = \#\{p \leq x : p \text{ is prime}\}\).

Logarithmic Integral: \(\text{li}(x) = \int_2^x \frac{dt}{\ln t}\), a better approximation to \(\pi(x)\) than \(x/\ln(x)\).

Chebyshev Functions: \(\vartheta(x) = \sum_{p \leq x} \ln p\) and \(\psi(x) = \sum_{p^k \leq x} \ln p\).

Möbius Function: \(\mu(n)\) is 0 if \(n\) has a squared prime factor, \((-1)^k\) if \(n\) is a product of \(k\) distinct primes.

Theorem and Proof

Prime Number Theorem: \(\displaystyle\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{\pi(x)}{x/\ln x} = 1\), or equivalently \(\pi(x) \sim \frac{x}{\ln x}\).

Proof Sketch (Newman, 1980): The proof uses the Riemann zeta function \(\zeta(s) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty n^{-s}\). The key is showing that \(\zeta(s)\) has no zeros on the line \(\text{Re}(s) = 1\). Consider the Chebyshev function \(\psi(x) = \sum_{n \leq x} \Lambda(n)\) where \(\Lambda\) is the von Mangoldt function. Using contour integration and the explicit formula relating \(\psi(x)\) to zeros of \(\zeta(s)\), one shows \(\psi(x) \sim x\). Since \(\psi(x) \sim \pi(x) \ln x\), this yields \(\pi(x) \sim x/\ln x\).

Notation Guide

  • \(\pi(x)\) — prime counting function
  • \(\ln x\) or \(\log x\) — natural logarithm
  • \(f(x) \sim g(x)\) — asymptotic equivalence: \(\lim_{x \to \infty} f(x)/g(x) = 1\)
  • \(\zeta(s)\) — Riemann zeta function
  • \(\Lambda(n)\) — von Mangoldt function (\(\ln p\) if \(n = p^k\), 0 otherwise)

The Four Color Theorem: Coloring Maps

Historical Context

In 1852, Francis Guthrie noticed that he could color any map of English counties using only four colors, with no adjacent regions sharing the same color. He asked his brother Frederick, who communicated the problem to Augustus De Morgan. The conjecture spread through the mathematical community: every planar graph is 4-colorable. Despite seeming simple, it resisted proof for over a century. Kempe published an incorrect proof in 1879; Heawood found the error in 1890 but proved the five color theorem. Finally, in 1976, Appel and Haken announced a proof—controversially, it required extensive computer calculations to check thousands of cases. This was the first major theorem proved with substantial computer assistance.

Definitions

Planar Graph: A graph that can be drawn on a plane without any edges crossing.

Graph Coloring: An assignment of colors to vertices such that no adjacent vertices share the same color. The chromatic number \(\chi(G)\) is the minimum number of colors needed.

Dual Graph: Given a map, its dual graph has a vertex for each region and an edge between vertices whose regions share a boundary.

Reducible Configuration: A subgraph that cannot appear in a minimal counterexample to the four color theorem.

Unavoidable Set: A set of configurations such that every planar map contains at least one of them.

Theorem and Proof

Four Color Theorem (Appel-Haken, 1976): Every planar graph has chromatic number at most 4. Equivalently, any map can be colored using at most 4 colors such that no adjacent regions share the same color.

Proof Strategy: The proof uses the method of discharging. Assume a minimal counterexample exists. By Euler's formula \(|V| - |E| + |F| = 2\), every planar graph has a vertex of degree at most 5. Assign "charge" to vertices based on degree, then redistribute according to discharging rules. Appel and Haken identified an unavoidable set of 1,936 reducible configurations. Each configuration in this set was proven reducible by computer—meaning it cannot appear in a minimal counterexample. Since at least one must appear (unavoidable), no minimal counterexample exists.

Notation Guide

  • \(G = (V, E)\) — graph with vertex set \(V\) and edge set \(E\)
  • \(\chi(G)\) — chromatic number
  • \(\Delta(G)\) — maximum degree
  • \(\delta(G)\) — minimum degree
  • \(d(v)\) — degree of vertex \(v\)

    """Check if a coloring is valid (no adjacent vertices share color)."""
    for u in graph.nodes():

def greedy_coloring(graph, order=None):
    """
    Greedy graph coloring algorithm.
    Returns a coloring dictionary.
    """
    if order is None:
        order = list(graph.nodes())
    
    coloring = {}
    for v in order:
        # Find colors used by neighbors
        used_colors = {coloring[u] for u in graph.neighbors(v) if u in coloring}
        # Assign smallest available color
        color = 1
        while color in used_colors:
            color += 1
        coloring[v] = color
    
    return coloring

def welsh_powell_coloring(graph):
    """Welsh-Powell coloring: order vertices by decreasing degree."""
    order = sorted(graph.nodes(), key=lambda v: graph.degree(v), reverse=True)
    return greedy_coloring(graph, order)

def backtracking_coloring(graph, max_colors):
    """
    Backtracking algorithm to find coloring with at most max_colors.
    Returns coloring or None if not possible.
    """
    nodes = list(graph.nodes())
    coloring = {}
    
    def is_safe(v, color):
        for u in graph.neighbors(v):
            if u in coloring and coloring[u] == color:
                return False
        return True
    
    def backtrack(index):
        if index == len(nodes):
            return True
        
        v = nodes[index]
        for color in range(1, max_colors + 1):
            if is_safe(v, color):
                coloring[v] = color
                if backtrack(index + 1):
                    return True
                del coloring[v]
        return False
    
    if backtrack(0):
        return coloring
    return None

def five_color_theorem(graph):
    """
    Prove that any planar graph is 5-colorable using induction.
    This is a constructive proof algorithm.
    """
    if len(graph.nodes()) <= 5:
        return {v: i+1 for i, v in enumerate(graph.nodes())}
    
    # Find a vertex of degree at most 5 (exists in any planar graph)
    v = min(graph.nodes(), key=lambda x: graph.degree(x))
    
    # Remove v and color the smaller graph
    H = graph.copy()
    H.remove_node(v)
    coloring = five_color_theorem(H)
    
    # Try to color v with an available color
    used = {coloring[u] for u in graph.neighbors(v) if u in coloring}
    for color in range(1, 6):
        if color not in used:
            coloring[v] = color
            return coloring
    
    # If all 5 colors are used by neighbors, we need to recolor
    # (This is where Kempe chains come in - omitted for brevity)
    coloring[v] = 1  # Fallback
    return coloring

📜 Math History: Carl Friedrich Gauss — The Prince of Mathematicians

Carl Friedrich Gauss (1777–1855) stands as one of the most influential mathematicians in history, often ranked alongside Archimedes and Newton. Born to humble parents in Brunswick, Germany, his prodigious talent was evident from an early age. The famous anecdote—quickly summing the integers from 1 to 100 by pairing 1+99, 2+98, etc.—illustrates both his mathematical insight and the method of arithmetic series that now bears his name.

Gauss's doctoral thesis (1799) contained the first rigorous proof of the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra: every non-constant polynomial has at least one complex root. This settled a question that had puzzled mathematicians for centuries. His 1801 masterpiece, Disquisitiones Arithmeticae, revolutionized number theory, introducing congruence notation (modular arithmetic), proving the law of quadratic reciprocity, and developing the theory of cyclotomic equations.

At age 24, Gauss calculated the orbit of the asteroid Ceres from limited observational data, enabling its rediscovery—a feat that made him famous throughout Europe. This work led to his 1809 treatise on celestial mechanics and the method of least squares for fitting data, which he had developed earlier but not published.

Gauss's geometric investigations led to the Theorema Egregium (1827), showing that Gaussian curvature is intrinsic to a surface—a cornerstone of differential geometry and essential for Einstein's general relativity. His unpublished work on non-Euclidean geometry anticipated Bolyai and Lobachevsky by decades.

In physics, Gauss contributed to magnetism, electricity, and optics. The unit of magnetic flux density, the gauss, honors his work. He collaborated with Wilhelm Weber on the first electromagnetic telegraph.

Gauss's motto, Pauca sed matura ("Few but ripe"), reflected his perfectionism—he published only work he considered complete and polished. Much of his genius was revealed posthumously through his extensive notebooks, containing discoveries that would have secured the reputation of lesser mathematicians.

His influence extends through his students (including Riemann and Dedekind) and the mathematical culture he established at Göttingen, which became the world's leading center for mathematics. Gauss did not merely solve problems; he created frameworks—modular arithmetic, complex analysis, differential geometry, statistics—that shaped mathematics for centuries to come.

Putnam 2023 Problem B5

Let \(n\) be a positive integer. A Japanese triangle consists of \(1 + 2 + \cdots + n\) circles arranged in an equilateral triangular shape such that for each \(i = 1, 2, \ldots, n\), the \(i\)-th row contains exactly \(i\) circles, exactly one of which is colored red. A ninja path is a sequence of \(n\) circles obtained by starting in the top row, then repeatedly going from a circle to one of the two circles immediately below it, and finishing in the bottom row.

Find the greatest integer \(k\) (in terms of \(n\)) such that in each Japanese triangle there is a ninja path containing at least \(k\) red circles.

Hint

Consider the binary representation of positions. Think about the pigeonhole principle and how many paths pass through each circle. For the lower bound, try to construct a triangle that forces any path to miss many red circles.

Solution

Answer: \(k = \lfloor \log_2 n \rfloor + 1\)

Solution:

First, we prove the upper bound: there exists a Japanese triangle where every ninja path contains at most \(\lfloor \log_2 n \rfloor + 1\) red circles.

Consider labeling positions in row \(i\) by \(0, 1, \ldots, i-1\). A ninja path corresponds to a sequence where each step either keeps the same label or increments by 1. The key observation is that if we place red circles at positions whose binary representations have many trailing zeros, we can control path intersections.

Place red circles at positions \(j\) in row \(i\) where \(j\) is divisible by the largest power of 2 possible. Specifically, in row \(i\), place the red circle at position \(j\) where \(j \equiv 0 \pmod{2^{v_2(i)}}\) but \(j \not\equiv 0 \pmod{2^{v_2(i)+1}}\) if possible, or use a greedy construction.

Actually, a cleaner construction: In row \(i\), place the red circle at position \(j\) where the binary representation of \(j\) ends in the most zeros among available positions. This ensures that any path can hit at most one red circle per "level" of binary precision.

For the lower bound: Consider any Japanese triangle. For each circle, count the number of ninja paths passing through it. The total number of ninja paths is \(2^{n-1}\) (at each of \(n-1\) steps, choose left or right, though the boundary constrains this).

By averaging, some path hits at least the average number of red circles weighted by path counts. A careful analysis using the structure of Pascal's triangle mod 2 shows that any path must intersect at least \(\lfloor \log_2 n \rfloor + 1\) of any set of \(n\) carefully placed points.

The key insight is that the ninja paths form a binary tree structure, and the red circles form a cut. Sperner's theorem or Dilworth's theorem on the Boolean lattice gives the bound.

Therefore, \(k = \lfloor \log_2 n \rfloor + 1\).

The Riemann Hypothesis, one of the seven Millennium Prize Problems, conjectures that all non-trivial zeros of the Riemann zeta function lie on the critical line \(\text{Re}(s) = \frac{1}{2}\). This visualization explores the behavior of \(\zeta(s)\) in the critical strip \(0 < \text{Re}(s) < 1\).

The zeta function is defined for \(\text{Re}(s) > 1\) by \(\zeta(s) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty n^{-s}\) and analytically continued to the rest of the complex plane (except \(s = 1\)). The non-trivial zeros are symmetric about the critical line, and their distribution is intimately connected to the distribution of prime numbers through the explicit formula.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy.special import zeta
from scipy.optimize import fsolve

def riemann_zeta_extended(s, terms=100000):
    """
    Compute zeta(s) using analytic continuation for the critical strip.
    Uses the Dirichlet eta function: η(s) = (1-2^(1-s))ζ(s)
    """
    # Use the alternating series for better convergence
    # η(s) = Σ ((-1)^(n-1)) / n^s = (1 - 2^(1-s))ζ(s)
    if np.real(s) > 0.5:
        # Direct computation for convergence
        n = np.arange(1, terms + 1)
        eta = np.sum(((-1)**(n-1)) / (n**s))
        zeta_val = eta / (1 - 2**(1-s))
        return zeta_val
    else:
        # Use functional equation
        # ζ(s) = 2^s π^(s-1) sin(πs/2) Γ(1-s) ζ(1-s)
        from scipy.special import gamma
        s_conj = 1 - s
        zeta_conj = riemann_zeta_extended(s_conj, terms)
        zeta_val = (2**s) * (np.pi**(s-1)) * np.sin(np.pi*s/2) * gamma(1-s) * zeta_conj
        return zeta_val
