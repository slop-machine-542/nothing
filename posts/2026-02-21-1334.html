🔬 Pure Math Deep-Dives

Spectral Theory: The Harmonic Analysis of Operators

spectral-theory

Motivation

Spectral theory generalizes the eigenvalue decomposition of matrices to infinite-dimensional spaces. Just as a symmetric matrix can be diagonalized, self-adjoint operators on Hilbert spaces admit a spectral decomposition. This theory, developed by Hilbert, von Neumann, and others, provides the mathematical foundation for quantum mechanics, where observables correspond to self-adjoint operators and their spectra represent possible measurement outcomes. Beyond physics, spectral theory underlies the analysis of differential equations, the study of vibrations in mechanical systems, and the convergence of numerical methods. The spectral theorem reveals that every self-adjoint operator is unitarily equivalent to a multiplication operator, making abstract operators concrete and computable.

Definitions

  • Resolvent Set: For a linear operator \( T \) on a Banach space, \(\rho(T) = \{\lambda \in \mathbb{C} : (T - \lambda I)^{-1} \text{ exists and is bounded}\}\).
  • Spectrum: \(\sigma(T) = \mathbb{C} \setminus \rho(T)\). The spectrum decomposes into point spectrum (eigenvalues), continuous spectrum, and residual spectrum.
  • Spectral Radius: \(r(T) = \sup\{|\lambda| : \lambda \in \sigma(T)\} = \lim_{n \to \infty} \|T^n\|^{1/n}\).
  • Self-Adjoint Operator: On a Hilbert space \( \mathcal{H} \), an operator \( T \) satisfying \( \langle Tx, y \rangle = \langle x, Ty \rangle \) for all \( x, y \in \mathcal{D}(T) \).
  • Projection-Valued Measure: A map \( E \) from Borel sets to orthogonal projections satisfying \( E(\emptyset) = 0 \), \( E(\mathbb{R}) = I \), and countable additivity.
  • Essential Spectrum: The spectrum modulo compact perturbations: \(\sigma_{\text{ess}}(T) = \{\lambda : T - \lambda I \text{ is not Fredholm}\}\).

Theorem: The Spectral Theorem for Self-Adjoint Operators

Theorem: Let \( T \) be a self-adjoint operator on a separable Hilbert space \( \mathcal{H} \). Then there exists a unique projection-valued measure \( E \) on \( \mathbb{R} \) such that: \[T = \int_{\mathbb{R}} \lambda \, dE(\lambda)\] Moreover, for any bounded Borel function \( f \): \[f(T) = \int_{\mathbb{R}} f(\lambda) \, dE(\lambda)\] and the domain of \( T \) is: \[\mathcal{D}(T) = \left\{x \in \mathcal{H} : \int_{\mathbb{R}} \lambda^2 \, d\|E(\lambda)x\|^2 \lt \infty\right\}\]

Proof Sketch

The proof proceeds in stages. First, for a bounded self-adjoint operator, one constructs a continuous functional calculus using the Stone-Weierstrass theorem. This extends to bounded Borel functions via the Riesz representation theorem. The projection-valued measure is defined by \( E(\Omega) = \chi_\Omega(T) \) for Borel sets \( \Omega \).

For unbounded operators, one uses the Cayley transform \( U = (T - iI)(T + iI)^{-1} \), which is unitary. The spectral theorem for unitary operators (proved via the Herglotz representation) yields the spectral measure for \( T \). The functional calculus formula follows from approximating \( f \) by simple functions and using the properties of the spectral integral.

Notation Guide

  • \(\mathcal{H}\): Hilbert space
  • \(\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})\): Bounded linear operators on \(\mathcal{H}\)
  • \(\mathcal{D}(T)\): Domain of operator \(T\)
  • \(\sigma(T)\): Spectrum of \(T\)
  • \(\sigma_p(T)\): Point spectrum (eigenvalues)
  • \(\sigma_c(T)\): Continuous spectrum
  • \(\rho(T)\): Resolvent set
  • \(R(\lambda, T) = (T - \lambda I)^{-1}\): Resolvent operator
  • \(E(\lambda)\): Spectral projection
  • \(L^2(\mathbb{R}, d\mu)\): Space of square-integrable functions with respect to measure \(\mu\)

Python Code

import numpy as np
from numpy.linalg import eig, inv
from scipy.linalg import expm

class SpectralDecomposition:
    """Computes and analyzes spectral decomposition of matrices."""
    
    def __init__(self, A):
        """Initialize with a Hermitian matrix A."""
        self.A = np.array(A, dtype=complex)
        if not np.allclose(self.A, self.A.conj().T):
            raise ValueError("Matrix must be Hermitian (self-adjoint)")
        
        # Compute eigendecomposition
        self.eigenvalues, self.eigenvectors = eig(self.A)
        # Sort by eigenvalue
        idx = np.argsort(self.eigenvalues)
        self.eigenvalues = self.eigenvalues[idx]
        self.eigenvectors = self.eigenvectors[:, idx]
    
    def spectral_projector(self, a, b):
        """Compute spectral projector E([a, b])."""
        mask = (self.eigenvalues >= a) & (self.eigenvalues <= b)
        if not np.any(mask):
            return np.zeros_like(self.A)
        
        P = np.zeros_like(self.A)
        for i in np.where(mask)[0]:
            v = self.eigenvectors[:, i:i+1]
            P += v @ v.conj().T
        return P
    
    def functional_calculus(self, f):
        """Compute f(A) = sum f(lambda_i) |v_i><v_i|."""
        result = np.zeros_like(self.A)
        for i, lam in enumerate(self.eigenvalues):
            v = self.eigenvectors[:, i:i+1]
            result += f(lam) * (v @ v.conj().T)
        return result

# Example
A = np.array([[2, 1, 0], [1, 3, 1], [0, 1, 2]], dtype=complex)
spec = SpectralDecomposition(A)
print("Eigenvalues:", spec.eigenvalues.real)

Modular Forms: Symmetry and Arithmetic

modular-forms

Motivation

Modular forms are complex-analytic functions with remarkable symmetry properties under the action of the modular group. They occupy a central position in modern mathematics, connecting number theory, algebraic geometry, representation theory, and physics. The proof of Fermat's Last Theorem by Andrew Wiles relied crucially on the connection between elliptic curves and modular forms. In physics, modular forms appear in string theory and the study of partition functions.

Definitions

  • Modular Group: \(\Gamma = \text{SL}(2, \mathbb{Z})\).
  • Action on Upper Half-Plane: \(\gamma \cdot \tau = \frac{a\tau + b}{c\tau + d}\)
  • Modular Form of Weight \(k\): A holomorphic function satisfying \(f(\gamma \cdot \tau) = (c\tau + d)^k f(\tau)\)
  • Eisenstein Series: \(G_k(\tau) = \sum_{(m,n) \neq (0,0)} \frac{1}{(m\tau + n)^k}\)

Theorem: Dimension Formula

Theorem: For even \(k \geq 2\): \[\dim M_k = \begin{cases} \left\lfloor \frac{k}{12} \right\rfloor & k \equiv 2 \pmod{12} \\ \left\lfloor \frac{k}{12} \right\rfloor + 1 & k \not\equiv 2 \pmod{12} \end{cases}\]

Lie Algebras: Infinitesimal Symmetries

lie-algebras

Motivation

Lie algebras are algebraic structures that capture the infinitesimal structure of Lie groups—continuous groups of symmetries. While Lie groups describe global symmetries, Lie algebras encode the local, linearized structure near the identity. This correspondence allows the study of nonlinear group theory through linear algebra.

Definitions

  • Lie Algebra: A vector space with bilinear bracket satisfying antisymmetry and Jacobi identity
  • Killing Form: \(\kappa(x, y) = \text{tr}(\text{ad}_x \circ \text{ad}_y)\)
  • Semisimple: Non-degenerate Killing form

Theorem: Cartan's Classification

Theorem: Every finite-dimensional simple Lie algebra over \(\mathbb{C}\) is isomorphic to one of: \(A_n, B_n, C_n, D_n\) (classical) or \(E_6, E_7, E_8, F_4, G_2\) (exceptional).

📜 Math History: Leonhard Euler and the Birth of Modern Analysis

Leonhard Euler stands as one of the most influential mathematicians in history, with his work touching virtually every branch of mathematics. Born in Basel, Switzerland in 1707, Euler spent most of his career in St. Petersburg and Berlin, producing over 800 papers and books despite losing his sight in later years.

Euler's contributions to analysis were revolutionary. He systematized the calculus, introduced the concept of functions as we understand them today, and established the notation \(f(x)\) that we still use. His work on infinite series, including the famous Basel problem solution \(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n^2} = \frac{\pi^2}{6}\), demonstrated the power of analytic methods.

Beyond analysis, Euler founded graph theory with his solution to the Königsberg bridge problem, made fundamental contributions to number theory through his work on the zeta function, and developed the theory of differential equations. His formula \(V - E + F = 2\) for polyhedra established the field of topology.

Euler's influence extends to notation: he introduced \(e\) for the base of natural logarithms, \(i\) for the imaginary unit, and \(\pi\) for the circle constant. His identity \(e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta\) unifies exponential and trigonometric functions, revealing deep connections in complex analysis.

🧩 Problem Corner

IMO 2023 Problem 2

Let \(ABC\) be an acute-angled triangle with \(AB \lt AC\). Let \(\Omega\) be the circumcircle of \(ABC\). Let \(S\) be the midpoint of the arc \(CB\) of \(\Omega\) containing \(A\). The perpendicular from \(A\) to \(BC\) meets \(BS\) at \(D\) and meets \(\Omega\) again at \(E \neq A\). The line through \(D\) parallel to \(BC\) meets line \(BE\) at \(L\). Denote the circumcircle of triangle \(BDL\) by \(\omega\). Let \(\omega\) meet \(\Omega\) again at \(P \neq B\). Prove that the line tangent to \(\omega\) at \(P\) passes through the midpoint of \(AS\).

Hint

Consider the spiral similarity centered at \(B\) that sends \(D\) to \(L\). Show that this similarity also sends \(S\) to \(A\).

Solution

The key observation is that \(BDSL\) is cyclic by construction. Using angle chasing and properties of the spiral similarity, we can show that \(P\) lies on the circle with diameter \(AS\). The tangent condition then follows from the power of a point and the fact that the tangent at \(P\) is perpendicular to the radius of \(\omega\).

Modular Group Action on the Upper Half-Plane

The modular group \(\text{SL}(2, \mathbb{Z})\) acts on the upper half-plane \(\mathbb{H} = \{z \in \mathbb{C} : \text{Im}(z) \gt 0\}\) by fractional linear transformations. The fundamental domain for this action is a key object in the theory of modular forms.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def modular_transform(z, a, b, c, d):
    """Apply transformation z -> (az+b)/(cz+d)"""
    return (a*z + b) / (c*z + d)

# Create grid in upper half-plane
x = np.linspace(-2, 2, 400)
y = np.linspace(0.1, 3, 300)
X, Y = np.meshgrid(x, y)
Z = X + 1j*Y

# Apply S: z -> -1/z
S = -1/Z

# Plot
