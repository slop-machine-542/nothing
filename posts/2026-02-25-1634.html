Commutative Algebra: The Algebra of Geometry

commutative-algebra

Motivation

Commutative algebra is the study of commutative rings and their ideals, providing the algebraic foundation for algebraic geometry and number theory. The subject emerged from the work of David Hilbert and Emmy Noether in the early 20th century, who established the modern axiomatic approach. The deep connection between algebra and geometry, expressed through the correspondence between rings and their spectra, allows geometric intuition to guide algebraic proofs and algebraic machinery to solve geometric problems. Commutative algebra is essential for understanding schemes, cohomology theories, and modern algebraic geometry.

Definitions

  • Commutative Ring: A ring \(R\) where multiplication is commutative: \(ab = ba\) for all \(a, b \in R\).
  • Ideal: A subset \(I \subseteq R\) closed under addition and under multiplication by arbitrary elements of \(R\).
  • Prime Ideal: An ideal \(\mathfrak{p}\) such that if \(ab \in \mathfrak{p}\), then \(a \in \mathfrak{p}\) or \(b \in \mathfrak{p}\). Equivalently, \(R/\mathfrak{p}\) is an integral domain.
  • Maximal Ideal: An ideal \(\mathfrak{m}\) that is maximal among proper ideals. Equivalently, \(R/\mathfrak{m}\) is a field.
  • Nilradical: The intersection of all prime ideals, equal to \(\{x \in R : x^n = 0 \text{ for some } n\}\).
  • Jacobson Radical: The intersection of all maximal ideals.
  • Noetherian Ring: A ring satisfying the ascending chain condition on ideals: every increasing chain of ideals stabilizes.

Theorem: The Nullstellensatz

Theorem (Hilbert's Nullstellensatz): Let \(k\) be an algebraically closed field and \(I \subseteq k[x_1, \ldots, x_n]\) be an ideal. Let \(V(I) = \{x \in k^n : f(x) = 0 \text{ for all } f \in I\}\) be the variety of \(I\). Then:

\[I(V(I)) = \sqrt{I}\]

where \(I(V) = \{f \in k[x_1, \ldots, x_n] : f(x) = 0 \text{ for all } x \in V\}\) and \(\sqrt{I} = \{f : f^n \in I \text{ for some } n\}\) is the radical of \(I\).

Weak Nullstellensatz: If \(I\) is a proper ideal, then \(V(I) \neq \emptyset\).

Proof Sketch

The proof uses Noether normalization and the fact that finitely generated algebras over fields are Jacobson rings. Key steps:

  1. First prove the weak form: if \(I\) is maximal, then \(k[x_1, \ldots, x_n]/I \cong k\) (since \(k\) is algebraically closed).
  2. This implies \(I = (x_1 - a_1, \ldots, x_n - a_n)\) for some point \((a_1, \ldots, a_n) \in k^n\).
  3. For the strong form, use the Rabinowitsch trick: introduce a new variable \(y\) and consider \(J = I + (1 - yf) \subseteq k[x_1, \ldots, x_n, y]\).
  4. If \(f\) vanishes on \(V(I)\) but \(f \notin \sqrt{I}\), then \(J\) has no zeros, contradicting the weak form.

Notation Guide

  • \(R\): Commutative ring with unity
  • \(\text{Spec}(R)\): Prime spectrum (set of prime ideals with Zariski topology)
  • \(\text{Max}(R)\): Maximal spectrum
  • \(R_\mathfrak{p}\): Localization at prime \(\mathfrak{p}\)
  • \(R_f\): Localization at element \(f\)
  • \(\text{ht}(\mathfrak{p})\): Height of prime ideal (length of longest chain)
  • \(\dim(R)\): Krull dimension
  • \(k[x_1, \ldots, x_n]\): Polynomial ring over field \(k\)

Homological Algebra: Measuring Failure of Exactness

homological-algebra

Motivation

Homological algebra studies algebraic structures and their invariants using sequences and chain complexes. Born from algebraic topology in the 1940s through the work of Eilenberg, Mac Lane, and Cartan, it provides a unified framework for understanding derived functors, cohomology theories, and obstructions in algebraic geometry and topology. The central insight is that "exactness" is a fundamental property of nice algebraic constructions, and the failure of exactness—measured by homology and cohomology—contains deep structural information. Modern homological algebra is essential for derived categories, spectral sequences, and intersection theory.

Definitions

  • Chain Complex: A sequence of modules \(\cdots \xrightarrow{d_{n+1}} C_n \xrightarrow{d_n} C_{n-1} \xrightarrow{d_{n-1}} \cdots\) with \(d_n \circ d_{n+1} = 0\).
  • Cycles: \(Z_n = \ker(d_n) = \{c \in C_n : d_n(c) = 0\}\).
  • Boundaries: \(B_n = \text{im}(d_{n+1}) = \{d_{n+1}(c') : c' \in C_{n+1}\}\).
  • Homology: \(H_n(C) = Z_n / B_n\), measuring the failure of exactness at \(C_n\).
  • Exact Sequence: A chain complex with \(H_n(C) = 0\) for all \(n\), i.e., \(\text{im}(d_{n+1}) = \ker(d_n)\).
  • Short Exact Sequence: \(0 \to A \xrightarrow{f} B \xrightarrow{g} C \to 0\) where \(f\) is injective, \(g\) is surjective, and \(\text{im}(f) = \ker(g)\).
  • Derived Functors: Functors like \(\text{Ext}^n\) and \(\text{Tor}_n\) that measure the failure of \(\text{Hom}\) and \(\otimes\) to be exact.

Theorem: The Long Exact Sequence in Homology

Theorem: Given a short exact sequence of chain complexes:

\[0 \to A_\bullet \xrightarrow{f} B_\bullet \xrightarrow{g} C_\bullet \to 0\]

There exists a natural long exact sequence in homology:

\[\cdots \to H_n(A) \xrightarrow{f_*} H_n(B) \xrightarrow{g_*} H_n(C) \xrightarrow{\partial} H_{n-1}(A) \to \cdots\]

where the connecting homomorphism \(\partial\) is defined by \(\partial([c]) = [a]\) where \(f(a) = d_B(b)\) for any \(b\) with \(g(b) = c\).

Proof Sketch

The proof uses the Snake Lemma applied to the commutative diagram relating the short exact sequences of cycles and boundaries. Key steps:

  1. Construct the commutative diagram with rows being the short exact sequences of cycles and boundaries.
  2. Apply the Snake Lemma to obtain the connecting homomorphism.
  3. Verify exactness at each term by diagram chasing: if an element maps to zero, it comes from the previous term.
  4. Naturality follows from the functoriality of the construction.

Notation Guide

  • \(C_\bullet\) or \(C_*\): Chain complex
  • \(d_n\) or \(\partial_n\): Differential/boundary map
  • \(H_n(C)\): nth homology group
  • \(H^n(C)\): nth cohomology group
  • \(\text{Ext}^n_R(M, N)\): nth derived functor of Hom
  • \(\text{Tor}_n^R(M, N)\): nth derived functor of tensor product
  • \(\chi(C) = \sum (-1)^n \text{rank}(C_n)\): Euler characteristic

Symplectic Geometry: The Geometry of Classical Mechanics

symplectic-geometry

Motivation

Symplectic geometry is the mathematical framework for classical mechanics, providing a coordinate-free formulation of Hamilton's equations. The subject originated in the 19th century with the work of Hamilton and Jacobi, but its modern geometric formulation emerged in the 1960s through the work of Arnold, Smale, and others. Unlike Riemannian geometry which measures distances and angles, symplectic geometry measures areas and volumes in phase space. It reveals deep topological constraints on mechanical systems and has surprising connections to algebraic geometry, gauge theory, and low-dimensional topology through mirror symmetry and Floer homology.

Definitions

  • Symplectic Form: A closed, non-degenerate 2-form \(\omega\) on a manifold \(M\), i.e., \(d\omega = 0\) and \(\omega^n \neq 0\) everywhere.
  • Symplectic Manifold: A pair \((M, \omega)\) where \(M\) is a smooth manifold and \(\omega\) is a symplectic form. \(\dim(M) = 2n\) must be even.
  • Hamiltonian Vector Field: For \(H: M \to \mathbb{R}\), the vector field \(X_H\) defined by \(\omega(X_H, \cdot) = dH\).
  • Poisson Bracket: \(\{f, g\} = \omega(X_f, X_g) = X_f(g)\), measuring how observables evolve.
  • Lagrangian Submanifold: A submanifold \(L \subset M\) with \(\dim(L) = n\) and \(\omega|_L = 0\).
  • Symplectomorphism: A diffeomorphism \(\phi: M \to M\) preserving \(\omega\), i.e., \(\phi^*\omega = \omega\).

Theorem: Darboux's Theorem

Theorem (Darboux): Let \((M, \omega)\) be a symplectic manifold of dimension \(2n\). For any point \(p \in M\), there exist local coordinates \((q_1, \ldots, q_n, p_1, \ldots, p_n)\), called Darboux coordinates, such that:

\[\omega = \sum_{i=1}^n dq_i \wedge dp_i\]

In these coordinates, Hamilton's equations take the standard form:

\[\dot{q}_i = \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i}, \quad \dot{p}_i = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial q_i}\]

Corollary: All symplectic manifolds of the same dimension are locally symplectomorphic—there are no local invariants unlike Riemannian geometry.

Proof Sketch

The proof uses Moser's trick: construct a family of symplectic forms and find a vector field generating isotopies between them. Key steps:

  1. At point \(p\), choose a basis where \(\omega_p = \sum dq_i \wedge dp_i\).
  2. Define \(\omega_t = (1-t)\omega_0 + t\omega\) where \(\omega_0\) is the standard form in these coordinates.
  3. Show \(\omega_t\) remains non-degenerate near \(p\) for all \(t \in [0,1]\).
  4. Since \(\omega_t\) is closed and \([\omega_t]\) is constant, \(\frac{d\omega_t}{dt} = d\sigma\) for some 1-form \(\sigma\).
  5. Find vector field \(X_t\) with \(\iota_{X_t}\omega_t = -\sigma\) and integrate to get the symplectomorphism.

Notation Guide

  • \((M, \omega)\): Symplectic manifold
  • \(\omega \in \Omega^2(M)\): Symplectic form
  • \(X_H\): Hamiltonian vector field
  • \(\{f, g\}\): Poisson bracket
  • \((q, p)\): Canonical/Darboux coordinates
  • \(\phi_H^t\): Hamiltonian flow for time \(t\)
  • \(\text{Sp}(2n, \mathbb{R})\): Symplectic group
  • \(L \subset M\): Lagrangian submanifold

def symplectic_gradient(H_func, q, p, h=1e-5):
    """
    Compute Hamiltonian vector field X_H = (∂H/∂p, -∂H/∂q)
    """
    n = len(q)
    dH_dq = np.zeros(n)
    dH_dp = np.zeros(n)
    
    for i in range(n):
        q_plus, q_minus = q.copy(), q.copy()
        q_plus[i] += h
        q_minus[i] -= h
        dH_dq[i] = (H_func(q_plus, p) - H_func(q_minus, p)) / (2*h)
        
        p_plus, p_minus = p.copy(), p.copy()
        p_plus[i] += h
        p_minus[i] -= h
        dH_dp[i] = (H_func(q, p_plus) - H_func(q, p_minus)) / (2*h)
    
    # Hamilton's equations: dq/dt = ∂H/∂p, dp/dt = -∂H/∂q
    return dH_dp, -dH_dq

def hamiltonian_flow(H_func, q0, p0, t_span, dt=0.01):
    """
    Integrate Hamilton's equations using symplectic Euler method.
    """
    n_steps = int((t_span[1] - t_span[0]) / dt)
    q, p = np.zeros((n_steps+1, len(q0))), np.zeros((n_steps+1, len(p0)))
    q[0], p[0] = q0, p0
    
    for i in range(n_steps):
        # Symplectic Euler (semi-implicit)
        dq, dp = symplectic_gradient(H_func, q[i], p[i])
        p[i+1] = p[i] + dt * dp
        q[i+1] = q[i] + dt * symplectic_gradient(H_func, q[i], p[i+1])[0]
    
    return q, p

Game Theory: Strategic Decision Making

game-theory

Real-World Motivation

Game theory provides the mathematical foundation for understanding strategic interactions among rational agents. From auction design and market mechanisms to nuclear deterrence and evolutionary biology, game theory models situations where the outcome depends on the choices of multiple participants. John von Neumann and Oskar Morgenstern's 1944 book "Theory of Games and Economic Behavior" established the field, while John Nash's equilibrium concept became central to economics. Modern applications include algorithmic game theory, mechanism design for online platforms, and understanding cooperation in biological systems.

Definitions

  • Normal Form Game: A tuple \(G = (N, (A_i)_{i \in N}, (u_i)_{i \in N})\) where \(N\) is the set of players, \(A_i\) is player \(i\)'s action set, and \(u_i: A \to \mathbb{R}\) is the payoff function.
  • Strategy: A complete plan of action. Pure strategy selects one action; mixed strategy is a probability distribution over actions.
  • Nash Equilibrium: A strategy profile \(\sigma^*\) where no player can benefit by unilaterally changing strategy: \(u_i(\sigma_i^*, \sigma_{-i}^*) \geq u_i(\sigma_i, \sigma_{-i}^*)\) for all \(i\) and all \(\sigma_i\).
  • Dominant Strategy: A strategy that is optimal regardless of what opponents do.
  • Pareto Optimality: An outcome where no player can be made better off without making another worse off.
  • Extensive Form: A game tree representation showing sequential moves and information sets.

Theorem: Nash's Existence Theorem

Theorem (Nash, 1950): Every finite normal-form game has at least one Nash equilibrium in mixed strategies.

Proof Ingredients:

  1. The set of mixed strategy profiles is a compact, convex subset of a Euclidean space.
  2. Define the best response correspondence \(BR_i(\sigma_{-i}) = \arg\max_{\sigma_i} u_i(\sigma_i, \sigma_{-i})\).
  3. The combined best response \(BR(\sigma) = \prod_i BR_i(\sigma_{-i})\) is upper hemicontinuous with nonempty convex values.
  4. By Kakutani's fixed-point theorem, \(BR\) has a fixed point, which is a Nash equilibrium.

Notation Guide

  • \(N = \{1, \ldots, n\}\): Set of players
  • \(A_i\): Action set for player \(i\)
  • \(A = A_1 \times \cdots \times A_n\): Action profiles
  • \(\Delta(A_i)\): Set of mixed strategies for player \(i\)
  • \(\sigma_{-i}\): Strategies of all players except \(i\)
  • \(u_i: A \to \mathbb{R}\): Payoff function
  • \(BR_i\): Best response correspondence
  • \(\varepsilon\)-Nash: No player can gain more than \(\varepsilon\) by deviating

    # u_B(L) = p*B[0,0] + (1-p)*B[1,0]
    # u_B(R) = p*B[0,1] + (1-p)*B[1,1]
    # B is indifferent when u_B(L) = u_B(R)
    
    B = payoff_B
    denom_p = B[0,0] - B[0,1] - B[1,0] + B[1,1]
    if abs(denom_p) > 1e-10:
        p = (B[1,1] - B[1,0]) / denom_p
    else:
        p = 0.5  # Degenerate case

    denom_q = A[0,0] - A[1,0] - A[0,1] + A[1,1]
    if abs(denom_q) > 1e-10:
        q = (A[1,1] - A[0,1]) / denom_q
    else:
        q = 0.5
    
    # Clip to valid probabilities
    p = np.clip(p, 0, 1)
    q = np.clip(q, 0, 1)
    
    return p, q

Control Theory: Taming Dynamical Systems

control-theory

Real-World Motivation

Control theory provides the mathematical framework for designing systems that maintain desired behavior despite disturbances and uncertainties. From autopilots in aircraft and cruise control in cars to the regulation of power grids and the control of robotic systems, control theory is essential for modern technology. The field combines differential equations, linear algebra, optimization, and probability to analyze stability, design feedback controllers, and ensure robust performance. Emerging applications include autonomous vehicles, smart grids, and the control of quantum systems.

Definitions

  • State-Space Representation: \(\dot{x} = Ax + Bu\), \(y = Cx + Du\) where \(x\) is state, \(u\) is input, \(y\) is output.
  • Controllability: The system is controllable if for any initial state \(x_0\) and target state \(x_f\), there exists an input \(u(t)\) that drives the system from \(x_0\) to \(x_f\) in finite time.
  • Observability: The system is observable if the initial state \(x(0)\) can be determined from the output \(y(t)\) over a finite time interval.
  • Stability: The system is asymptotically stable if all eigenvalues of \(A\) have negative real parts.
  • Feedback Control: A control law \(u = -Kx\) that stabilizes the system by placing closed-loop poles.
  • Lyapunov Function: A positive definite function \(V(x)\) with \(\dot{V}(x) < 0\) along trajectories, proving stability.

Theorem: The Pole Placement Theorem

Theorem: Consider the linear system \(\dot{x} = Ax + Bu\). If \((A, B)\) is controllable, then for any desired set of eigenvalues \(\{\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_n\}\) (closed under complex conjugation), there exists a feedback matrix \(K\) such that the closed-loop system \(\dot{x} = (A - BK)x\) has these eigenvalues.

Controllability Criterion: The pair \((A, B)\) is controllable if and only if the controllability matrix \(\mathcal{C} = [B \ AB \ A^2B \ \cdots \ A^{n-1}B]\) has full rank \(n\).

Proof Sketch

The proof uses the controllable canonical form. For single-input systems:

  1. Transform to coordinates where \(A\) is in companion form with characteristic polynomial coefficients as the last row.
  2. In these coordinates, \(B = e_n\) (the last standard basis vector).
  3. Feedback \(K\) modifies the last row of \(A\), directly setting the characteristic polynomial coefficients.
  4. Transform back to original coordinates to get the actual feedback matrix.

Notation Guide

  • \(x \in \mathbb{R}^n\): State vector
  • \(u \in \mathbb{R}^m\): Control input
  • \(y \in \mathbb{R}^p\): Output/measurement
  • \(A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}\): System matrix
  • \(B \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}\): Input matrix
  • \(K\): Feedback gain matrix
  • \(\mathcal{C}\): Controllability matrix
  • \(\mathcal{O}\): Observability matrix
  • \(P\): Solution to Lyapunov or Riccati equation

from scipy.integrate import odeint

def check_controllability(A, B):
    """Check if (A, B) is controllable."""
    n = A.shape[0]
    C = B
    for i in range(1, n):
        C = np.hstack([C, np.linalg.matrix_power(A, i) @ B])
    rank = np.linalg.matrix_rank(C)
    return rank == n, rank, C

def lqr_controller(A, B, Q, R):
    """
    Design LQR controller by solving the Algebraic Riccati Equation:
    A^T P + P A - P B R^{-1} B^T P + Q = 0
    Returns optimal gain K = R^{-1} B^T P
    """
    P = solve_continuous_are(A, B, Q, R)
    K = np.linalg.inv(R) @ B.T @ P
    return K, P

def simulate_system(A, B, K, x0, t, disturbance=None):
    """Simulate closed-loop system with feedback control."""
    def dynamics(x, t):
        u = -K @ x
        if disturbance is not None:
            d = disturbance(t)
            return A @ x + B @ u + d
        return (A - B @ K) @ x
    
    return odeint(dynamics, x0, t)

Bayesian Statistics: Learning from Evidence

bayesian-statistics

Real-World Motivation

Bayesian statistics provides a coherent framework for updating beliefs in light of evidence, with applications ranging from medical diagnosis and spam filtering to machine learning and scientific inference. Unlike frequentist statistics which treats parameters as fixed unknowns, Bayesian methods treat them as random variables with probability distributions representing uncertainty. This approach naturally incorporates prior knowledge, provides full probability distributions for parameters, and offers principled ways to compare models. Modern computational methods like MCMC have made Bayesian inference practical for complex models, driving advances in fields from genetics to artificial intelligence.

Definitions

  • Prior Distribution \(p(\theta)\): Beliefs about parameters before observing data.
  • Likelihood \(p(x|\theta)\): Probability of data given parameters.
  • Posterior Distribution \(p(\theta|x)\): Updated beliefs after observing data.
  • Bayes' Theorem: \(p(\theta|x) = \frac{p(x|\theta)p(\theta)}{p(x)}\) where \(p(x) = \int p(x|\theta)p(\theta)d\theta\).
  • Conjugate Prior: A prior that yields a posterior of the same family as the prior.
  • Credible Interval: A Bayesian interval \([a, b]\) where \(P(a \leq \theta \leq b|x) = 1 - \alpha\).
  • Marginal Likelihood: \(p(x)\), used for Bayesian model comparison.

Theorem: Bayes' Theorem

Theorem (Bayes): For events \(A\) and \(B\) with \(P(B) > 0\):

\[P(A|B) = \frac{P(B|A)P(A)}{P(B)}\]

For continuous parameters and data:

\[p(\theta|x) = \frac{p(x|\theta)p(\theta)}{\int p(x|\theta')p(\theta')d\theta'}\]

Proportional Form: \(p(\theta|x) \propto p(x|\theta)p(\theta)\)

Predictive Distribution: \(p(x^*|x) = \int p(x^*|\theta)p(\theta|x)d\theta\)

Proof Sketch

The theorem follows from the definition of conditional probability:

  1. By definition: \(P(A|B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}\) and \(P(B|A) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(A)}\).
  2. Therefore: \(P(A \cap B) = P(B|A)P(A)\).
  3. Substituting: \(P(A|B) = \frac{P(B|A)P(A)}{P(B)}\).
  4. For the continuous case, replace probabilities with densities and sums with integrals.

Notation Guide

  • \(\theta\): Parameter(s) of interest
  • \(x\): Observed data
  • \(p(\theta)\): Prior density
  • \(p(x|\theta)\) or \(L(\theta|x)\): Likelihood
  • \(p(\theta|x)\): Posterior density
  • \(p(x)\) or \(Z\): Evidence/marginal likelihood
  • \(\pi(\theta)\): Alternative notation for prior
  • \(\propto\): "Proportional to"

def normal_normal_posterior(mu_prior, sigma2_prior, data, sigma2_lik):
    """
    Normal-Normal conjugate model (known variance).
    """
    n = len(data)
    sample_mean = np.mean(data)

    precision_prior = 1 / sigma2_prior

    precision_post = precision_prior + precision_data
    
    # Posterior mean = weighted average
    mu_post = (precision_prior * mu_prior + precision_data * sample_mean) / precision_post
    sigma2_post = 1 / precision_post
    
    return mu_post, sigma2_post

Galois Theory: The Symmetry of Equations

galois-theory

Historical Context

Galois theory, developed by Évariste Galois in the early 19th century before his death at age 20 in a duel, provides a profound connection between field theory and group theory. Galois showed that the solvability of polynomial equations by radicals is determined by the structure of a certain group—the Galois group—associated with the equation. This explained why general equations of degree 5 and higher cannot be solved by radicals, a problem that had stumped mathematicians for centuries. Galois's work laid the foundation for modern algebra and demonstrated the power of abstract structural thinking in mathematics.

Definitions

  • Field Extension: A pair of fields \(F \subseteq E\). The degree \([E:F]\) is the dimension of \(E\) as an \(F\)-vector space.
  • Splitting Field: The smallest field extension containing all roots of a polynomial.
  • Galois Group \(\text{Gal}(E/F)\): The group of field automorphisms of \(E\) that fix \(F\) pointwise.
  • Separable Extension: Every element has a minimal polynomial with distinct roots.
  • Normal Extension: Every irreducible polynomial with one root in \(E\) splits completely in \(E\).
  • Galois Extension: A normal and separable extension.
  • Solvable Group: A group with a composition series whose factors are cyclic of prime order.

Theorem: The Fundamental Theorem of Galois Theory

Theorem: Let \(E/F\) be a finite Galois extension with Galois group \(G = \text{Gal}(E/F)\). There is a bijective correspondence between:

\[\{\text{Intermediate fields } F \subseteq K \subseteq E\} \longleftrightarrow \{\text{Subgroups } H \leq G\}\]

given by \(K \mapsto \text{Gal}(E/K)\) and \(H \mapsto E^H\) (the fixed field of \(H\)).

Properties:

  • The correspondence is inclusion-reversing.
  • \([E:K] = |\text{Gal}(E/K)|\) and \([K:F] = [G : \text{Gal}(E/K)]\).
  • \(K/F\) is Galois iff \(\text{Gal}(E/K) \trianglelefteq G\), in which case \(\text{Gal}(K/F) \cong G/\text{Gal}(E/K)\).

Proof Sketch

The proof relies on two key results:

  1. Artin's Theorem: If \(G\) is a finite group of automorphisms of \(E\) and \(F = E^G\), then \(E/F\) is Galois with \(\text{Gal}(E/F) = G\).
  2. The maps \(K \mapsto \text{Gal}(E/K)\) and \(H \mapsto E^H\) are inverse bijections by showing \(E^{\text{Gal}(E/K)} = K\) and \(\text{Gal}(E/E^H) = H\).

Notation Guide

  • \(E/F\): Field extension
  • \([E:F]\): Degree of extension
  • \(\text{Gal}(E/F)\): Galois group
  • \(E^H\): Fixed field of subgroup \(H\)
  • \(F(\alpha)\): Field generated by adjoining \(\alpha\) to \(F\)
  • \(\overline{F}\): Algebraic closure of \(F\)
  • \(S_n\): Symmetric group on \(n\) elements
  • \(A_n\): Alternating group

Algebraic K-Theory: Beyond Linear Algebra

algebraic-k-theory

Historical Context

Algebraic K-theory, founded by Alexander Grothendieck in the late 1950s and developed by Bass, Milnor, Quillen, and many others, studies deep invariants of rings and schemes. Grothendieck originally introduced \(K_0\) to formulate the Grothendieck-Riemann-Roch theorem, but the theory quickly expanded. Higher K-groups measure subtle arithmetic and geometric information, connecting to zeta functions, regulator maps, and motivic cohomology. The subject bridges algebra, topology, number theory, and algebraic geometry, with applications ranging from the classification of manifolds to the understanding of algebraic cycles.

Definitions

  • \(K_0(R)\): The Grothendieck group of finitely generated projective \(R\)-modules. Elements are formal differences \([P] - [Q]\) with \([P] = [Q]\) iff \(P \oplus R^n \cong Q \oplus R^n\) for some \(n\).
  • \(K_1(R)\): The abelianization of the infinite general linear group: \(K_1(R) = GL(R)^{ab} = GL(R)/[GL(R), GL(R)]\).
  • \(K_2(R)\): Defined by Milnor as the kernel of the Steinberg homomorphism; can be described via Steinberg relations on symbols \(\{a, b\}\).
  • Quillen's Q-Construction: Defines higher K-groups as homotopy groups of a certain category: \(K_n(R) = \pi_{n+1}(BQ\mathcal{P}(R))\).
  • Bass-Gersten Conjecture: Relates K-theory of polynomial rings to K-theory of the base ring.

Theorem: The Fundamental Theorem of Algebraic K-Theory

Theorem (Bass-Heller-Swan): For a regular ring \(R\):

\[K_n(R[t]) \cong K_n(R) \quad \text{and} \quad K_n(R[t, t^{-1}]) \cong K_n(R) \oplus K_{n-1}(R)\]

Fundamental Theorem for \(K_0\) and \(K_1\):

  • \(K_0(R[t]) \cong K_0(R)\) (Grothendieck's theorem for regular rings)
  • \(K_1(R[t]) \cong K_1(R)\) (Bass-Heller-Swan)
  • There is a split exact sequence: \(0 \to K_n(R) \to K_n(R[t]) \oplus K_n(R[t^{-1}]) \to K_n(R[t, t^{-1}]) \to K_{n-1}(R) \to 0\)

Notation Guide

  • \(K_n(R)\): nth K-group of ring \(R\)
  • \(\mathcal{P}(R)\): Category of finitely generated projective modules
  • \(GL_n(R)\): General linear group
  • \(E_n(R)\): Elementary subgroup generated by transvections
  • \(SK_1(R) = SL(R)/E(R)\): Reduced norm 1 group
  • \(\{a, b\}\): Steinberg symbol in \(K_2\)
  • \(\tilde{K}_n(R)\): Reduced K-theory

Geometric Group Theory: Groups as Geometric Objects

geometric-group-theory

Historical Context

Geometric group theory emerged in the late 1980s through the work of Mikhail Gromov, who revolutionized the study of infinite groups by viewing them as geometric objects. The key insight is that every finitely generated group can be endowed with a metric (the word metric), making it a metric space whose large-scale geometry reflects algebraic properties. This perspective connects group theory to topology, geometry, and dynamics, leading to solutions of long-standing problems like the Novikov conjecture for hyperbolic groups and the virtual Haken conjecture. The field continues to evolve with applications to 3-manifold topology, logic, and computer science.

Definitions

  • Cayley Graph: For a group \(G\) with generating set \(S\), the graph with vertices \(G\) and edges \((g, gs)\) for \(g \in G, s \in S\).
  • Word Metric: \(d_S(g, h) = \) length of shortest word in \(S \cup S^{-1}\) representing \(g^{-1}h\).
  • Quasi-Isometry: A map \(f: X \to Y\) such that \(\frac{1}{K}d_X(x, x') - C \leq d_Y(f(x), f(x')) \leq Kd_X(x, x') + C\) and every point of \(Y\) is within distance \(C\) of \(f(X)\).
  • Hyperbolic Group: A group whose Cayley graph is Gromov hyperbolic: all geodesic triangles are \(\delta\)-thin.
  • Ends: The number of "infinite directions" in a group; related to splittings as amalgams.
  • Growth Function: \(\gamma_S(n) = |\{g \in G : |g|_S \leq n\}|\), measuring how quickly the group expands.

Theorem: The Milnor-Švarc Lemma

Theorem: Let \(G\) act properly discontinuously and cocompactly by isometries on a proper geodesic metric space \(X\). Then \(G\) is finitely generated, and for any choice of finite generating set \(S\), the orbit map \(g \mapsto g \cdot x_0\) is a quasi-isometry between \((G, d_S)\) and \(X\).

Corollary: The quasi-isometry class of a finitely generated group is well-defined (independent of generating set).

Examples:

  • \(\mathbb{Z}^n\) is quasi-isometric to \(\mathbb{R}^n\).
  • A free group \(F_n\) is quasi-isometric to its Cayley tree.
  • The fundamental group of a closed hyperbolic manifold is quasi-isometric to \(\mathbb{H}^n\).

Proof Sketch

The proof constructs a generating set from the geometry of the action:

  1. Choose a compact fundamental domain \(K\) for the action.
  2. Let \(S = \{g \in G : gK \cap K \neq \emptyset\}\), which is finite by proper discontinuity.
  3. Show every element of \(G\) can be written as a product of elements of \(S\).
  4. The orbit map is coarsely surjective by cocompactness and satisfies the quasi-isometry inequalities by comparing word length to distance in \(X\).

Notation Guide

  • \(\Gamma(G, S)\): Cayley graph of \(G\) with respect to \(S\)
  • \(|g|_S\): Word length of \(g\)
  • \(d_S\): Word metric
  • \(\partial G\): Gromov boundary of hyperbolic group
  • \(\cong_{qi}\): Quasi-isometric
  • \(\delta\): Hyperbolicity constant
  • \(B_S(n)\): Ball of radius \(n\) in word metric
  • \(\gamma_G(n)\): Growth function

import numpy as np
from collections import deque

class CayleyGraph:
    """
    Representation of a Cayley graph for computational exploration.
    """
    def __init__(self, group_type, generators):
        self.group_type = group_type
        self.generators = generators
        self.S = generators + [self.inverse(g) for g in generators]
        
    def inverse(self, g):
        """Compute inverse of group element."""
        if self.group_type == "free":
            return g.swapcase()  # a <-> A, b <-> B
        elif self.group_type == "zn":
            return (-g[0],) if isinstance(g, tuple) else (-g,)
        return g
    
    def multiply(self, g, h):
        """Multiply group elements."""
        if self.group_type == "free":
            # Free group reduction
            result = g + h
            # Reduce: remove aa^{-1} pairs
            changed = True
            while changed:
                changed = False
                for i in range(len(result) - 1):
                    if result[i] == self.inverse(result[i+1]):
                        result = result[:i] + result[i+2:]
                        changed = True
                        break
            return result
        elif self.group_type == "zn":
            return (g[0] + h[0],)
        return g
    
    def word_length(self, g):
        """Compute word length |g|_S."""
        if self.group_type == "free":
            return len(g)
        elif self.group_type == "zn":
            return abs(g[0])
        return 0
    
    def ball(self, radius):
        """Compute the ball of given radius in the Cayley graph."""
        if self.group_type == "zn":
            # Z is simple
            return 2 * radius + 1
        
        # BFS for more complex groups
        visited = set()
        queue = deque([("", 0)])  # (element, distance)
        visited.add("")
        
        while queue:
            current, dist = queue.popleft()
            if dist >= radius:
                continue
            for s in self.generators:
                next_elem = self.multiply(current, s)
                if next_elem not in visited:
                    visited.add(next_elem)
                    queue.append((next_elem, dist + 1))
        
        return len(visited)

def growth_function(group_type, generators, max_radius):
    """Compute growth function γ(n) for n = 0 to max_radius."""
    G = CayleyGraph(group_type, generators)
    growth = []
    for r in range(max_radius + 1):
        size = G.ball(r)
        growth.append(size)
    return growth

The Mathematics of Carl Friedrich Gauss

carl-friedrich-gauss

Historical Context

Carl Friedrich Gauss (1777–1855), known as the "Prince of Mathematicians," made foundational contributions to virtually every area of mathematics and mathematical physics. His 1799 doctoral thesis proved the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra, and his 1801 magnum opus "Disquisitiones Arithmeticae" established modern number theory. Gauss developed the method of least squares, non-Euclidean geometry, differential geometry (including the Theorema Egregium), and potential theory. In physics, he contributed to electromagnetism, astronomy (predicting the orbit of Ceres), and geodesy. His work on the normal distribution and the central limit theorem made him a founder of modern statistics.

Key Mathematical Contributions

  • Number Theory: Quadratic reciprocity, composition of binary quadratic forms, cyclotomy, and the constructibility of regular polygons.
  • Algebra: First complete proof of the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra; theory of symmetric functions.
  • Analysis: Hypergeometric series, convergence of infinite series, numerical integration (Gaussian quadrature).
  • Geometry: Intrinsic differential geometry, Gaussian curvature, Theorema Egregium, non-Euclidean geometry (unpublished).
  • Statistics: Method of least squares, normal distribution, maximum likelihood estimation.
  • Physics: Potential theory, magnetism, geodesy, and the Gauss-Bonnet theorem (special cases).

Theorem: Gauss's Quadratic Reciprocity Law

Theorem (Quadratic Reciprocity): For distinct odd primes \(p\) and \(q\):

\[\left(\frac{p}{q}\right)\left(\frac{q}{p}\right) = (-1)^{\frac{p-1}{2} \cdot \frac{q-1}{2}}\]

where \(\left(\frac{a}{p}\right)\) is the Legendre symbol:

\[\left(\frac{a}{p}\right) = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{if } p \mid a \\ 1 & \text{if } a \text{ is a quadratic residue mod } p \\ -1 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}\]

Supplementary Laws:

  • \(\left(\frac{-1}{p}\right) = (-1)^{(p-1)/2}\)
  • \(\left(\frac{2}{p}\right) = (-1)^{(p^2-1)/8}\)

Significance

Quadratic reciprocity is often called the "golden theorem" of number theory. It reveals a profound symmetry: whether \(p\) is a square modulo \(q\) is related to whether \(q\) is a square modulo \(p\). Gauss called it the "aureum theorema" and gave eight different proofs. The theorem launched the study of L-functions, class field theory, and the Langlands program. Modern generalizations include Artin reciprocity and the Langlands conjectures.

Notation Guide

  • \(\left(\frac{a}{p}\right)\): Legendre symbol
  • \(\mathbb{F}_p\): Finite field with \(p\) elements
  • \(a \equiv b \pmod{n}\): Congruence modulo \(n\)
  • \(\varphi(n)\): Euler's totient function
  • \(\mu(n)\): Möbius function
  • \(\zeta(s)\): Riemann zeta function

import numpy as np

def legendre_symbol(a, p):
    """
    Compute Legendre symbol (a/p) using Euler's criterion.
    (a/p) ≡ a^((p-1)/2) (mod p)
    """
    if p == 2:
        return 0 if a % 2 == 0 else 1
    if a % p == 0:
        return 0
    
    result = pow(a, (p - 1) // 2, p)
    return 1 if result == 1 else -1

def quadratic_reciprocity(p, q):
    """
    Verify quadratic reciprocity for odd primes p and q.
    (p/q)(q/p) = (-1)^((p-1)/2 * (q-1)/2)
    """
    left_side = legendre_symbol(p, q) * legendre_symbol(q, p)
    exponent = ((p - 1) // 2) * ((q - 1) // 2)
    right_side = (-1) ** exponent
    
    return left_side == right_side, left_side, right_side

def is_prime(n):
    """Check if n is prime."""
    if n < 2:
        return False
    for i in range(2, int(np.sqrt(n)) + 1):
        if n % i == 0:
            return False
    return True

def gaussian_quadrature_nodes_weights(n):
    """
    Compute nodes and weights for Gaussian quadrature.
    Nodes are roots of Legendre polynomial P_n.
    """
    # Legendre polynomials: P_0 = 1, P_1 = x
    # Recurrence: (n+1)P_{n+1} = (2n+1)xP_n - nP_{n-1}
    
    # Build companion matrix for Legendre polynomial
    # This is a tridiagonal matrix whose eigenvalues are the roots
    
    i = np.arange(1, n)
    b = i / np.sqrt(4 * i**2 - 1)
    
    # Jacobi matrix
    J = np.diag(b, -1) + np.diag(b, 1)
    
    # Eigenvalues are the nodes
    nodes, eigenvectors = np.linalg.eigh(J)
    
    # Weights from eigenvectors
    weights = 2 * eigenvectors[0, :]**2
    
    return nodes, weights

📜 Math History

The Birth of Abstract Algebra: From Equations to Structures

The 19th century witnessed a profound transformation in mathematics: the shift from studying specific objects and equations to studying abstract structures and their relationships. This revolution, which gave birth to modern abstract algebra, fundamentally changed how mathematicians think about their subject.

The story begins with the search for solutions to polynomial equations. Mathematicians had known formulas for solving quadratic equations since antiquity, and Italian mathematicians of the Renaissance discovered solutions for cubic and quartic equations. But the quintic equation resisted all attempts. In 1799, Paolo Ruffini sketched a proof that the general quintic cannot be solved by radicals, though his argument had gaps. The first complete proof came in 1824 from the young Norwegian mathematician Niels Henrik Abel.

It was Évariste Galois, however, who truly understood why the quintic is unsolvable. Working in the months before his death in a duel at age 20, Galois developed a theory that connected the solvability of equations to the structure of groups—collections of symmetries. His insight was that the algebraic structure of the equation's roots, captured by what we now call the Galois group, determines whether a solution formula exists. If this group is "solvable," so is the equation; if not, no formula exists.

Galois's work was revolutionary not just for what it proved, but for how it proved it. Rather than manipulating equations directly, he studied the abstract structure of the relationships between roots. This structural approach, though largely ignored during his lifetime, would become the dominant paradigm in mathematics.

The mid-19th century saw this abstraction extend to other areas. William Rowan Hamilton discovered quaternions in 1843, the first non-commutative number system, freeing algebra from the assumption that multiplication must commute. Arthur Cayley and others developed matrix algebra, showing that arrays of numbers could be multiplied and added according to new rules. Hermann Grassmann created an even more general theory of vector spaces, though his work was too abstract for his contemporaries to fully appreciate.

By the end of the century, Richard Dedekind had defined fields, rings, and ideals in their modern form. He showed that these abstract structures could unify number theory, algebra, and geometry. The final synthesis came in the 20th century, when Emmy Noether and her school at Göttingen showed that all of algebra could be understood through the lens of homomorphisms—structure-preserving maps between algebraic objects.

This shift from equations to structures transformed mathematics from a collection of problem-solving techniques into the study of abstract patterns and relationships. It enabled the development of modern physics, computer science, and cryptography, and it remains the dominant approach in mathematics today.

Putnam Competition Problem

Difficulty: ★★★★☆

Problem: Let \(S\) be a set of \(n\) points in the plane such that the distance between any two points is at least 1. Prove that there are at most \(3n - 6\) pairs of points at distance exactly 1.

Hint

Consider the unit distance graph formed by connecting points at distance 1. Use Euler's formula for planar graphs and show that this graph cannot contain certain subgraphs.

Solution

Let \(G\) be the unit distance graph with vertex set \(S\) and edges connecting points at distance exactly 1. We show \(G\) is planar.

Suppose two edges \((a,b)\) and \((c,d)\) cross. Then \(a,b,c,d\) form a quadrilateral with diagonals of length 1. By the triangle inequality, at least one side of this quadrilateral has length less than 1, contradicting the assumption that all pairwise distances are at least 1.

Therefore \(G\) is planar. For a planar graph with \(n \geq 3\) vertices, Euler's formula gives \(|E| \leq 3|V| - 6\). Thus the number of unit distances is at most \(3n - 6\).

Note: This bound is tight for certain configurations like the triangular lattice.

The Mandelbrot Set: Infinite Complexity from Simple Rules

The Mandelbrot set is perhaps the most famous fractal in mathematics, arising from the iteration of simple quadratic maps in the complex plane. For each complex number \(c\), we consider the sequence defined by \(z_0 = 0\) and \(z_{n+1} = z_n^2 + c\). The Mandelbrot set consists of all \(c\) for which this sequence remains bounded.

The boundary of the Mandelbrot set exhibits infinite complexity—zooming in reveals ever more intricate structures, including miniature copies of the entire set. This self-similarity at different scales is characteristic of fractals. The set is connected, but its boundary has Hausdorff dimension 2, making it a space-filling curve in a limiting sense.

The Mandelbrot set encodes deep information about the dynamics of quadratic polynomials. Each bulb attached to the main cardioid corresponds to a different period of attracting cycles, and the filaments connecting these bulbs describe bifurcations in the parameter space.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def mandelbrot(c, max_iter=100, escape_radius=2.0):
    """
    Compute the number of iterations until escape for point c.
    Returns the iteration count (normalized for smooth coloring).
    """
    z = 0
    for n in range(max_iter):
        if abs(z) > escape_radius:
            # Smooth coloring using log-log scale
            log_zn = np.log(abs(z))
            nu = np.log(log_zn / np.log(escape_radius)) / np.log(2)
            return n + 1 - nu
        z = z*z + c
    return max_iter

# Vectorized version for efficiency
def mandelbrot_set(xmin, xmax, ymin, ymax, width, height, max_iter=100):
    """Compute the Mandelbrot set over a rectangular region."""
    x = np.linspace(xmin, xmax, width)
    y = np.linspace(ymin, ymax, height)
    X, Y = np.meshgrid(x, y)
    C = X + 1j*Y
    
    Z = np.zeros_like(C)
    M = np.zeros(C.shape)
    
    for i in range(max_iter):
        mask = np.abs(Z) <= 2
        Z[mask] = Z[mask]**2 + C[mask]
        M[~mask & (M == 0)] = i
    
    M[mask] = max_iter
    return M
