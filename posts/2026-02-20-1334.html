🔬 Pure Math Deep-Dives

Functional Analysis: Infinite-Dimensional Linear Algebra

functional-analysis

Motivation

Functional analysis extends the methods of linear algebra and calculus to infinite-dimensional spaces. Born from the study of integral equations and Fourier series in the early 20th century, it provides the mathematical foundation for quantum mechanics, partial differential equations, and optimization theory.

Definitions

  • Normed Vector Space: A vector space \(V\) over \(\mathbb{R}\) or \(\mathbb{C}\) equipped with a norm \(\|\cdot\|: V \to \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\).
  • Banach Space: A complete normed vector space.
  • Hilbert Space: A complete inner product space.
  • Bounded Linear Operator: A linear map \(T: X \to Y\) with finite operator norm.
  • Dual Space: For a normed space \(X\), the dual \(X^* = \mathcal{L}(X, \mathbb{C})\).

Theorem: The Hahn-Banach Theorem

Theorem: Let \(X\) be a normed vector space over \(\mathbb{R}\) or \(\mathbb{C}\), \(Y \subseteq X\) a subspace, and \(f: Y \to \mathbb{C}\) a bounded linear functional. Then there exists a bounded linear functional \(F: X \to \mathbb{C}\) extending \(f\) with \(\|F\| = \|f\|\).

Proof Sketch

The proof proceeds in stages: First, for the real case with codimension 1, define \(F(y + tz) = f(y) + tc\) where \(c\) is chosen to preserve the norm bound. Then use Zorn's lemma to extend to all of \(X\). For the complex case, write \(f = \text{Re}(f) + i\text{Im}(f)\) and extend the real part, then reconstruct the complex extension.

Knot Theory: The Topology of Embeddings

knot-theory

Motivation

Knot theory studies the embedding of circles in three-dimensional space, asking when two such embeddings are equivalent under continuous deformation. What began as a Victorian parlor curiosity has evolved into a rich field connecting topology, algebra, and physics.

Definitions

  • Knot: An embedding of \(S^1\) into \(\mathbb{R}^3\) or \(S^3\).
  • Link: An embedding of one or more disjoint circles into \(\mathbb{R}^3\).
  • Knot Diagram: A planar projection of a knot with crossing information indicated.
  • Reidemeister Moves: Three local moves that generate all ambient isotopies of knots.

Category Theory: The Mathematics of Structure

category-theory

Motivation

Category theory, introduced by Eilenberg and Mac Lane in 1945, provides a unified language for mathematics, revealing structural similarities across diverse fields. The Yoneda lemma shows that an object is completely determined by its relationships to other objects.

Theorem: The Yoneda Lemma

Theorem: Let \(\\mathcal{C}\) be a category, \(F: \\mathcal{C}^{\\text{op}} \\to \text{Set}\) a functor, and \(X \in \\mathcal{C}\). Then there is a natural bijection:

\[\text{Nat}(\\text{Hom}_{\\mathcal{C}}(-, X), F) \\cong F(X)\]

Symplectic Geometry: The Geometry of Classical Mechanics

Historical Context

Symplectic geometry emerged from the study of classical mechanics in the 19th century. Hamilton's reformulation of Newtonian mechanics revealed that phase space carries a natural geometric structure—the symplectic form.

Theorem: Liouville's Theorem

Theorem: Let \((M, \\omega)\) be a symplectic manifold and \(H: M \\to \\mathbb{R}\) a Hamiltonian. The flow \(\phi_t\) of the Hamiltonian vector field \(X_H\) preserves the symplectic form:

\[\\phi_t^* \\omega = \\omega\]

Ergodic theory originated in statistical mechanics, where Boltzmann hypothesized that time averages equal space averages for systems in equilibrium.

📜 Math History: Bernhard Riemann and the Foundations of Modern Geometry

Georg Friedrich Bernhard Riemann (1826–1866) revolutionized mathematics in his brief 39 years of life, laying the foundations for modern differential geometry, complex analysis, and even aspects of number theory and physics that would not be fully appreciated until decades after his death. His 1854 habilitation lecture "Über die Hypothesen, welche der Geometrie zu Grunde liegen" (On the Hypotheses Which Lie at the Foundations of Geometry) was a watershed moment that freed geometry from the constraints of Euclidean space and opened the door to the curved spacetime of general relativity.

Born in the village of Breselenz in the Kingdom of Hanover, Riemann showed early mathematical promise despite initially studying theology at his father's wish. Under the mentorship of Carl Friedrich Gauss at Göttingen, and later with the analysts Dirichlet and Jacobi in Berlin, Riemann developed into one of the most original mathematical minds of the 19th century.

The 1854 lecture on geometry was delivered to an audience that included the aging Gauss, who reportedly was deeply impressed. Riemann's insight was to define geometry intrinsically: rather than considering space as embedded in some larger Euclidean space, he studied the metric properties of space itself through the measurement of distances along curves.

Riemann's 1859 paper "Ueber die Anzahl der Primzahlen unter einer gegebenen Grösse" introduced the Riemann zeta function as a function of a complex variable and stated the Riemann hypothesis about the location of its non-trivial zeros. The Riemann hypothesis remains the most famous unsolved problem in mathematics.

Riemann died of tuberculosis in Italy at the age of 39, having published relatively little but changed everything. The Riemannian legacy is not merely a collection of theorems but a way of thinking about mathematics—geometric, conceptual, and profoundly interconnected—that continues to shape the field today.

🧩 Problem Corner

IMO 2023 Problem 2 — Number Theory

Problem: Let \(n\) be a positive integer. A Japanese triangle consists of \(1 + 2 + \\cdots + n\) circles arranged in an equilateral triangular shape such that for each \(i = 1, 2, \\ldots, n\), the \(i\)-th row contains exactly \(i\) circles, exactly one of which is colored red. A ninja path in a Japanese triangle is a sequence of \(n\) circles obtained by starting in the top row, then repeatedly going from a circle to one of the two circles immediately below it, and finishing in the bottom row.

Find the greatest integer \(k\) (in terms of \(n\)) such that in each Japanese triangle there is a ninja path containing at least \(k\) red circles.

💡 Hint

Consider the binary representation of positions in the triangle. Each ninja path corresponds to a sequence of left/right choices. Think about how to use the probabilistic method or an averaging argument to show that some path must contain many red circles.

📖 Solution

The answer is \(k = \\lfloor \\log_2 n \\rfloor + 1\).

Lower bound: We show there always exists a path with at least \(\lfloor \\log_2 n \\rfloor + 1\) red circles. Consider the binary tree structure of the triangle. Each ninja path corresponds to a root-to-leaf path in a complete binary tree of depth \(n-1\).

Upper bound: Construct a Japanese triangle where every ninja path contains at most \(\lfloor \\log_2 n \\rfloor + 1\) red circles by placing red circles according to the binary representation of row indices. ∎

🎨 Visualization of the Day: The Mandelbrot Set

The Mandelbrot set is one of mathematics' most iconic images—a fractal boundary between order and chaos in the complex plane. Discovered by Benoit Mandelbrot in 1980, it arises from iterating the simple function \(f_c(z) = z^2 + c\) starting from \(z = 0\). The set consists of all complex numbers \(c\) for which this iteration remains bounded.

What makes the Mandelbrot set remarkable is its infinite complexity: zooming in reveals ever-finer structures, including miniature copies of the entire set, connected by delicate filaments. The boundary has Hausdorff dimension 2, making it a space-filling curve in a limiting sense.

Python Code

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def mandelbrot(c, max_iter=100):
    """Compute iterations until escape for point c."""
    z = 0
    for n in range(max_iter):
        if abs(z) > 2:
            return n
        z = z*z + c
    return max_iter

def mandelbrot_set(xmin, xmax, ymin, ymax, width, height, max_iter=100):
    """Compute the Mandelbrot set over a rectangular region."""
    x = np.linspace(xmin, xmax, width)
    y = np.linspace(ymin, ymax, height)
    X, Y = np.meshgrid(x, y)
    C = X + 1j*Y
    
    Z = np.zeros_like(C)
    iterations = np.zeros(C.shape, dtype=int)
    mask = np.ones(C.shape, dtype=bool)
    
    for i in range(max_iter):
        Z[mask] = Z[mask]**2 + C[mask]
        mask_new = abs(Z) <= 2
        iterations[~mask_new & (iterations == 0)] = i
        mask = mask_new
    
    iterations[mask] = max_iter
    return iterations

# Create Mandelbrot set
mandel = mandelbrot_set(-2.5, 1.5, -1.5, 1.5, width=1000, height=1000, max_iter=100)

plt.figure(figsize=(12, 12))
plt.imshow(mandel, extent=[-2.5, 1.5, -1.5, 1.5], cmap='hot', origin='lower')
plt.colorbar(label='Iterations to escape')
plt.title('The Mandelbrot Set')
plt.xlabel('Re(c)')
plt.ylabel('Im(c)')
plt.savefig('mandelbrot.png', dpi=150, bbox_inches='tight')
