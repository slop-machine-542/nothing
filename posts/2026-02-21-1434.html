Knot Theory: The Topology of Embeddings

knot-theory

Motivation

Knot theory studies embeddings of circles in 3-dimensional space, asking fundamental questions about when two knots are equivalent under continuous deformation. What began as a Victorian curiosity—Lord Kelvin's theory of vortex atoms—has grown into a rich field with applications to DNA topology, polymer physics, and quantum field theory. The discovery of the Jones polynomial in 1984 revolutionized the subject, connecting knot theory to statistical mechanics and quantum groups. Modern knot theory sits at the intersection of topology, algebra, and physics, with the volume conjecture and categorification programs driving current research.

Definitions

  • Knot: An embedding of \( S^1 \) into \( S^3 \) (or \( \mathbb{R}^3 \)). Two knots are equivalent if related by an ambient isotopy.
  • Link: An embedding of a disjoint union of circles; the number of components is the link's multiplicity.
  • Crossing Number: The minimum number of crossings in any diagram of the knot, denoted \( c(K) \).
  • Seifert Surface: A compact, connected, orientable surface in \( S^3 \) whose boundary is the given knot.
  • Genus: The minimum genus of any Seifert surface for the knot, denoted \( g(K) \).
  • Reidemeister Moves: Three local moves on diagrams (twist, poke, slide) that generate all ambient isotopies.
  • Chirality: A knot is chiral if it is not equivalent to its mirror image; otherwise it is amphichiral.

Theorem: The Reidemeister Theorem

Theorem (Reidemeister, 1927): Two knot diagrams represent equivalent knots if and only if one can be transformed into the other by a finite sequence of Reidemeister moves (Type I, II, and III) and planar isotopy.

Proof Sketch

The proof relates diagrammatic moves to the projection of 3-dimensional isotopies. A knot isotopy in \( S^3 \) can be decomposed into elementary moves: birth/death of maxima/minima, saddle moves, and isotopy within level sets. When projected to the plane, these correspond to Reidemeister moves. Type I handles the introduction/removal of kinks, Type II handles pairs of crossings, and Type III handles triple points. The key insight is that any generic isotopy in 3-space projects to a sequence of these local diagram changes.

Notation Guide

  • \( K, L \): Knots and links

  • \( g(K) \): Genus of knot
  • \( \Delta_K(t) \): Alexander polynomial
  • \( V_K(t) \): Jones polynomial
  • \( P_K(a, z) \): HOMFLY polynomial
  • \( \pi_1(S^3 \setminus K) \): Knot group
  • \( X_K \): Knot complement
  • \( T_{p,q} \): Torus knot of type (p,q)
  • \( 4_1, 5_2, \ldots \): Knot tables (Alexander-Briggs notation)

Ergodic Theory: Long-Term Behavior of Dynamical Systems

ergodic-theory

Motivation

Ergodic theory studies measure-preserving dynamical systems, seeking to understand the long-term statistical behavior of orbits. Originating from Boltzmann's work in statistical mechanics, the field asks: when does time average equal space average? The ergodic theorem provides the answer, showing that for ergodic systems, almost every orbit visits all regions of phase space with frequency proportional to measure. This has profound implications for number theory (uniform distribution of sequences), physics (foundations of statistical mechanics), and probability theory. Modern ergodic theory connects to combinatorics via ergodic Ramsey theory and to geometry via the study of flows on homogeneous spaces.

Definitions

  • Measure-Preserving System: A quadruple \((X, \mathcal{B}, \mu, T)\) where \((X, \mathcal{B}, \mu)\) is a probability space and \(T: X \to X\) is measurable with \(\mu(T^{-1}(A)) = \mu(A)\) for all measurable \(A\).
  • Ergodicity: A system is ergodic if every invariant measurable set has measure 0 or 1; equivalently, the only invariant functions are constant almost everywhere.
  • Time Average: For \(f \in L^1(\mu)\), \(\bar{f}(x) = \lim_{N \to \infty} \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} f(T^n x)\).
  • Space Average: \(\int_X f \, d\mu\).
  • Mixing: A stronger condition than ergodicity: \(\mu(T^{-n}A \cap B) \to \mu(A)\mu(B)\) as \(n \to \infty\).
  • Entropy: A measure of the complexity of the system, quantifying the rate of information generation.

Theorem: Birkhoff's Ergodic Theorem

Theorem (Birkhoff, 1931): Let \((X, \mathcal{B}, \mu, T)\) be a measure-preserving system and \(f \in L^1(\mu)\). Then the time average exists almost everywhere: \[\bar{f}(x) = \lim_{N \to \infty} \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} f(T^n x)\] Moreover, \(\bar{f} \in L^1(\mu)\), \(\bar{f} \circ T = \bar{f}\) almost everywhere, and: \[\int_X \bar{f} \, d\mu = \int_X f \, d\mu\] If the system is ergodic, then \(\bar{f} = \int_X f \, d\mu\) almost everywhere.

Proof Sketch

The proof uses the maximal ergodic lemma: for \(f^* = \sup_N \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} f \circ T^n\), we have \(\int_{\{f^* > 0\}} f \, d\mu \geq 0\). Define \(f^+(x) = \limsup_{N} \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} f(T^n x)\) and \(f^-(x) = \liminf\). Both are invariant, so for ergodic systems they are constant. Applying the maximal lemma to \(\lambda - f\) and \(f - \lambda\) shows \(f^+ = f^- = \int f\) almost everywhere.

Notation Guide

  • \((X, \mathcal{B}, \mu, T)\): Measure-preserving dynamical system
  • \(T^n\): n-fold composition of T
  • \(T^{-1}(A)\): Preimage of set A
  • \(L^p(\mu)\): Lebesgue space
  • \(\mathbb{E}[f|\mathcal{I}]\): Conditional expectation given invariant σ-algebra
  • \(h_\mu(T)\): Measure-theoretic entropy
  • \(H(\mathcal{P})\): Shannon entropy of partition
  • \(\mathcal{P}_n = \bigvee_{i=0}^{n-1} T^{-i}\mathcal{P}\): Join of partitions

import numpy as np
from typing import Callable, List, Tuple

class MeasurePreservingSystem:
    """Represents a measure-preserving dynamical system."""
    
    def __init__(self, transformation: Callable[[float], float], 
                 space_name: str = "[0,1]"):
        self.T = transformation
        self.space = space_name
    
    def orbit(self, x0: float, n_steps: int) -> List[float]:
        """Generate orbit starting from x0."""
        orbit = [x0]
        x = x0
        for _ in range(n_steps):
            x = self.T(x)
            orbit.append(x)
        return orbit
    
    def time_average(self, f: Callable[[float], float], 
                     x0: float, n_steps: int) -> float:
        """Compute (1/N) * sum_{n=0}^{N-1} f(T^n(x0))."""
        orbit = self.orbit(x0, n_steps)
        values = [f(x) for x in orbit[:-1]]
        return np.mean(values)

Representation Theory: Symmetries as Linear Transformations

representation-theory

Motivation

Representation theory studies abstract groups by realizing them as groups of linear transformations (matrices). This translation from abstract algebra to linear algebra makes group-theoretic questions concrete and computable. The theory originated from Frobenius and Schur's work on finite groups but quickly expanded to Lie groups, where it became essential for physics—representations of the Lorentz group classify elementary particles, and representation theory underlies the Standard Model. Modern applications include harmonic analysis, number theory (Langlands program), and quantum computing. The power of representation theory lies in its ability to decompose complex structures into irreducible building blocks.

Definitions

  • Representation: A homomorphism \(\rho: G \to GL(V)\) where \(G\) is a group and \(V\) is a vector space. We write \(g \cdot v = \rho(g)(v)\).
  • Irreducible Representation: A representation with no nontrivial invariant subspaces; the building blocks of all representations.
  • Character: The function \(\chi_\rho: G \to \mathbb{C}\) defined by \(\chi_\rho(g) = \text{tr}(\rho(g))\).
  • Regular Representation: The representation of \(G\) on the group algebra \(\mathbb{C}[G]\) by left multiplication.
  • Maschke's Theorem: For finite groups over \(\mathbb{C}\), every representation decomposes as a direct sum of irreducibles.
  • Schur's Lemma: If \(V\) and \(W\) are irreducible, any intertwining map is either zero or an isomorphism.

Theorem: Orthogonality Relations for Characters

Theorem: Let \(G\) be a finite group with irreducible characters \(\chi_1, \ldots, \chi_r\). Then:

  1. (First orthogonality) For irreducible characters: \[\frac{1}{|G|} \sum_{g \in G} \chi_i(g) \overline{\chi_j(g)} = \delta_{ij}\]
  2. (Second orthogonality) For conjugacy classes \(C_k\): \[\sum_{i=1}^{r} \chi_i(C_k) \overline{\chi_i(C_l)} = \frac{|G|}{|C_k|} \delta_{kl}\]
The number of irreducible representations equals the number of conjugacy classes.

Proof Sketch

For (1), define the averaging operator \(P = \frac{1}{|G|} \sum_{g \in G} \rho(g)\) on \(V_i \otimes V_j^*\). By Schur's lemma, \(P\) is an intertwining map, so for \(i \neq j\), \(P = 0\). For \(i = j\), \(P\) projects onto the invariant subspace, which is 1-dimensional for irreducible representations. Taking traces gives the orthogonality relation.

Notation Guide

  • \(G, H\): Groups
  • \(\rho, \sigma, \pi\): Representations
  • \(V, W\): Representation spaces
  • \(GL(V)\): General linear group
  • \(\chi_\rho\): Character of representation \(\rho\)
  • \(V^G\): Invariant subspace
  • \(V \otimes W\): Tensor product
  • \(V^*\): Dual representation
  • \(\text{Ind}_H^G\): Induced representation
  • \(\text{Res}_H^G\): Restricted representation
  • \(\hat{G}\): Set of irreducible representations

import numpy as np
from typing import List, Dict, Tuple, Callable
from collections import defaultdict

class FiniteGroup:
    """Represents a finite group with multiplication table."""
    
    def __init__(self, elements: List[str], mult_table: Dict[Tuple[str, str], str]):
        self.elements = elements
        self.mult = mult_table
        self.order = len(elements)
        self.identity = elements[0]  # Assume first element is identity
        self._compute_conjugacy_classes()
    
    def _compute_conjugacy_classes(self):
        """Compute conjugacy classes of the group."""
        visited = set()
        self.conjugacy_classes = []
        
        for g in self.elements:
            if g in visited:
                continue
            # Conjugacy class of g: {hgh^{-1} : h in G}
            cls = set()
            for h in self.elements:
                # Compute h * g * h^{-1}
                hg = self.mult[(h, g)]
                h_inv = self._inverse(h)
                conj = self.mult[(hg, h_inv)]
                cls.add(conj)
            self.conjugacy_classes.append(cls)
            visited.update(cls)
    
    def _inverse(self, g: str) -> str:
        """Find inverse of g."""
        for h in self.elements:
            if self.mult[(g, h)] == self.identity:
                return h
        raise ValueError(f"No inverse found for {g}")
    
    def character_table(self) -> np.ndarray:
        """Compute character table (simplified for abelian groups)."""
        # For abelian groups, each element is its own conjugacy class
        # and all irreps are 1-dimensional
        n = len(self.conjugacy_classes)
        return np.eye(n)  # Placeholder

class Representation:
    """Represents a matrix representation of a finite group."""
    
    def __init__(self, group: FiniteGroup, 
                 matrices: Dict[str, np.ndarray]):
        self.G = group
        self.matrices = matrices
        self.dimension = list(matrices.values())[0].shape[0]
    
    def character(self, g: str) -> complex:
        """Character value at group element g."""
        return np.trace(self.matrices[g])
    
    def is_irreducible(self) -> bool:
        """Check irreducibility using Schur's lemma / character inner product."""
        # (χ, χ) = 1 iff irreducible
        inner = sum(self.character(g) * np.conj(self.character(g)) 
                    for g in self.G.elements) / self.G.order
        return np.isclose(inner, 1.0)
    
    def tensor_product(self, other: 'Representation') -> 'Representation':
        """Compute tensor product representation."""
        new_mats = {}
        for g in self.G.elements:
            new_mats[g] = np.kron(self.matrices[g], other.matrices[g])
        return Representation(self.G, new_mats)

Control Theory: Steering Dynamical Systems

control-theory

Real-World Motivation

Control theory addresses the fundamental question: how can we influence the behavior of a dynamical system to achieve desired objectives? From autopilots in aircraft to cruise control in automobiles, from insulin pumps to power grid stabilization, control systems are ubiquitous in modern technology. The mathematical foundations were laid by Maxwell (governor analysis), Lyapunov (stability theory), and Pontryagin (optimal control). Modern control theory combines differential equations, optimization, and probability to design robust systems that perform reliably despite uncertainty and disturbances. Emerging applications include autonomous vehicles, swarm robotics, and precision medicine.

Definitions

  • State-Space System: \(\dot{x} = f(x, u)\), \(y = h(x)\) where \(x\) is state, \(u\) is control input, \(y\) is output.
  • Linear Time-Invariant (LTI): \(\dot{x} = Ax + Bu\), \(y = Cx + Du\) with constant matrices.
  • Controllability: The system is controllable if any state can be reached from any other state in finite time.
  • Observability: The system is observable if the initial state can be determined from output measurements.
  • Stability: An equilibrium is stable if nearby trajectories remain nearby; asymptotically stable if they converge.
  • Feedback Control: Control law \(u = -Kx\) where \(K\) is chosen to achieve desired closed-loop dynamics.

Theorem: The Kalman Controllability Condition

Theorem (Kalman, 1960): The LTI system \(\dot{x} = Ax + Bu\) with \(x \in \mathbb{R}^n\) is controllable if and only if the controllability matrix: \[\mathcal{C} = [B \quad AB \quad A^2B \quad \cdots \quad A^{n-1}B]\] has full rank \(n\). Equivalently, the controllability Gramian: \[W_c = \int_0^T e^{A\tau}BB^Te^{A^T\tau} d\tau\] is positive definite for some \(T > 0\).

Proof Sketch

The solution to \(\dot{x} = Ax + Bu\) with \(x(0) = x_0\) is: \[x(T) = e^{AT}x_0 + \int_0^T e^{A(T-\tau)}Bu(\tau) d\tau\] To reach \(x_f\) from \(x_0\), we need: \[x_f - e^{AT}x_0 = \int_0^T e^{A(T-\tau)}Bu(\tau) d\tau\] By the Cayley-Hamilton theorem, \(e^{At}\) can be expressed as a linear combination of \(\{I, A, \ldots, A^{n-1}\}\). Thus the reachable set is exactly the column space of \(\mathcal{C}\). Full rank means any state is reachable.

  • \(C \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times n}\): Output matrix
  • \(D \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times m}\): Feedthrough matrix
  • \(\mathcal{C}\): Controllability matrix
  • \(\mathcal{O}\): Observability matrix
  • \(K\): Feedback gain matrix
  • \(P\): Solution to Riccati equation

import numpy as np
from scipy.linalg import expm, solve_continuous_are, eig
from typing import Tuple, Optional

class LinearSystem:
    """Represents a linear time-invariant system."""
    
    def __init__(self, A: np.ndarray, B: np.ndarray, 
                 C: Optional[np.ndarray] = None, 
                 D: Optional[np.ndarray] = None):
        self.A = np.array(A)
        self.B = np.array(B)
        self.n = A.shape[0]  # State dimension
        self.m = B.shape[1]  # Input dimension
        
        self.C = C if C is not None else np.eye(self.n)
        self.D = D if D is not None else np.zeros((self.C.shape[0], self.m))
        self.p = self.C.shape[0]  # Output dimension
    
    def controllability_matrix(self) -> np.ndarray:
        """Compute the controllability matrix."""
        C = self.B
        for i in range(1, self.n):
            C = np.hstack([C, np.linalg.matrix_power(self.A, i) @ self.B])
        return C
    
    def is_controllable(self, tol: float = 1e-10) -> bool:
        """Check if system is controllable."""
        C = self.controllability_matrix()
        return np.linalg.matrix_rank(C, tol=tol) == self.n
    
    def observability_matrix(self) -> np.ndarray:
        """Compute the observability matrix."""
        O = self.C
        for i in range(1, self.n):
            O = np.vstack([O, self.C @ np.linalg.matrix_power(self.A, i)])
        return O
    
    def is_observable(self, tol: float = 1e-10) -> bool:
        """Check if system is observable."""
        O = self.observability_matrix()
        return np.linalg.matrix_rank(O, tol=tol) == self.n
    
    def lqr_controller(self, Q: np.ndarray, R: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
        """
        Design LQR controller minimizing ∫(x^T Q x + u^T R u)dt.
        Returns feedback gain K where u = -Kx.
        """
        # Solve continuous-time algebraic Riccati equation
        P = solve_continuous_are(self.A, self.B, Q, R)
        # K = R^{-1} B^T P
        K = np.linalg.solve(R, self.B.T @ P)
        return K
    
    def simulate(self, x0: np.ndarray, u_func: callable, 
                 T: float, dt: float = 0.01) -> Tuple[np.ndarray, np.ndarray]:
        """Simulate system with given control input."""
        t = np.arange(0, T, dt)
        x = np.zeros((len(t), self.n))
        y = np.zeros((len(t), self.p))
        x[0] = x0
        
        for i in range(len(t) - 1):
            u = u_func(t[i], x[i])
            # Euler integration
            x[i+1] = x[i] + dt * (self.A @ x[i] + self.B @ u)
            y[i] = self.C @ x[i] + self.D @ u
        y[-1] = self.C @ x[-1]
        
        return t, x, y

Game Theory: Strategic Interaction and Equilibrium

game-theory

Real-World Motivation

Game theory studies strategic interactions where the outcome for each participant depends on the choices of all. From economics (auctions, market competition) to biology (evolutionary strategies), from political science (voting, international relations) to computer science (network routing, mechanism design), game-theoretic analysis illuminates how rational agents make decisions. John von Neumann and Oskar Morgenstern's 1944 book "Theory of Games and Economic Behavior" established the field, while John Nash's equilibrium concept became its cornerstone. Modern applications include algorithmic game theory, auction design for online advertising, and understanding cooperation in social dilemmas.

Definitions

  • Normal Form Game: \(\Gamma = (N, (S_i)_{i \in N}, (u_i)_{i \in N})\) where \(N\) is players, \(S_i\) is strategies, \(u_i\) is payoff.
  • Nash Equilibrium: A strategy profile \(s^*\) where no player can benefit by unilaterally deviating: \(u_i(s_i^*, s_{-i}^*) \geq u_i(s_i, s_{-i}^*)\) for all \(s_i\).
  • Dominant Strategy: A strategy that is optimal regardless of what others do.
  • Mixed Strategy: A probability distribution over pure strategies.
  • Zero-Sum Game: A game where \(\sum_i u_i(s) = 0\) for all strategy profiles.
  • Pareto Optimality: An outcome where no player can be made better off without making another worse off.

Theorem: Nash's Existence Theorem

Theorem (Nash, 1950): Every finite normal-form game has at least one Nash equilibrium in mixed strategies. That is, there exists a profile of mixed strategies \(\sigma^* = (\sigma_1^*, \ldots, \sigma_n^*)\) such that for each player \(i\): \[u_i(\sigma_i^*, \sigma_{-i}^*) \geq u_i(\sigma_i, \sigma_{-i}^*)\] for all mixed strategies \(\sigma_i\) of player \(i\).

Proof Sketch

The proof uses Brouwer's fixed-point theorem. Define the best-response correspondence \(BR_i(\sigma_{-i}) = \arg\max_{\sigma_i} u_i(\sigma_i, \sigma_{-i})\). The product correspondence \(BR(\sigma) = \prod_i BR_i(\sigma_{-i})\) maps the strategy simplex to itself. By Kakutani's fixed-point theorem (generalization of Brouwer for correspondences), there exists a fixed point \(\sigma^* \in BR(\sigma^*)\), which is precisely a Nash equilibrium.

Notation Guide

  • \(N = \{1, \ldots, n\}\): Set of players
  • \(S_i\): Strategy set of player \(i\)
  • \(S = \prod_i S_i\): Set of strategy profiles
  • \(s_{-i}\): Strategies of all players except \(i\)
  • \(\Delta(S_i)\): Set of mixed strategies
  • \(u_i: S \to \mathbb{R}\): Payoff function
  • \(BR_i\): Best response correspondence
  • \(NE\): Set of Nash equilibria
  • \(\epsilon\)-NE: Approximate Nash equilibrium

class NormalFormGame:
    """Represents a finite normal-form game."""
    
    def __init__(self, payoff_matrices: List[np.ndarray], player_names: List[str] = None):
        """
        payoff_matrices[i] is the payoff matrix for player i.
        For 2 players: matrix shape is (n_actions_p1, n_actions_p2)
        """
        self.payoffs = payoff_matrices
        self.n_players = len(payoff_matrices)
        self.n_actions = payoff_matrices[0].shape
        self.player_names = player_names or [f"P{i+1}" for i in range(self.n_players)]
    
    def expected_payoff(self, strategies: List[np.ndarray], player: int) -> float:
        """Compute expected payoff for player given mixed strategies."""
        # For 2-player games
        if self.n_players == 2:
            return float(strategies[0].T @ self.payoffs[player] @ strategies[1])
        else:
            raise NotImplementedError("Only 2-player games implemented")
    
    def best_response(self, opponent_strategy: np.ndarray, player: int) -> np.ndarray:
        """Find best response for player against opponent_strategy."""
        if player == 0:
            expected_payoffs = self.payoffs[0] @ opponent_strategy
        else:
            expected_payoffs = self.payoffs[1].T @ opponent_strategy
        
        # Best response: put all weight on action(s) with max payoff
        max_payoff = np.max(expected_payoffs)
        br = np.zeros_like(expected_payoffs)
        br[expected_payoffs >= max_payoff - 1e-10] = 1.0
        return br / np.sum(br)  # Normalize
    
    def find_pure_nash_equilibrium(self) -> List[Tuple[int, ...]]:
        """Find all pure strategy Nash equilibria."""
        equilibria = []
        
        # Iterate over all action profiles
        for actions in product(*[range(n) for n in self.n_actions]):
            is_equilibrium = True
            
            for i in range(self.n_players):
                current_payoff = self.payoffs[i][actions]
                
                # Check if player i can deviate profitably
                for alt_action in range(self.n_actions[i]):
                    if alt_action == actions[i]:
                        continue
                    alt_actions = list(actions)
                    alt_actions[i] = alt_action
                    alt_payoff = self.payoffs[i][tuple(alt_actions)]
                    
                    if alt_payoff > current_payoff + 1e-10:
                        is_equilibrium = False
                        break
                
                if not is_equilibrium:
                    break
            
            if is_equilibrium:
                equilibria.append(actions)
        
        return equilibria

Random Matrix Theory: Statistical Properties of Large Matrices

random-matrix-theory

Real-World Motivation

Random matrix theory studies the statistical properties of matrices with random entries, revealing universal patterns that emerge as matrix size grows large. Originating from Eugene Wigner's work on nuclear physics energy levels in the 1950s, the field has expanded to applications in quantum chaos, number theory (distribution of zeros of the Riemann zeta function), wireless communications, and machine learning. The remarkable universality phenomenon—different random matrix ensembles share the same limiting spectral statistics—suggests deep mathematical structures. Modern applications include understanding the generalization of neural networks, covariance estimation in high-dimensional statistics, and the design of MIMO communication systems.

Definitions

  • Gaussian Orthogonal Ensemble (GOE): Real symmetric matrices with independent Gaussian entries: \(H_{ij} = H_{ji} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1/N)\) for \(i \neq j\) and \(H_{ii} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 2/N)\).
  • Gaussian Unitary Ensemble (GUE): Hermitian matrices with complex Gaussian entries.
  • Empirical Spectral Distribution (ESD): \(\mu_N = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \delta_{\lambda_i}\) where \(\lambda_i\) are eigenvalues.
  • Wigner's Semicircle Law: The limiting distribution of eigenvalues for Wigner matrices.
  • Level Spacing Distribution: The distribution of gaps between consecutive eigenvalues.
  • Tracy-Widom Distribution: The limiting distribution of the largest eigenvalue in certain ensembles.

Theorem: Wigner's Semicircle Law

Theorem (Wigner, 1955): Let \(H_N\) be an \(N \times N\) Wigner matrix (real symmetric with independent entries above diagonal, mean 0, variance \(1/N\)). Then the empirical spectral distribution converges weakly almost surely to the semicircle distribution: \[\mu_N \xrightarrow{a.s.} \mu_{sc}\] where \(\mu_{sc}\) has density: \[\rho_{sc}(x) = \frac{1}{2\pi} \sqrt{4 - x^2} \cdot \mathbf{1}_{|x| \leq 2}\]

Proof Sketch

The proof uses the moment method. The \(k\)-th moment of \(\mu_N\) is: \[m_k^{(N)} = \frac{1}{N} \mathbb{E}[\text{tr}(H_N^k)] = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i_1, \ldots, i_k} \mathbb{E}[H_{i_1 i_2} H_{i_2 i_3} \cdots H_{i_k i_1}]\] By independence and zero mean, only terms where each matrix element appears at least twice contribute. In the limit, these correspond to non-crossing pairings (Catalan objects). The number of such pairings is the \(k/2\)-th Catalan number \(C_{k/2}\) for even \(k\), giving the moments of the semicircle distribution.

Notation Guide

  • \(H_N\): \(N \times N\) random Hermitian matrix
  • \(\lambda_1 \leq \cdots \leq \lambda_N\): Eigenvalues
  • \(\mu_N\): Empirical spectral distribution
  • \(\mu_{sc}\): Semicircle distribution
  • \(\rho_{sc}\): Semicircle density
  • \(m_k\): \(k\)-th moment
  • \(C_k = \frac{1}{k+1}\binom{2k}{k}\): Catalan numbers
  • \(\beta\): Dyson index (1 for GOE, 2 for GUE, 4 for GSE)
  • \(S(s)\): Two-point correlation function
  • \(F_\beta\): Tracy-Widom distribution

class WignerMatrix:
    """Generates and analyzes Wigner random matrices."""
    
    def __init__(self, N: int, beta: int = 1):
        """
        Args:
            N: Matrix size
            beta: Dyson index (1=GOE real symmetric, 2=GUE Hermitian)
        """
        self.N = N
        self.beta = beta
    
    def generate(self) -> np.ndarray:
        """Generate a random Wigner matrix."""
        if self.beta == 1:
            # GOE: real symmetric
            A = np.random.randn(self.N, self.N)
            H = (A + A.T) / np.sqrt(2 * self.N)
        elif self.beta == 2:
            # GUE: Hermitian
            A = (np.random.randn(self.N, self.N) + 
                 1j * np.random.randn(self.N, self.N)) / np.sqrt(2)
            H = (A + A.conj().T) / np.sqrt(2 * self.N)
        else:
            raise ValueError("beta must be 1 or 2")
        return H
    
    def eigenvalues(self, n_samples: int = 1) -> np.ndarray:
        """Generate eigenvalues from n_samples matrices."""
        all_eigenvals = []
        for _ in range(n_samples):
            H = self.generate()
            eigs = np.linalg.eigvalsh(H)
            all_eigenvals.extend(eigs)
        return np.array(all_eigenvals)
    
    @staticmethod
    def semicircle_pdf(x: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
        """Semicircle probability density function."""
        rho = np.zeros_like(x)
        mask = np.abs(x) <= 2
        rho[mask] = np.sqrt(4 - x[mask]**2) / (2 * np.pi)
        return rho
    
    @staticmethod
    def semicircle_cdf(x: float) -> float:
        """Semicircle cumulative distribution function."""
        from scipy.special import ellipe
        if x <= -2:
            return 0.0
        elif x >= 2:
            return 1.0
        else:
            # CDF = 1/2 + (x*sqrt(4-x^2) + 4*arcsin(x/2))/(4*pi)
            return (0.5 + (x * np.sqrt(4 - x**2) + 
                          4 * np.arcsin(x/2)) / (4 * np.pi))

class MarchenkoPastur:
    """Marchenko-Pastur law for sample covariance matrices."""
    
    def __init__(self, gamma: float):
        """
        Args:
            gamma: Aspect ratio p/n (features/samples)
        """
        self.gamma = gamma
        self.lambda_plus = (1 + np.sqrt(gamma))**2
        self.lambda_minus = (1 - np.sqrt(gamma))**2
    
    def pdf(self, x: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
        """Marchenko-Pastur density."""
        rho = np.zeros_like(x)
        mask = (x >= self.lambda_minus) & (x <= self.lambda_plus)
        if self.gamma <= 1:
            rho[mask] = (np.sqrt((self.lambda_plus - x[mask]) * 
                                (x[mask] - self.lambda_minus)) / 
                        (2 * np.pi * self.gamma * x[mask]))
        return rho

Algebraic Topology: Homotopy and Homology

algebraic-topology

Historical Context

Algebraic topology, founded by Poincaré in the 1890s, translates topological problems into algebraic ones. Poincaré's famous question—whether every simply connected closed 3-manifold is homeomorphic to the 3-sphere—became the Poincaré conjecture, solved by Perelman in 2003 using Ricci flow.

Definitions

  • Homotopy: Continuous deformation of one map into another.
  • Fundamental Group \(\pi_1(X, x_0)\): Homotopy classes of loops based at \(x_0\).
  • Homology \(H_n(X)\): Algebraic invariant counting n-dimensional holes.
  • Eilenberg-MacLane Space: Space \(K(G, n)\) with \(\pi_n = G\) and other homotopy groups trivial.

Theorem: The Hurewicz Theorem

Theorem: For a simply connected space \(X\), the first non-trivial homotopy and homology groups appear in the same dimension and are isomorphic: if \(\pi_k(X) = 0\) for \(k < n\), then \(\pi_n(X) \cong H_n(X)\).

Proof Sketch

The Hurewicz map \(h: \pi_n(X) \to H_n(X)\) sends a homotopy class of maps \(f: S^n \to X\) to the image of the fundamental class. The theorem asserts this is an isomorphism when lower homotopy groups vanish. The proof uses the long exact sequence of a fibration and induction on dimension.

Notation Guide

  • \(\pi_n(X)\): n-th homotopy group
  • \(H_n(X; G)\): Homology with coefficients in G
  • \(H^n(X; G)\): Cohomology
  • \(\chi(X)\): Euler characteristic
  • \(\beta_n\): n-th Betti number
  • \(S^n\): n-sphere
  • \(\mathbb{C}P^n\): Complex projective space

import numpy as np
from itertools import combinations

class SimplicialHomology:
    """Computes simplicial homology of a simplicial complex."""
    
    def __init__(self, simplices: dict):
        """
        simplices[dim] = list of simplices of that dimension
        Each simplex is a frozenset of vertices
        """
        self.simplices = simplices
        self.max_dim = max(simplices.keys())
    
    def boundary_matrix(self, dim: int) -> np.ndarray:
        """Compute boundary matrix ∂_dim: C_dim → C_{dim-1}."""
        if dim == 0:
            return np.zeros((1, len(self.simplices[0])))
        
        n_dim = len(self.simplices[dim])
        n_lower = len(self.simplices[dim-1])
        
        # Create index maps
        lower_indices = {s: i for i, s in enumerate(self.simplices[dim-1])}
        
        boundary = np.zeros((n_lower, n_dim), dtype=int)
        
        for j, simplex in enumerate(self.simplices[dim]):
            # Boundary is alternating sum of faces
            for i, v in enumerate(sorted(simplex)):
                face = frozenset(simplex - {v})
                if face in lower_indices:
                    boundary[lower_indices[face], j] = (-1)**i
        
        return boundary
    
    def betti_numbers(self) -> dict:
        """Compute Betti numbers β_k = dim(H_k)."""
        betti = {}
        
        for dim in range(self.max_dim + 1):
            boundary_dim = self.boundary_matrix(dim)
            boundary_next = self.boundary_matrix(dim + 1) if dim < self.max_dim else np.zeros((1, 1))
            
            # H_k = ker(∂_k) / im(∂_{k+1})
            rank_boundary_dim = np.linalg.matrix_rank(boundary_dim)
            rank_boundary_next = np.linalg.matrix_rank(boundary_next)
            
            n_k = boundary_dim.shape[1]  # Number of k-simplices
            
            # dim(ker ∂_k) = n_k - rank(∂_k)
            # dim(im ∂_{k+1}) = rank(∂_{k+1})
            betti[dim] = n_k - rank_boundary_dim - rank_boundary_next
        
        return betti

Partial Differential Equations: The Language of Continuum Physics

partial-differential-equations

Historical Context

Partial differential equations emerged in the 18th century with Euler's work on fluid dynamics and Fourier's analysis of heat conduction. The classification into elliptic, parabolic, and hyperbolic equations, developed in the 19th century, reveals fundamental differences in behavior and appropriate solution methods.

Definitions

  • Elliptic Equation: Characterized by \(\Delta u = f\); Laplace and Poisson equations. Solutions are smooth, boundary value problems are well-posed.
  • Parabolic Equation: Characterized by \(\partial_t u = \Delta u\); heat equation. Solutions smooth instantly, information propagates infinitely fast.
  • Hyperbolic Equation: Characterized by \(\partial_{tt} u = \Delta u\); wave equation. Finite propagation speed, conservation of energy.
  • Weak Solution: Solution in distributional sense, allowing for discontinuities (shocks).

Theorem: The Cauchy-Kovalevskaya Theorem

Theorem: If the coefficients and initial data of a PDE are analytic, then there exists a unique analytic solution in a neighborhood of the initial surface.

import numpy as np
from scipy.sparse import diags
from scipy.sparse.linalg import spsolve

class HeatEquationSolver:
    """Solves 1D heat equation using finite differences."""
    
    def __init__(self, L: float, T: float, nx: int, nt: int, alpha: float = 1.0):
        self.L = L
        self.T = T
        self.nx = nx
        self.nt = nt
        self.alpha = alpha
        self.dx = L / (nx - 1)
        self.dt = T / nt
        self.r = alpha * self.dt / self.dx**2
    
    def solve_crank_nicolson(self, u0: np.ndarray) -> np.ndarray:
        """
        Solve using Crank-Nicolson (implicit, unconditionally stable).
        """
        n = self.nx
        r = self.r
        
        # Build tridiagonal matrices
        # A * u^{n+1} = B * u^n
        main_A = (1 + r) * np.ones(n)
        off_A = -r/2 * np.ones(n-1)
        A = diags([off_A, main_A, off_A], [-1, 0, 1])
        
        main_B = (1 - r) * np.ones(n)
        off_B = r/2 * np.ones(n-1)
        B = diags([off_B, main_B, off_B], [-1, 0, 1])
        
        # Time stepping
        u = u0.copy()
        for _ in range(self.nt):
            u = spsolve(A, B @ u)
        
        return u

Analytic Number Theory: The Distribution of Primes

number-theory

Historical Context

The study of prime numbers dates to Euclid's proof of their infinitude. Gauss conjectured the prime number theorem at age 15, but it took until 1896 for Hadamard and de la Vallée Poussin to prove it using complex analysis. The Riemann Hypothesis, connecting primes to the zeros of the zeta function, remains the most important open problem in mathematics.

Definitions

  • Prime Counting Function: \(\pi(x) = \#\{p \leq x : p \text{ prime}\}\).
  • Logarithmic Integral: \(\text{li}(x) = \int_2^x \frac{dt}{\ln t}\).
  • Riemann Zeta Function: \(\zeta(s) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty n^{-s} = \prod_p (1 - p^{-s})^{-1}\) for \(\text{Re}(s) > 1\).
  • Möbius Function: \(\mu(n) = (-1)^k\) if \(n\) is product of \(k\) distinct primes, 0 otherwise.
  • von Mangoldt Function: \(\Lambda(n) = \ln p\) if \(n = p^k\), 0 otherwise.

Theorem: The Prime Number Theorem

Theorem (Hadamard, de la Vallée Poussin, 1896): \[\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{\pi(x)}{x / \ln x} = 1\] Equivalently, \(\pi(x) \sim \text{li}(x)\). The error term satisfies: \[\pi(x) = \text{li}(x) + O(x \exp(-c\sqrt{\ln x}))\]

Proof Sketch

The proof uses the Riemann zeta function. Key steps: (1) Show \(\zeta(s)\) has no zeros on \(\text{Re}(s) = 1\). (2) Relate \(\pi(x)\) to \(\psi(x) = \sum_{n \leq x} \Lambda(n)\) via partial summation. (3) Use contour integration and the explicit formula connecting \(\psi(x)\) to zeros of \(\zeta(s)\). The zero-free region gives the error term.

import numpy as np
from scipy.special import expi

class PrimeNumberTheory:
    """Functions related to the distribution of primes."""
    
    @staticmethod
    def sieve_of_eratosthenes(n: int) -> list:
        """Generate all primes up to n."""
        sieve = [True] * (n + 1)
        sieve[0] = sieve[1] = False
        for i in range(2, int(np.sqrt(n)) + 1):
            if sieve[i]:
                sieve[i*i::i] = [False] * len(sieve[i*i::i])
        return [i for i, is_prime in enumerate(sieve) if is_prime]
    
    @staticmethod
    def pi(x: int) -> int:
        """Prime counting function π(x)."""
        if x < 2:
            return 0
        return len(PrimeNumberTheory.sieve_of_eratosthenes(x))
    
    @staticmethod
    def li(x: float) -> float:
        """Logarithmic integral li(x)."""
        # li(x) = Ei(ln x) where Ei is the exponential integral
        if x <= 2:
            return 0.0
        return expi(np.log(x))
    
    @staticmethod
    def zeta(s: complex, terms: int = 100000) -> complex:
        """Riemann zeta function (naive implementation)."""
        # Only valid for Re(s) > 1
        if s.real <= 1:
            # Use analytic continuation (not implemented here)
            return float('nan')
        return sum(n**(-s) for n in range(1, terms + 1))
    
    @staticmethod
    def mobius(n: int) -> int:
        """Möbius function μ(n)."""
        if n == 1:
            return 1
        
        # Count prime factors
        factors = 0
        d = 2
        temp = n
        while d * d <= temp:
            if temp % d == 0:
                count = 0
                while temp % d == 0:
                    temp //= d
                    count += 1
                if count > 1:
                    return 0  # Not squarefree
                factors += 1
            d += 1
        
        if temp > 1:
            factors += 1
        
        return (-1)**factors

Évariste Galois: Revolutionary Mathematician

galois

Historical Context

Évariste Galois (1811–1832) transformed mathematics during his tragically short life. Before dying in a duel at age 20, he developed Galois theory, establishing the connection between field extensions and groups. His work provided the definitive answer to which polynomial equations are solvable by radicals.

Key Contributions

  • Galois Theory: The Galois group of a polynomial relates the symmetries of its roots to the solvability of the equation.
  • Finite Fields: Galois proved the existence of finite fields with \(p^n\) elements for any prime \(p\) and integer \(n\).
  • Group Theory: He was among the first to recognize groups as fundamental algebraic structures.

Theorem: The Fundamental Theorem of Galois Theory

Theorem: Let \(K/F\) be a finite Galois extension with Galois group \(G = \text{Gal}(K/F)\). There is a bijection between intermediate fields \(F \subseteq E \subseteq K\) and subgroups \(H \subseteq G\), given by: \[E \mapsto \text{Gal}(K/E) \quad \text{and} \quad H \mapsto K^H\] Moreover, \(E/F\) is Galois if and only if the corresponding subgroup is normal, in which case \(\text{Gal}(E/F) \cong G/H\).

import numpy as np

class GaloisField:
    """Represents GF(p^n) - finite field with p^n elements."""
    
    def __init__(self, p: int, n: int, poly: int = None):
        """
        Args:
            p: Prime characteristic
            n: Extension degree
            poly: Irreducible polynomial (if None, use default)
        """
        self.p = p
        self.n = n
        self.q = p**n
        
        # Default irreducible polynomials
        if poly is None:
            # Simple defaults for small cases
            if n == 1:
                self.poly = 2  # x
            elif p == 2 and n == 2:
                self.poly = 0b111  # x^2 + x + 1
            elif p == 2 and n == 3:
                self.poly = 0b1011  # x^3 + x + 1
            elif p == 2 and n == 4:
                self.poly = 0b10011  # x^4 + x + 1
            else:
                raise ValueError("Please specify irreducible polynomial")
        else:
            self.poly = poly
    
    def add(self, a: int, b: int) -> int:
        """Addition in GF(p^n): XOR for p=2."""
        if self.p == 2:
            return a ^ b
        return (a + b) % self.p
    
    def multiply(self, a: int, b: int) -> int:
        """Multiplication in GF(p^n) modulo irreducible polynomial."""
        if self.p != 2:
            raise NotImplementedError("Only p=2 implemented")
        
        result = 0
        while b > 0:
            if b & 1:
                result ^= a
            b >>= 1
            a <<= 1
            # Reduce modulo irreducible polynomial if degree >= n
            if a & (1 << self.n):
                a ^= self.poly
        return result
    
    def power(self, a: int, k: int) -> int:
        """Compute a^k in GF(p^n)."""
        result = 1
        base = a
        while k > 0:
            if k & 1:
                result = self.multiply(result, base)
            base = self.multiply(base, base)
            k >>= 1
        return result
    
    def multiplicative_order(self, a: int) -> int:
        """Order of element in multiplicative group."""
        if a == 0:
            return float('inf')
        order = 1
        current = a
        while current != 1:
            current = self.multiply(current, a)
            order += 1
        return order
    
    def is_primitive(self, a: int) -> bool:
        """Check if a is a primitive element (generator of multiplicative group)."""
        return self.multiplicative_order(a) == self.q - 1

📜 Math History: The Development of Abstract Algebra

The emergence of abstract algebra in the 19th century marked a profound shift in mathematical thinking—from studying specific numbers and equations to understanding general algebraic structures. This transformation was driven by several parallel developments: the search for solutions to polynomial equations, the study of number theory, and the need to understand symmetry in geometry.

The story begins with the work of Évariste Galois (1811–1832), who, in his short life, revolutionized our understanding of polynomial equations. Galois developed what we now call Galois theory, showing that the solvability of a polynomial equation by radicals depends on the structure of its associated permutation group. This was the first major application of group theory, though the abstract concept of a group would not be formalized until later.

Meanwhile, Carl Friedrich Gauss was laying foundations in number theory. His Disquisitiones Arithmeticae (1801) introduced the concept of congruence and studied quadratic forms systematically. Gauss's work on cyclotomic equations and Gaussian integers extended the notion of integer to new domains, foreshadowing the development of ring theory.

The abstract definition of a group emerged gradually through the work of Arthur Cayley, Camille Jordan, and Heinrich Weber in the mid-to-late 19th century. Weber's 1895 textbook Lehrbuch der Algebra presented groups, rings, and fields in something close to their modern form. This abstraction allowed mathematicians to recognize structural similarities across different areas—permutation groups, matrix groups, and transformation groups all shared common properties.

The 20th century brought further abstraction with Emmy Noether's work on ring theory and ideal theory. Her conceptual approach, emphasizing structural properties over computational techniques, transformed algebra. Noether's theorem in physics, connecting symmetries to conservation laws, demonstrated the power of abstract algebraic thinking.

Today, abstract algebra underlies much of modern mathematics and its applications. Group theory is essential in crystallography, chemistry, and particle physics. Ring theory and field theory provide foundations for algebraic geometry and number theory. The categorical viewpoint, developed in the mid-20th century, further abstracts these structures, revealing deep connections between algebra, topology, and logic.

Problem: Putnam 2023 A1

For a positive integer \(n\), let \(f(n)\) be the number of \(n\)-digit positive integers formed only using digits 1, 2, and 3 such that no two adjacent digits differ by exactly 1. Find a closed-form expression for \(f(n)\).

Hint

Use dynamic programming. Let \(a_n\) be the number of valid strings ending in 1 or 3, and \(b_n\) be the number ending in 2. Find recurrences for \(a_n\) and \(b_n\), then solve the recurrence relation.

Solution

Step 1: Set up recurrences.

Let \(a_n\) = number of valid n-digit strings ending in 1 or 3.
Let \(b_n\) = number of valid n-digit strings ending in 2.

For strings ending in 1 or 3: the previous digit can be 1, 2, or 3 (but not 2 if current is 1 or 3, since |2-1|=1). Actually, let's be more careful:

  • To end in 1: previous can be 1 or 3 (not 2)
  • To end in 2: previous can be 2 (not 1 or 3)
  • To end in 3: previous can be 1 or 3 (not 2)

Let \(c_n\) = strings ending in 1, \(d_n\) = ending in 2, \(e_n\) = ending in 3.
By symmetry, \(c_n = e_n\).

Recurrences:
\(c_n = c_{n-1} + e_{n-1} = 2c_{n-1}\) (wait, this isn't right either)

Let me reconsider: valid transitions are 1→1, 1→3, 2→2, 3→1, 3→3.

So:
\(c_n = c_{n-1} + e_{n-1}\)
\(d_n = d_{n-1}\)
\(e_n = c_{n-1} + e_{n-1}\)

By symmetry \(c_n = e_n\), so:
\(c_n = 2c_{n-1}\)
\(d_n = d_{n-1}\)

Step 2: Initial conditions.
For n=1: \(c_1 = 1, d_1 = 1, e_1 = 1\)

Step 3: Solve.
\(c_n = 2^{n-1}\)
\(d_n = 1\)

Therefore: \(f(n) = 2c_n + d_n = 2 \cdot 2^{n-1} + 1 = 2^n + 1\)

Answer: \(f(n) = 2^n + 1\)

🎨 Visualization of the Day: The Lorenz Attractor

The Lorenz attractor is a set of chaotic solutions to the Lorenz system, a simplified mathematical model for atmospheric convection. Discovered by Edward Lorenz in 1963, it was one of the first demonstrations of deterministic chaos—sensitive dependence on initial conditions.

The system is defined by three differential equations: \[\frac{dx}{dt} = \sigma(y - x)\] \[\frac{dy}{dt} = x(\rho - z) - y\] \[\frac{dz}{dt} = xy - \beta z\]

With parameters \(\sigma = 10\), \(\beta = 8/3\), and \(\rho = 28\), the system exhibits chaotic behavior, with trajectories forming the famous butterfly-shaped attractor.

    """
    Lorenz system of differential equations.
    dx/dt = σ(y - x)
    dy/dt = x(ρ - z) - y
    dz/dt = xy - βz
