1. Tensor Calculus: The Tensor Divergence Theorem

Tensor calculus extends vector calculus to higher-dimensional and curved spaces. It is essential in general relativity, continuum mechanics, and differential geometry.

Notation: Let \(T^{i_1 \cdots i_p}{}_{j_1 \cdots j_q}\) be a \((p,q)\)-tensor field on a Riemannian manifold \(M\) with metric \(g_{ij}\). The covariant derivative is denoted \(\nabla_k\).

Theorem (Tensor Divergence/Divergence Theorem for Tensors): Let \(T^{ij}\) be a symmetric 2-tensor on a compact Riemannian manifold \(M\) with boundary \(\partial M\). Then: \[\int_M \nabla_i T^{ij} \, dV = \int_{\partial M} T^{ij} n_i \, dS\] where \(n_i\) is the unit normal to the boundary and \(dV = \sqrt{|g|} \, dx^1 \wedge \cdots \wedge dx^n\) is the volume form.

Notation Explanation:

  • \(\nabla_i\): Covariant derivative, generalizing partial derivatives to curved spaces
  • \(g_{ij}\): Metric tensor defining distances and angles
  • \(|g| = \det(g_{ij})\): Determinant of the metric
  • Christoffel symbols: \(\Gamma^k_{ij} = \frac{1}{2}g^{kl}(\partial_i g_{jl} + \partial_j g_{il} - \partial_l g_{ij})\)
Proof Sketch:

The proof follows from Stokes' theorem applied to the differential form \(\omega = T^{ij} \epsilon_{j k_2 \cdots k_n} dx^{k_2} \wedge \cdots \wedge dx^{k_n}\) where \(\epsilon\) is the Levi-Civita symbol. The divergence \(\nabla_i T^{ij}\) appears when computing \(d\omega\). In local coordinates, we use the identity \(\partial_i \sqrt{|g|} = \sqrt{|g|} \Gamma^j_{ji}\) to show that the covariant divergence integrates to a boundary term.

import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import quad
import sympy as sp

def christoffel_symbols(g, coords):
    """
    Compute Christoffel symbols Γ^k_ij for metric g.
    
    Args:
        g: metric tensor as sympy matrix
        coords: list of coordinate symbols
    
    Returns:
        Gamma[k][i][j] = Γ^k_ij
    """
    n = len(coords)
    g_inv = g.inv()
    Gamma = [[[0 for _ in range(n)] for _ in range(n)] for _ in range(n)]
    
    for k in range(n):
        for i in range(n):
            for j in range(n):
                s = 0
                for l in range(n):
                    s += g_inv[k, l] * (
                        sp.diff(g[j, l], coords[i]) + 
                        sp.diff(g[i, l], coords[j]) - 
                        sp.diff(g[i, j], coords[l])
                    )
                Gamma[k][i][j] = sp.Rational(1, 2) * s
    
    return Gamma

def covariant_derivative_tensor(T, g, coords):
    """
    Compute covariant derivative ∇_i T^j for a (1,1) tensor.
    
    Args:
        T: tensor components T^j as list
        g: metric tensor
        coords: coordinate symbols
    
    Returns:
        ∇_i T^j as 2D array
    """
    n = len(coords)
    Gamma = christoffel_symbols(g, coords)
    nabla_T = [[0 for _ in range(n)] for _ in range(n)]
    
    for i in range(n):
        for j in range(n):
            # ∇_i T^j = ∂_i T^j + Γ^j_ik T^k
            partial = sp.diff(T[j], coords[i])
            connection_terms = sum(Gamma[j][i][k] * T[k] for k in range(n))
            nabla_T[i][j] = partial + connection_terms
    
    return nabla_T

2. Complex Dynamics: The Fatou-Julia Theorem

Complex dynamics studies the iteration of holomorphic functions, particularly rational maps on the Riemann sphere. The theory reveals intricate fractal structures through the interplay of stable and chaotic behavior.

Notation: Let \(f: \hat{\mathbb{C}} \to \hat{\mathbb{C}}\) be a rational function of degree \(d \geq 2\). The Fatou set \(F(f)\) consists of points with neighborhoods where iterates form a normal family. The Julia set \(J(f) = \hat{\mathbb{C}} \setminus F(f)\).

Theorem (Fatou-Julia): For a rational function \(f\) of degree \(d \geq 2\):
  1. The Julia set \(J(f)\) is nonempty, compact, perfect, and has empty interior (unless \(J(f) = \hat{\mathbb{C}}\))
  2. \(J(f)\) is completely invariant: \(f(J(f)) = J(f) = f^{-1}(J(f))\)
  3. \(J(f)\) is the closure of the set of repelling periodic points
  4. For any open set \(U\) intersecting \(J(f)\), \(\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty} f^n(U)\) covers \(J(f)\) except at most 2 points

Notation Explanation:

  • \(f^n\): n-fold composition of \(f\) with itself
  • Normal family: family of functions where every sequence has a locally uniformly convergent subsequence
  • Repelling periodic point: \(f^n(z) = z\) with \(|(f^n)'(z)| > 1\)
  • \(\hat{\mathbb{C}} = \mathbb{C} \cup \{\infty\}\): Riemann sphere
Proof of Key Properties:

Non-emptiness: If \(J(f) = \emptyset\), then \(\{f^n\}\) is normal on all of \(\hat{\mathbb{C}}\). By Montel's theorem, a family omitting three values is normal. But a degree \(d \geq 2\) rational map takes every value \(d\) times (counting multiplicity), so iterates cannot omit three values.

Complete invariance: If \(z \in F(f)\), there's a neighborhood where iterates are normal. The same holds for \(f(z)\) and \(f^{-1}(z)\) by the definition of normality.

Repelling periodic points: Uses the fact that repelling cycles are dense in \(J(f)\), proved via the Ahlfors five islands theorem or quasiconformal surgery techniques.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def julia_set(c, xmin=-2, xmax=2, ymin=-2, ymax=2, width=800, height=800, max_iter=100):
    """
    Compute the Julia set for f(z) = z² + c.
    
    The Julia set is the boundary between points that escape to infinity
    and those that remain bounded under iteration.
    """
    x = np.linspace(xmin, xmax, width)
    y = np.linspace(ymin, ymax, height)
    X, Y = np.meshgrid(x, y)
    Z = X + 1j * Y
    
    # Iteration count
    iterations = np.zeros(Z.shape, dtype=int)
    mask = np.ones(Z.shape, dtype=bool)
    
    for i in range(max_iter):
        Z[mask] = Z[mask]**2 + c
        mask_new = np.abs(Z) < 2
        iterations[~mask_new & (iterations == 0)] = i
        mask = mask_new
    
    iterations[mask] = max_iter
    return iterations

def mandelbrot_set(xmin=-2.5, xmax=1, ymin=-1.25, ymax=1.25, width=800, height=800, max_iter=100):
    """
    Compute the Mandelbrot set (parameter space of Julia sets).
    
    The Mandelbrot set M = {c : J(f_c) is connected} where f_c(z) = z² + c.
    Equivalently, M = {c : 0 does not escape under iteration of f_c}.
    """
    x = np.linspace(xmin, xmax, width)
    y = np.linspace(ymin, ymax, height)
    X, Y = np.meshgrid(x, y)
    C = X + 1j * Y
    
    Z = np.zeros_like(C)
    iterations = np.zeros(C.shape, dtype=int)
    mask = np.ones(C.shape, dtype=True)
    
    for i in range(max_iter):
        Z[mask] = Z[mask]**2 + C[mask]
        mask_new = np.abs(Z) < 2
        iterations[~mask_new & (iterations == 0)] = i
        mask = mask_new
    
    iterations[mask] = max_iter
    return iterations

3. Functional Equations: Cauchy's Functional Equation

Functional equations seek functions satisfying specific algebraic relations. Cauchy's equation \(f(x+y) = f(x) + f(y)\) is the foundational example, with surprising depth when regularity conditions are relaxed.

Theorem (Cauchy's Functional Equation): Let \(f: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}\) satisfy \(f(x + y) = f(x) + f(y)\) for all \(x, y \in \mathbb{R}\).
  1. If \(f\) is continuous at one point (or bounded on any interval, or measurable), then \(f(x) = cx\) for some constant \(c\).
  2. Without regularity assumptions, there exist nonlinear solutions that are dense in \(\mathbb{R}^2\) and whose graph is not measurable.

Notation: A function satisfying \(f(x+y) = f(x) + f(y)\) is called additive. The constant \(c = f(1)\) when the linear solution exists.

Proof of Linear Solutions:

Step 1: \(f(0) = f(0+0) = 2f(0)\), so \(f(0) = 0\).

Step 2: By induction, \(f(nx) = nf(x)\) for all integers \(n\).

Step 3: For rationals \(q = m/n\): \(f(q) = f(m/n) = mf(1/n) = m \cdot \frac{f(1)}{n} = qf(1)\).

Step 4: If \(f\) is continuous at any point \(x_0\), then for any \(x\), choose \(q_n \to x - x_0\) rational. Then \(f(x) = f(x_0 + (x-x_0)) = \lim f(x_0 + q_n) = f(x_0) + \lim f(q_n) = f(x_0) + c(x-x_0)\). With \(x = x_0\), we get \(f(x) = cx\).

Nonlinear solutions: View \(\mathbb{R}\) as a vector space over \(\mathbb{Q}\). Using the axiom of choice (Hamel basis), there exist bases \(\{e_i\}_{i \in I}\) for \(\mathbb{R}\) over \(\mathbb{Q}\). Define \(f\) arbitrarily on basis elements and extend linearly over \(\mathbb{Q}\). This gives additive but nonlinear functions.

    """The 'nice' solution f(x) = cx."""
    return c * x

def additive_on_rationals(q, c=1):
    """
    Compute f(q) = cq for rational q = m/n.
    This is the unique additive function agreeing with f(x) = cx on Q.
    """
    if isinstance(q, Fraction):
        return c * float(q)
    return c * q

    This demonstrates how continuity extends the solution from Q to R.

    """Test if function f satisfies f(x+y) = f(x) + f(y)."""

        for y in test_points:
            lhs = f(x + y)
            rhs = f(x) + f(y)
            results.append(abs(lhs - rhs) < tolerance)
    return all(results)

1. Approximation Theory: The Weierstrass Approximation Theorem

Approximation theory studies how functions can be approximated by simpler functions, with polynomial approximation being fundamental to numerical analysis and machine learning.

Theorem (Weierstrass Approximation Theorem): Let \(f: [a, b] \to \mathbb{R}\) be continuous. For every \(\epsilon > 0\), there exists a polynomial \(p\) such that: \[\sup_{x \in [a,b]} |f(x) - p(x)| < \epsilon\] Equivalently, polynomials are dense in \(C([a,b])\) with the uniform norm.

Notation: The Bernstein polynomials provide a constructive proof: \[B_n(f; x) = \sum_{k=0}^{n} f\left(\frac{k}{n}\right) \binom{n}{k} x^k (1-x)^{n-k}\]

Proof via Bernstein Polynomials:

Key steps for \(f\) on \([0,1]\):

  1. Define \(B_n(f;x)\) as above. Note \(B_n(1;x) = 1\) and \(B_n(x;x) = x\).
  2. Variance: \(B_n((t-x)^2; x) = \frac{x(1-x)}{n} \leq \frac{1}{4n}\).
  3. By uniform continuity of \(f\), for \(\epsilon > 0\), choose \(\delta\) such that \(|x-y| < \delta \Rightarrow |f(x)-f(y)| < \epsilon/2\).
  4. Split sum based on \(|k/n - x|\):
\[|B_n(f;x) - f(x)| \leq \sum_{|k/n-x|<\delta} + \sum_{|k/n-x|\geq\delta}\]

The first sum is \(< \epsilon/2\) by continuity. The second uses Chebyshev: \[\sum_{|k/n-x|\geq\delta} \leq 2\|f\|_\infty \cdot \frac{x(1-x)}{n\delta^2} \leq \frac{\|f\|_\infty}{2n\delta^2}\]

For large \(n\), this is \(< \epsilon/2\).

import numpy as np
from scipy.special import comb
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def bernstein_polynomial(f, n, x):
    """
    Compute the nth Bernstein polynomial approximation of f at x.
    
    B_n(f; x) = Σ_{k=0}^n f(k/n) * C(n,k) * x^k * (1-x)^(n-k)
    """
    result = 0
    for k in range(n + 1):
        binomial = comb(n, k, exact=True)
        result += f(k / n) * binomial * (x ** k) * ((1 - x) ** (n - k))
    return result

def bernstein_vectorized(f, n, x_vals):
    """Vectorized Bernstein polynomial computation."""
    result = np.zeros_like(x_vals, dtype=float)
    for k in range(n + 1):
        binomial = comb(n, k, exact=True)
        result += f(k / n) * binomial * (x_vals ** k) * ((1 - x_vals) ** (n - k))
    return result

# Chebyshev comparison
def chebyshev_nodes(n, a=0, b=1):
    """Chebyshev nodes of the second kind, mapped to [a,b]."""
    k = np.arange(n + 1)
    nodes = np.cos(np.pi * k / n)  # [-1, 1]
    return (b - a) * (nodes + 1) / 2 + a  # [a, b]

def lagrange_interpolation(f, nodes, x):
    """Lagrange interpolation polynomial."""
    n = len(nodes) - 1
    result = np.zeros_like(x, dtype=float)
    for i in range(n + 1):
        Li = np.ones_like(x, dtype=float)
        for j in range(n + 1):
            if i != j:
                Li *= (x - nodes[j]) / (nodes[i] - nodes[j])
        result += f(nodes[i]) * Li
    return result

2. Random Matrix Theory: The Semicircle Law

Random matrix theory studies the statistical properties of matrices with random entries. It has profound applications in quantum physics, number theory, wireless communications, and deep learning.

Theorem (Wigner's Semicircle Law): Let \(X_n\) be an \(n \times n\) symmetric random matrix with entries \(X_{ij} = X_{ji}\) for \(i \neq j\), where:
  • \(\{X_{ij}: i \leq j\}\) are independent
  • \(\mathbb{E}[X_{ij}] = 0\), \(\mathbb{E}[X_{ij}^2] = \sigma^2\) for \(i \neq j\)
  • \(\mathbb{E}[X_{ii}^2] < \infty\) (diagonal allowed different variance)
  • \(\mathbb{E}[|X_{ij}|^k] \leq M_k < \infty\) for all \(k\) (or Lindeberg condition)
Then the empirical spectral distribution (ESD) of \(X_n/\sqrt{n}\) converges weakly to the semicircle distribution: \[\mu_{sc}(dx) = \frac{1}{2\pi\sigma^2}\sqrt{4\sigma^2 - x^2} \, \mathbf{1}_{|x| \leq 2\sigma} \, dx\]

Notation: The empirical spectral distribution is \(\mu_n = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \delta_{\lambda_i}\) where \(\lambda_i\) are eigenvalues of \(X_n/\sqrt{n}\).

Proof Sketch (Moment Method):

The semicircle distribution has Catalan numbers as moments: \[m_{2k} = \int x^{2k} \mu_{sc}(dx) = C_k = \frac{1}{k+1}\binom{2k}{k}, \quad m_{2k+1} = 0\]

For the random matrix, compute: \[\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}[\text{tr}(X_n/\sqrt{n})^{2k}] = \frac{1}{n^{k+1}}\sum_{i_1,\ldots,i_{2k}} \mathbb{E}[X_{i_1 i_2} X_{i_2 i_3} \cdots X_{i_{2k} i_1}]\]

By independence and mean zero, nonzero contributions require paired indices. This corresponds to counting certain graphs (non-crossing pairings), yielding Catalan numbers in the limit.

def marchenko_pastur_pdf(x, gamma, sigma=1):
    """Marchenko-Pastur density for sample covariance matrices."""
    lambda_plus = sigma**2 * (1 + np.sqrt(gamma))**2
    lambda_minus = sigma**2 * (1 - np.sqrt(gamma))**2
    
    pdf = np.zeros_like(x, dtype=float)
    mask = (x > lambda_minus) & (x < lambda_plus)

                    np.sqrt((lambda_plus - x[mask]) * (x[mask] - lambda_minus))
    else:
        # Add atom at 0 for gamma > 1
        pdf[mask] = (1 / (2 * np.pi * sigma**2 * gamma * x[mask])) * \
                    np.sqrt((lambda_plus - x[mask]) * (x[mask] - lambda_minus))
    
    return pdf

3. Queuing Theory: The Pollaczek-Khinchine Formula

Queuing theory models waiting lines and is essential for designing computer networks, call centers, manufacturing systems, and transportation infrastructure.

Theorem (Pollaczek-Khinchine Formula): Consider an M/G/1 queue (Poisson arrivals with rate \(\lambda\), general service time distribution with mean \(1/\mu\) and variance \(\sigma_s^2\), single server). Let \(\rho = \lambda/\mu\) be the traffic intensity. For \(\rho < 1\) (stable system), the expected waiting time in the queue is: \[\mathbb{E}[W_q] = \frac{\lambda \mathbb{E}[S^2]}{2(1-\rho)} = \frac{\rho^2 + \lambda^2 \sigma_s^2}{2\lambda(1-\rho)}\] where \(S\) is the service time random variable. The expected number in the system (Little's Law): \[\mathbb{E}[L] = \lambda \mathbb{E}[W] = \rho + \frac{\lambda^2 \mathbb{E}[S^2]}{2(1-\rho)}\]

Notation:

  • M/G/1: Markovian (Poisson) arrivals, General service, 1 server
  • \(\rho = \lambda/\mu\): Traffic intensity (utilization)
  • \(\mathbb{E}[S^2] = \text{Var}(S) + \mathbb{E}[S]^2 = \sigma_s^2 + 1/\mu^2\): Second moment of service time
Proof Sketch:

Using the residual service time approach:

  1. When a customer arrives, with probability \(\rho\) the server is busy.
  2. If busy, the customer must wait for the remaining service time of the customer in service.
  3. By the PASTA property (Poisson Arrivals See Time Averages), the expected residual service time is \(\mathbb{E}[S^2]/(2\mathbb{E}[S])\).
  4. Additionally, the customer waits for all customers already in queue: if \(L_q\) is the number in queue, this contributes \(\mathbb{E}[L_q] \cdot \mathbb{E}[S] = \lambda \mathbb{E}[W_q] \cdot \mathbb{E}[S] = \rho \mathbb{E}[W_q]\).

Thus: \(\mathbb{E}[W_q] = \rho \cdot \frac{\mathbb{E}[S^2]}{2\mathbb{E}[S]} + \rho \mathbb{E}[W_q]\)

Solving: \(\mathbb{E}[W_q](1-\rho) = \frac{\lambda \mathbb{E}[S^2]}{2}\)

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy import stats

def pollaczek_khinchine(lambda_rate, service_mean, service_var):
    """
    Compute expected waiting time using Pollaczek-Khinchine formula.
    
    Args:
        lambda_rate: arrival rate (customers per unit time)
        service_mean: mean service time
        service_var: variance of service time
    
    Returns:
        dict with various performance metrics
    """
    mu = 1 / service_mean
    rho = lambda_rate / mu
    
    if rho >= 1:
        return {'stable': False, 'rho': rho}
    
    # Second moment of service time
    E_S2 = service_var + service_mean**2
    
    # Expected waiting time in queue
    W_q = (lambda_rate * E_S2) / (2 * (1 - rho))
    
    # Expected waiting time in system
    W = W_q + service_mean
    
    # Expected number in queue (Little's Law)
    L_q = lambda_rate * W_q
    
    # Expected number in system
    L = lambda_rate * W
    
    return {
        'stable': True,
        'rho': rho,
        'W_q': W_q,
        'W': W,
        'L_q': L_q,
        'L': L,
        'E_S2': E_S2
    }

def simulate_mg1_queue(lambda_rate, service_dist, n_customers=10000, warmup=1000):
    """
    Simulate an M/G/1 queue.
    
    Args:
        lambda_rate: arrival rate
        service_dist: function that generates service times
        n_customers: number of customers to simulate
        warmup: number of initial customers to discard
    
    Returns:
        dict with simulation results
    """
    # Generate interarrival times (exponential)
    interarrivals = np.random.exponential(1/lambda_rate, n_customers + warmup)
    arrivals = np.cumsum(interarrivals)
    
    # Generate service times
    service_times = service_dist(n_customers + warmup)
    
    # Simulate queue
    departure_times = np.zeros(n_customers + warmup)
    waiting_times = np.zeros(n_customers + warmup)
    
    for i in range(n_customers + warmup):
        if i == 0:
            departure_times[i] = arrivals[i] + service_times[i]
            waiting_times[i] = 0
        else:
            # Wait if server is busy
            start_service = max(arrivals[i], departure_times[i-1])
            waiting_times[i] = start_service - arrivals[i]
            departure_times[i] = start_service + service_times[i]
    
    # Discard warmup
    waiting_times = waiting_times[warmup:]
    service_times = service_times[warmup:]
    system_times = waiting_times + service_times
    
    return {
        'mean_wait': np.mean(waiting_times),
        'mean_system_time': np.mean(system_times),
        'mean_service_time': np.mean(service_times),
        'var_service_time': np.var(service_times, ddof=1),
        'waiting_times': waiting_times,
        'system_times': system_times
    }

1. Fractal Geometry: The Hausdorff Dimension

Fractal geometry studies sets with non-integer dimension and self-similar structure. The Hausdorff dimension provides a rigorous way to measure the "roughness" of fractal sets.

Notation: For a set \(E \subset \mathbb{R}^n\) and \(s \geq 0\), the s-dimensional Hausdorff measure is: \[\mathcal{H}^s(E) = \lim_{\delta \to 0} \inf\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{\infty} |U_i|^s : E \subseteq \bigcup_{i} U_i, |U_i| \leq \delta\right\}\]

Theorem (Hausdorff Dimension): The Hausdorff dimension of a set \(E\) is: \[\dim_H(E) = \inf\{s : \mathcal{H}^s(E) = 0\} = \sup\{s : \mathcal{H}^s(E) = \infty\}\] For self-similar sets satisfying the open set condition: if \(E = \bigcup_{i=1}^{m} f_i(E)\) where each \(f_i\) is a similarity with ratio \(r_i\), then \(\dim_H(E)\) is the unique \(s\) satisfying: \[\sum_{i=1}^{m} r_i^s = 1\]
Proof for Self-Similar Sets:

Let \(E\) be the unique nonempty compact set satisfying \(E = \bigcup_{i=1}^{m} f_i(E)\) (exists by Banach fixed-point theorem on the space of compact sets with Hausdorff metric).

Upper bound: Cover \(E\) by iterates. At level \(k\), we have \(m^k\) sets of diameter \(\leq r_{\max}^k |E|\). The \(s\)-dimensional measure satisfies: \[\mathcal{H}^s(E) \leq \lim_{k \to \infty} m^k (r_{\max}^k)^s = \lim_{k \to \infty} (m r_{\max}^s)^k\]

If \(\sum r_i^s = 1\), then \(m r_{\max}^s \geq 1\) with equality only when all \(r_i = r_{\max}\). More refined analysis shows \(\mathcal{H}^s(E) < \infty\) when \(\sum r_i^s = 1\).

Lower bound: Uses the open set condition (the images \(f_i(V)\) are disjoint for some open \(V\)) and a mass distribution argument.

def koch_snowflake_points(n_iterations):
    """
    Generate Koch snowflake curve points.
    
    The Koch curve has Hausdorff dimension log(4)/log(3) ≈ 1.262.
    """
    def koch_segment(p1, p2, n):
        if n == 0:
            return [p1, p2]
        
        # Divide segment into 3 parts
        dx = (p2[0] - p1[0]) / 3
        dy = (p2[1] - p1[1]) / 3
        
        a = p1
        b = [p1[0] + dx, p1[1] + dy]
        d = [p1[0] + 2*dx, p1[1] + 2*dy]
        e = p2
        
        # Create equilateral triangle bump
        # Rotate vector (dx, dy) by 60 degrees
        cx = b[0] + dx * 0.5 - dy * np.sqrt(3)/2
        cy = b[1] + dx * np.sqrt(3)/2 + dy * 0.5
        c = [cx, cy]
        
        # Recursively subdivide
        result = []
        result.extend(koch_segment(a, b, n-1)[:-1])
        result.extend(koch_segment(b, c, n-1)[:-1])
        result.extend(koch_segment(c, d, n-1)[:-1])
        result.extend(koch_segment(d, e, n-1))
        
        return result
    
    # Start with equilateral triangle
    side = 1
    h = side * np.sqrt(3) / 2
    p1 = [0, 0]
    p2 = [side, 0]
    p3 = [side/2, h]
    
    # Generate each side
    side1 = koch_segment(p1, p2, n_iterations)
    side2 = koch_segment(p2, p3, n_iterations)
    side3 = koch_segment(p3, p1, n_iterations)
    
    # Combine (avoid duplicate points)
    points = side1[:-1] + side2[:-1] + side3

        new_intervals = []
        for a, b in intervals:
            length = (b - a) / 3
            new_intervals.append((a, a + length))
            new_intervals.append((a + 2*length, b))
        intervals = new_intervals
    
    return intervals

def box_counting_dimension(points, min_box=1e-4, max_box=1.0, n_boxes=20):
    """
    Estimate box-counting (Minkowski) dimension.
    
    D = -lim_{ε→0} log(N(ε)) / log(ε)
    where N(ε) is the number of boxes of size ε needed to cover the set.
    """
    # Normalize points to unit square
    xmin, ymin = points.min(axis=0)
    xmax, ymax = points.max(axis=0)
    normalized = (points - [xmin, ymin]) / max(xmax - xmin, ymax - ymin)
    
    box_sizes = np.logspace(np.log10(min_box), np.log10(max_box), n_boxes)
    counts = []
    
    for box_size in box_sizes:
        # Count occupied boxes
        box_indices = (normalized / box_size).astype(int)
        unique_boxes = set(map(tuple, box_indices))
        counts.append(len(unique_boxes))
    
    # Linear regression on log-log plot
    log_boxes = np.log(1 / box_sizes)
    log_counts = np.log(counts)
    
    # Fit line to middle portion
    slope = np.polyfit(log_boxes[5:-5], log_counts[5:-5], 1)[0]
    
    return slope, box_sizes, counts

2. Algebraic Combinatorics: The Hook Length Formula

Algebraic combinatorics connects combinatorial structures with algebraic methods. The hook length formula counts standard Young tableaux, with applications in representation theory and algebraic geometry.

Notation: A Young diagram (or Ferrers diagram) of partition \(\lambda = (\lambda_1 \geq \lambda_2 \geq \cdots \geq \lambda_k)\) is a left-justified array of boxes with \(\lambda_i\) boxes in row \(i\).

Theorem (Hook Length Formula - Frame-Robinson-Thrall): The number of standard Young tableaux of shape \(\lambda\) (with \(n\) boxes) is: \[f^\lambda = \frac{n!}{\prod_{(i,j) \in \lambda} h(i,j)}\] where \(h(i,j)\) is the hook length of box \((i,j)\): \[h(i,j) = \lambda_i - j + \lambda'_j - i + 1\] Here \(\lambda'_j\) is the length of column \(j\).

Notation: A standard Young tableau fills the boxes with \(\{1, 2, \ldots, n\}\) such that rows and columns increase. The hook at \((i,j)\) consists of the box itself, all boxes to the right in the same row, and all boxes below in the same column.

Proof Sketch (Probabilistic - Greene-Nijenhuis-Wilf):

Consider a random process: start at a random box and move to a uniformly random box in its hook (excluding the current box). Continue until reaching a corner. The probability that box \((i,j)\) is the last box visited is \(1/h(i,j)\).

By induction on \(n\), the probability of generating a specific SYT is \(1/(n! / \prod h(i,j))\), establishing the formula.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def hook_lengths(partition):
    """
    Compute hook lengths for a Young diagram.
    
    Args:
        partition: list of row lengths (non-increasing)
    
    Returns:
        2D list of hook lengths
    """
    n_rows = len(partition)
    # Compute column lengths (conjugate partition)
    max_col = max(partition) if partition else 0
    col_lengths = []
    for j in range(max_col):
        col_len = sum(1 for row in partition if row > j)
        col_lengths.append(col_len)
    
    hooks = []
    for i, row_len in enumerate(partition):
        row_hooks = []
        for j in range(row_len):
            # Hook length = boxes to right + boxes below + 1 (itself)
            right = row_len - j - 1
            below = col_lengths[j] - i - 1
            hook_len = right + below + 1
            row_hooks.append(hook_len)
        hooks.append(row_hooks)
    
    return hooks

def count_standard_young_tableaux(partition):
    """
    Compute number of SYT using hook length formula.
    
    f^λ = n! / ∏ hook lengths
    """
    n = sum(partition)
    hooks = hook_lengths(partition)
    
    # Compute product of hook lengths
    hook_product = 1
    for row in hooks:
        for h in row:
            hook_product *= h
    
    # f^λ = n! / hook_product
    import math
    return math.factorial(n) // hook_product

def generate_random_syt(partition):
    """
    Generate a random SYT using the Greene-Nijenhuis-Wilf algorithm.
    """
    n = sum(partition)
    # Create diagram as list of (row, col) positions
    boxes = []
    for i, row_len in enumerate(partition):
        for j in range(row_len):
            boxes.append((i, j))
    
    # Result tableau
    tableau = [[0 for _ in range(row_len)] for row_len in partition]
    
    # Fill from n down to 1
    for value in range(n, 0, -1):
        # Choose random box
        idx = np.random.randint(len(boxes))
        row, col = boxes[idx]
        
        # Hook walk: move to random box in hook until corner
        while True:
            # Find hook (same row to right, same column below)
            hook = [(row, c) for c in range(col + 1, partition[row]) if (row, c) in boxes]
            hook.extend([(r, col) for r in range(row + 1, len(partition)) 
                        if col < partition[r] and (r, col) in boxes])
            
            if not hook:  # Corner reached
                break
            
            row, col = hook[np.random.randint(len(hook))]
        
        # Place value at corner
        tableau[row][col] = value
        boxes.remove((row, col))
    
    return tableau

def is_valid_syt(tableau):
    """Check if tableau is valid (rows and columns increasing)."""
    # Check rows
    for row in tableau:
        for i in range(len(row) - 1):
            if row[i] >= row[i+1]:
                return False
    
    # Check columns
    max_col = max(len(row) for row in tableau)
    for col in range(max_col):
        col_vals = []
        for row in tableau:
            if col < len(row):
                col_vals.append(row[col])
        for i in range(len(col_vals) - 1):
            if col_vals[i] >= col_vals[i+1]:
                return False
    
    return True

3. Inverse Problems: The Moore-Penrose Pseudoinverse

Inverse problems seek to determine causes from observed effects. They arise in medical imaging (CT, MRI), geophysics, astronomy, and machine learning. The Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse provides the least-squares solution to linear inverse problems.

Theorem (Moore-Penrose Pseudoinverse): For any matrix \(A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}\), there exists a unique matrix \(A^+ \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}\) satisfying:
  1. \(AA^+A = A\) (generalized inverse)
  2. \(A^+AA^+ = A^+\) (reflexive)
  3. \((AA^+)^T = AA^+\) (orthogonal projection onto range)
  4. \((A^+A)^T = A^+A\) (orthogonal projection onto row space)
If \(A = U\Sigma V^T\) is the SVD with \(\Sigma = \text{diag}(\sigma_1, \ldots, \sigma_r, 0, \ldots)\), then: \[A^+ = V\Sigma^+ U^T, \quad \Sigma^+ = \text{diag}(1/\sigma_1, \ldots, 1/\sigma_r, 0, \ldots)\]

Notation: The pseudoinverse gives the minimum-norm least-squares solution to \(Ax = b\): \(x^* = A^+b\) minimizes \(\|Ax - b\|_2\) and among all minimizers, has smallest \(\|x\|_2\).

Proof of Properties:

Existence: Via SVD: \(A = U\Sigma V^T\), define \(A^+ = V\Sigma^+ U^T\).

Uniqueness: Suppose \(X\) and \(Y\) both satisfy the four conditions. Then: \[X = XAX = X(AX)^T = XX^TA^T = XX^T(AYA)^T = XX^TA^TY^TA^T\] Continuing and using all four conditions shows \(X = Y\).

Least squares: The normal equations \(A^TAx = A^Tb\) have solution \(x = (A^TA)^+A^Tb = A^+b\) (when \(A\) has full column rank). For general \(A\), \(A^+b\) projects \(b\) onto the range of \(A\) and finds the minimum norm preimage.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy import linalg

def pseudoinverse_svd(A, rcond=1e-15):
    """
    Compute Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse using SVD.
    
    A^+ = V Σ^+ U^T where Σ^+ has reciprocals of nonzero singular values.
    """
    U, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(A, full_matrices=False)
    
    # Threshold for numerical rank
    threshold = rcond * s[0] if len(s) > 0 else 0
    
    # Compute Σ^+
    s_plus = np.array([1/si if si > threshold else 0 for si in s])
    
    # A^+ = V Σ^+ U^T
    return Vt.T @ np.diag(s_plus) @ U.T

def verify_pseudoinverse_properties(A, A_plus):
    """Verify the four Moore-Penrose conditions."""
    props = {
        'AA+A = A': np.allclose(A @ A_plus @ A, A),
        'A+AA+ = A+': np.allclose(A_plus @ A @ A_plus, A_plus),
        '(AA+)^T = AA+': np.allclose((A @ A_plus).T, A @ A_plus),
        '(A+A)^T = A+A': np.allclose((A_plus @ A).T, A_plus @ A)
    }
    return props

    """Create Gaussian blur matrix."""

def tikhonov_regularization(A, b, lambda_reg):
    """
    Tikhonov regularization: min ||Ax - b||² + λ||x||²

    n = A.shape[1]
    return np.linalg.solve(A.T @ A + lambda_reg * np.eye(n), A.T @ b)

4. Geometric Group Theory: The Svarc-Milnor Lemma

Geometric group theory studies groups by connecting their algebraic properties to the geometry of spaces they act upon. The Svarc-Milnor lemma establishes quasi-isometry between a group and its Cayley graph.

Notation: A Cayley graph \(\text{Cay}(G, S)\) of a group \(G\) with generating set \(S\) has vertices \(G\) and edges \((g, gs)\) for \(g \in G, s \in S\).

Theorem (Svarc-Milnor Lemma): Let \(G\) be a group acting properly discontinuously, cocompactly by isometries on a proper geodesic metric space \(X\). Then \(G\) is finitely generated and for any basepoint \(x_0 \in X\), the orbit map: \[\phi: G \to X, \quad \phi(g) = g \cdot x_0\] is a quasi-isometry. In particular, \(G\) with any word metric is quasi-isometric to \(X\).

Notation:

  • Quasi-isometry: A map \(f: X \to Y\) is a quasi-isometry if there exist \(K \geq 1, C \geq 0\) such that: \[\frac{1}{K}d_X(x,y) - C \leq d_Y(f(x),f(y)) \leq Kd_X(x,y) + C\] and every point in \(Y\) is within distance \(C\) of \(f(X)\).
  • Proper action: For all compact \(K \subset X\), the set \(\{g : gK \cap K \neq \emptyset\}\) is finite.
  • Cocompact: \(X/G\) is compact.
Proof Sketch:

Step 1: Choose compact \(K \subset X\) with \(G \cdot K = X\) (cocompactness).

Step 2: Let \(S = \{g \in G : gK \cap K \neq \emptyset\}\). By properness, \(S\) is finite.

Step 3: Show \(S\) generates \(G\). For any \(g \in G\), connect \(x_0\) to \(gx_0\) by geodesic. Cover with finitely many \(G\)-translates of \(K\), giving a sequence \(g_0 = e, g_1, \ldots, g_n = g\) where consecutive elements differ by \(S\).

Step 4: The orbit map is a quasi-isometry. The upper bound follows from the triangle inequality. The lower bound uses that the action is proper and \(K\) is compact.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from collections import deque

class CayleyGraph:
    """
    Represents a Cayley graph for a finitely generated group.
    For visualization, we use free abelian groups Z^n and free groups F_n.
    """
    
    def __init__(self, group_type, generators, max_depth=5):
        """
        Args:
            group_type: 'Z2' (Z×Z), 'free' (free group), or 'modular' (SL(2,Z))
            generators: list of generator labels
            max_depth: maximum word length to explore
        """
        self.group_type = group_type
        self.generators = generators
        self.max_depth = max_depth
        self.vertices = set()
        self.edges = []
        self.positions = {}  # For visualization
        
        self._build_graph()
    
    def _build_graph(self):
        """Build the Cayley graph up to max_depth."""
        if self.group_type == 'Z2':
            self._build_z2_graph()
        elif self.group_type == 'free':
            self._build_free_group_graph()
        elif self.group_type == 'modular':
            self._build_modular_graph()
    
    def _build_z2_graph(self):
        """Build Cayley graph of Z × Z with generators a=(1,0), b=(0,1)."""
        # Vertices are integer lattice points
        n = self.max_depth
        for i in range(-n, n+1):
            for j in range(-n, n+1):
                if abs(i) + abs(j) <= n:  # Diamond shape
                    vertex = (i, j)
                    self.vertices.add(vertex)
                    self.positions[vertex] = (i, j)
        
        # Edges connect points at distance 1
        for v in self.vertices:
            i, j = v
            neighbors = [(i+1, j), (i-1, j), (i, j+1), (i, j-1)]
            for w in neighbors:
                if w in self.vertices:
                    self.edges.append((v, w))
    
    def _build_free_group_graph(self):
        """Build Cayley graph of free group F_2 (infinite 4-regular tree)."""
        # Use reduced words as vertices
        # Generators: a, b, A=a^{-1}, B=b^{-1}
        gens = ['a', 'b', 'A', 'B']
        inverses = {'a': 'A', 'A': 'a', 'b': 'B', 'B': 'b'}
        
        # BFS from identity
        identity = ''
        self.vertices.add(identity)
        self.positions[identity] = (0, 0)
        
        queue = deque([(identity, 0)])
        visited = {identity}
        
        # Simple layout: radial
        angles = {'a': 0, 'b': np.pi/2, 'A': np.pi, 'B': -np.pi/2}
        
        while queue:
            word, depth = queue.popleft()
            if depth >= self.max_depth:
                continue
            
            for g in gens:
                # Check if reduced (not canceling last generator)
                if word and word[-1] == inverses[g]:
                    continue
                
                new_word = word + g if word else g
                
                if new_word not in visited:
                    visited.add(new_word)
                    self.vertices.add(new_word)
                    
                    # Simple tree layout
                    if word:
                        px, py = self.positions[word]
                        angle = angles[g] + 0.1 * (hash(word) % 10 - 5) / 10
                        dist = 1.0 / (depth + 1)
                        self.positions[new_word] = (px + dist * np.cos(angle), 
                                                     py + dist * np.sin(angle))
                    
                    self.edges.append((word, new_word))
                    queue.append((new_word, depth + 1))
    
    def _build_modular_graph(self):
        """Build partial Cayley graph of PSL(2,Z)."""
        # PSL(2,Z) is generated by S: z -> -1/z and T: z -> z+1
        # We'll use a finite portion
        # Represent elements as 2x2 matrices [[a,b],[c,d]] with ad-bc=1
        
        def mat_mult(A, B):
            return ((A[0][0]*B[0][0] + A[0][1]*B[1][0], A[0][0]*B[0][1] + A[0][1]*B[1][1]),
                    (A[1][0]*B[0][0] + A[1][1]*B[1][0], A[1][0]*B[0][1] + A[1][1]*B[1][1]))
        
        S = ((0, -1), (1, 0))   # z -> -1/z
        T = ((1, 1), (0, 1))    # z -> z+1
        
        identity = ((1, 0), (0, 1))
        
        # BFS
        queue = deque([(identity, 0)])
        visited = {identity: (0, 0)}
        self.vertices.add(identity)
        self.positions[identity] = (0, 0)
        
        while queue:
            mat, depth = queue.popleft()
            if depth >= self.max_depth:
                continue
            
            for gen, gen_mat in [('S', S), ('T', T)]:
                new_mat = mat_mult(mat, gen_mat)
                
                # Normalize (handle PSL vs SL)
                if new_mat not in visited and (-new_mat[0][0], -new_mat[0][1], 
                                                -new_mat[1][0], -new_mat[1][1]) not in visited:
                    visited[new_mat] = True
                    self.vertices.add(new_mat)
                    
                    # Position based on action on i (upper half-plane)
                    a, b, c, d = new_mat[0][0], new_mat[0][1], new_mat[1][0], new_mat[1][1]
                    # z = (ai + b) / (ci + d)
                    denom = (c**2 + d**2)
                    if denom > 0:
                        x = (a*c + b*d) / denom
                        y = 1 / denom  # Im(z)
                        self.positions[new_mat] = (x, y)
                    else:
                        self.positions[new_mat] = (0, 0)
                    
                    self.edges.append((mat, new_mat))
                    queue.append((new_mat, depth + 1))
    
    def word_metric(self, v1, v2):
        """Compute word metric distance between vertices."""
        if self.group_type == 'Z2':
            # L1 distance for Z^2
            return abs(v1[0] - v2[0]) + abs(v1[1] - v2[1])
        return None  # Not implemented for other groups
    
    def visualize(self, filename=None):
        """Visualize the Cayley graph."""
        fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(10, 10))
        
        # Draw edges
        for v1, v2 in self.edges:
            if v1 in self.positions and v2 in self.positions:
                p1 = self.positions[v1]
                p2 = self.positions[v2]
                ax.plot([p1[0], p2[0]], [p1[1], p2[1]], 'b-', alpha=0.5, linewidth=0.5)
        
        # Draw vertices
        xs = [self.positions[v][0] for v in self.vertices if v in self.positions]
        ys = [self.positions[v][1] for v in self.vertices if v in self.positions]
        ax.scatter(xs, ys, s=20, c='red', zorder=5)
        
        ax.set_aspect('equal')
        ax.axis('off')
        ax.set_title(f'Cayley Graph of {self.group_type} (depth ≤ {self.max_depth})')
        
        if filename:
            plt.savefig(filename, dpi=150, bbox_inches='tight')
        plt.close()

The Development of Abstract Algebra: From Equations to Structures

The 19th century witnessed a profound transformation in mathematics: the shift from studying specific objects to studying abstract structures. This revolution, now called "abstract algebra," emerged from attempts to solve polynomial equations.

1770-1830: The Solvability Crisis

Mathematicians had long sought formulas for solving polynomial equations. The quadratic formula was known to Babylonians. Cardano published solutions for cubics and quartics in 1545. But the quintic resisted all attempts.

In 1799, Paolo Ruffini sketched a proof that the general quintic has no solution in radicals, though his argument had gaps. The complete proof came from Niels Henrik Abel in 1824, who showed that no such formula could exist.

1830-1850: Galois and Group Theory

Évariste Galois, killed in a duel at age 20 in 1832, transformed the question entirely. Rather than asking "what is the solution?" he asked "what symmetries does the equation have?" He associated to each polynomial equation a group of permutations of its roots—the Galois group—and proved that the equation is solvable by radicals if and only if this group is "solvable" in the group-theoretic sense.

Galois's work, published posthumously by Liouville in 1846, was initially obscure. It took decades for mathematicians to understand that he had invented group theory.

1870-1900: The Structural Revolution

Richard Dedekind, in his lectures at Göttingen, began presenting Galois theory abstractly, speaking of "fields" and "groups" as independent structures. In 1871, he defined ideals in rings of algebraic integers, unifying the work of Kummer on Fermat's Last Theorem.

Heinrich Weber's 1895 textbook Lehrbuch der Algebra presented groups, rings, and fields as abstract structures. Emmy Noether, in the 1920s, would complete this revolution, showing that all of linear algebra and invariant theory could be understood through abstract structures.

Key Insight: The shift from "what is X?" to "what are the morphisms between X and Y?"—from objects to structures—became the defining characteristic of modern mathematics.
