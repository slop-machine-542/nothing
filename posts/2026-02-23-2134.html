Algebraic Geometry: The Language of Schemes

Tags: algebraic-geometry schemes sheaf-theory

Motivation

Algebraic geometry studies solutions to polynomial equations, but the classical approach using varieties has limitations. Schemes, introduced by Alexander Grothendieck in the 1960s, revolutionized the field by incorporating nilpotent elements and providing a unified framework for arithmetic and geometry. This allows algebraic geometers to study phenomena over any ring, not just algebraically closed fields, enabling powerful connections between number theory and geometry.

Definitions

An affine scheme is a locally ringed space isomorphic to \(\text{Spec}(A)\) for some commutative ring \(A\), where the underlying topological space consists of prime ideals of \(A\) with the Zariski topology.

A scheme is a locally ringed space \((X, \mathcal{O}_X)\) that admits an open cover by affine schemes. The structure sheaf \(\mathcal{O}_X\) assigns to each open set a ring of "regular functions."

A morphism of schemes \(f: X \to Y\) is a morphism of locally ringed spaces, consisting of a continuous map on underlying topological spaces together with a morphism of sheaves \(f^\#: \mathcal{O}_Y \to f_*\mathcal{O}_X\).

Theorem and Proof

Theorem (Serre's GAGA): Let \(X\) be a proper scheme over \(\mathbb{C}\). The functor from coherent sheaves on \(X\) to coherent analytic sheaves on \(X^{an}\) is an equivalence of categories.

Proof Sketch: GAGA (Géométrie Algébrique et Géométrie Analytique) establishes that for proper varieties, algebraic and analytic cohomology coincide.

Step 1: Show that for a coherent algebraic sheaf \(\mathcal{F}\) on \(X\), the natural map \(\mathcal{F} \to (\mathcal{F}^{an})^{alg}\) is an isomorphism.

Step 2: Prove that the analytification functor is fully faithful by showing: \[\text{Hom}_{\mathcal{O}_X}(\mathcal{F}, \mathcal{G}) \cong \text{Hom}_{\mathcal{O}_X^{an}}(\mathcal{F}^{an}, \mathcal{G}^{an})\]

Step 3: Establish essential surjectivity: every coherent analytic sheaf comes from an algebraic sheaf. This uses the properness hypothesis crucially via Chow's lemma and cohomological methods. ∎

Notation Guide

  • \(\text{Spec}(A)\) — Spectrum of ring \(A\)
  • \(\mathcal{O}_X\) — Structure sheaf
  • \(\mathfrak{p} \in \text{Spec}(A)\) — Prime ideal
  • \(A_\mathfrak{p}\) — Localization at \(\mathfrak{p}\)
  • \(\kappa(\mathfrak{p}) = A_\mathfrak{p}/\mathfrak{p}A_\mathfrak{p}\) — Residue field
  • \(X^{an}\) — Analytification of scheme \(X\)

Algebraic K-Theory: From Vector Bundles to Higher Invariants

Tags: k-theory algebraic-topology homological-algebra

Motivation

K-theory originated in the late 1950s through the work of Grothendieck in algebraic geometry and Atiyah-Hirzebruch in topology. It provides a way to study topological spaces and rings by examining their vector bundles or projective modules. The "K" stands for "Klasse" (German for class), reflecting its origins in classification problems. Today, K-theory connects algebraic geometry, topology, number theory, and even physics, where it appears in string theory and condensed matter physics.

Definitions

For a compact Hausdorff space \(X\), the complex K-theory \(K^0(X)\) is the Grothendieck group of isomorphism classes of complex vector bundles over \(X\). Explicitly: \[K^0(X) = \text{Gr}(\text{Vect}_\mathbb{C}(X), \oplus)\]

For a ring \(R\), algebraic K-theory \(K_0(R)\) is the Grothendieck group of finitely generated projective \(R\)-modules.

The reduced K-theory \(\tilde{K}^0(X)\) is the kernel of the rank homomorphism \(K^0(X) \to K^0(\text{pt}) = \mathbb{Z}\).

Theorem and Proof

Theorem (Bott Periodicity): For a compact Hausdorff space \(X\): \[K^0(X) \cong \tilde{K}^0(S^2 \wedge X) \cong K^{-2}(X)\] Equivalently, \(\tilde{K}^0(S^n)\) is periodic with period 2: \[\tilde{K}^0(S^n) = \begin{cases} \mathbb{Z} & n \text{ even} \\ 0 & n \text{ odd} \end{cases}\]

Proof Sketch: Bott periodicity is proved using the clutching construction and analysis of loop spaces.

Step 1: Show that \(\Omega U \simeq \mathbb{Z} \times BU\), where \(U\) is the infinite unitary group and \(BU\) is its classifying space.

Step 2: The suspension isomorphism gives \(K^{-1}(X) = K^0(SX)\), where \(SX\) is the reduced suspension.

Step 3: Using the fact that \(K^0(X) = [X, \mathbb{Z} \times BU]\) (homotopy classes of maps), we compute: \[K^{-2}(X) = K^{-1}(SX) = [S^2X, \mathbb{Z} \times BU] = [X, \Omega^2(\mathbb{Z} \times BU)]\]

Step 4: By Bott's result, \(\Omega^2 U \simeq U\), which implies \(\Omega^2 BU \simeq \mathbb{Z} \times BU\). This gives the periodicity. ∎

Notation Guide

  • \(K^0(X)\) — Topological K-theory (even degree)
  • \(K^{-n}(X)\) — Higher K-groups via suspension
  • \(K_0(R)\) — Algebraic K-theory of ring \(R\)
  • \(\text{Vect}_\mathbb{C}(X)\) — Category of complex vector bundles
  • \(BU\) — Classifying space for the unitary group
  • \(\Omega X\) — Loop space of \(X\)

        """
        Compute reduced K-theory of S^n using Bott periodicity.
        """

            # Even spheres: K-tilde^0(S^{2k}) = Z
            return {'K0': 'Z', 'K1': 0}
        else:
            # Odd spheres: K-tilde^0(S^{2k+1}) = 0
            return {'K0': 0, 'K1': 'Z'}

    def k_theory_projective_space(n, field='complex'):
        """
        K-theory of complex/real projective space.
        """
        if field == 'complex':
            # CP^n: K^0 = Z[ξ]/(ξ^{n+1}) where ξ = 1 - [L]
            # L is the tautological line bundle
            return {
                'description': f'K^0(CP^{n}) = Z[ξ]/(ξ^{n+1})',
                'rank': n + 1,
                'generator': 'ξ = 1 - [tautological line bundle]'
            }

            # RP^n is more complicated
            return {'description': 'KO(RP^n) has torsion'}

    def chern_character(roots):
        """
        Compute Chern character from Chern roots.
        ch(E) = sum exp(x_i) where x_i are Chern roots.

        c1 = sum(roots)
        c2 = sum(x*y for i, x in enumerate(roots) 
                    for y in roots[i+1:])
        
        return {
            'rank': rank,
            'ch_0': rank,
            'ch_1': c1,

            'E2_page': f'E_2^{{p,q}} = H^p({X_type}; K^q(pt))',

Ergodic Theory: The Long-Term Behavior of Dynamical Systems

Tags: ergodic-theory dynamical-systems measure-theory

Motivation

Ergodic theory studies dynamical systems from a statistical perspective, asking when time averages equal space averages. Originating in Boltzmann's work on statistical mechanics, the field was put on rigorous foundations by Birkhoff, von Neumann, and Kolmogorov. Today, ergodic theory has applications to number theory (uniform distribution), combinatorics (Szemerédi's theorem), and even computer science (pseudorandomness).

Definitions

A measure-preserving dynamical system is a quadruple \((X, \mathcal{B}, \mu, T)\) where \((X, \mathcal{B}, \mu)\) is a probability space and \(T: X \to X\) is a measurable map with \(\mu(T^{-1}(A)) = \mu(A)\) for all measurable \(A\).

A system is ergodic if the only invariant measurable sets have measure 0 or 1: \[T^{-1}(A) = A \implies \mu(A) \in \{0, 1\}\]

The time average of an observable \(f\) is: \[\hat{f}(x) = \lim_{N \to \infty} \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} f(T^n x)\]

Theorem and Proof

Theorem (Birkhoff's Ergodic Theorem): Let \((X, \mathcal{B}, \mu, T)\) be a measure-preserving system and \(f \in L^1(X, \mu)\). Then the time average \(\hat{f}(x)\) exists for \(\mu\)-almost every \(x\). Moreover, if the system is ergodic: \[\hat{f}(x) = \int_X f \, d\mu \quad \text{a.e.}\]

Proof Sketch: Define \(f^*(x) = \limsup_{N \to \infty} \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} f(T^n x)\).

Step 1: Show \(f^* \circ T = f^*\) (invariance). This follows from: \[f^*(Tx) = \limsup_{N \to \infty} \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} f(T^{n+1}x) = f^*(x)\]

Step 2: For any \(\alpha \in \mathbb{R}\), the set \(A_\alpha = \{x : f^*(x) > \alpha\}\) is invariant, so by ergodicity has measure 0 or 1.

Step 3: Apply the maximal ergodic lemma: if \(g \in L^1\) and \(E = \{x : \sup_{N \geq 1} \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} g(T^n x) > 0\}\), then \(\int_E g \, d\mu \geq 0\).

Step 4: Use the maximal lemma to show \(\int f^* \, d\mu \leq \int f \, d\mu\) and the reverse inequality, yielding equality. ∎

Notation Guide

  • \((X, \mathcal{B}, \mu)\) — Probability space
  • \(T: X \to X\) — Measure-preserving transformation
  • \(L^p(X, \mu)\) — Lebesgue space
  • \(\hat{f}\) — Time average of \(f\)
  • \(\bar{f} = \int f \, d\mu\) — Space average
  • \(U_T f = f \circ T\) — Koopman operator

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

class ErgodicTheory:
    """Demonstrations of ergodic theory concepts."""
    
    @staticmethod
    def circle_rotation(x0, alpha, n_steps):
        """
        Irrational rotation on circle: T(x) = x + alpha mod 1.
        This is the prototypical ergodic system.
        """
        trajectory = np.zeros(n_steps)
        x = x0
        for i in range(n_steps):
            trajectory[i] = x
            x = (x + alpha) % 1
        return trajectory
    
    @staticmethod
    def doubling_map(x0, n_steps):
        """
        Doubling map: T(x) = 2x mod 1.
        Chaotic, ergodic, mixing.
        """
        trajectory = np.zeros(n_steps)
        x = x0
        for i in range(n_steps):
            trajectory[i] = x
            x = (2 * x) % 1
        return trajectory
    
    @staticmethod
    def time_average(trajectory, observable, n_samples=None):
        """
        Compute time average of observable along trajectory.
        """
        if n_samples is None:
            n_samples = len(trajectory)
        values = observable(trajectory[:n_samples])
        return np.cumsum(values) / np.arange(1, n_samples + 1)
    
    @staticmethod
    def check_ergodic_theorem(trajectory, observable, expected_average, bins=10):
        """
        Verify Birkhoff's ergodic theorem.
        """
        time_avg = ErgodicTheory.time_average(trajectory, observable)
        final_time_avg = time_avg[-1]
        
        # Check convergence
        convergence = np.abs(final_time_avg - expected_average)
        
        return {
            'time_average': final_time_avg,
            'space_average': expected_average,
            'error': convergence,
            'converged': convergence < 0.01
        }
    
    @staticmethod
    def correlation_function(trajectory, lag_max):
        """
        Compute autocorrelation: measures mixing rate.
        """
        n = len(trajectory)
        mean = np.mean(trajectory)
        var = np.var(trajectory)
        
        correlations = []
        for lag in range(lag_max):
            if lag == 0:
                corr = 1.0
            else:
                corr = np.mean((trajectory[:-lag] - mean) * 
                               (trajectory[lag:] - mean)) / var
            correlations.append(corr)
        
        return np.array(correlations)

Game Theory: Strategic Interaction and Equilibrium

Tags: game-theory economics nash-equilibrium

Real-World Motivation

Game theory provides the mathematical foundation for understanding strategic interactions among rational agents. From auction design and market mechanisms to evolutionary biology and cybersecurity, game-theoretic concepts shape modern decision-making. The 1994 and 2005 Nobel Prizes in Economics recognized game theory's importance, with applications ranging from FCC spectrum auctions (generating billions in revenue) to nuclear deterrence strategy.

Definitions

A normal-form game is a triple \((N, (S_i)_{i \in N}, (u_i)_{i \in N})\) where \(N\) is the set of players, \(S_i\) is player \(i\)'s strategy set, and \(u_i: S \to \mathbb{R}\) is the payoff function, where \(S = \prod_{i \in N} S_i\).

A Nash equilibrium is a strategy profile \(s^* = (s_1^*, \ldots, s_n^*)\) such that for each player \(i\): \[u_i(s_i^*, s_{-i}^*) \geq u_i(s_i, s_{-i}^*) \quad \forall s_i \in S_i\]

A mixed strategy is a probability distribution over pure strategies. A mixed Nash equilibrium satisfies the above inequality in expectation.

Theorem and Proof

Theorem (Nash, 1950): Every finite normal-form game has at least one Nash equilibrium in mixed strategies.

Proof (via Brouwer Fixed-Point Theorem):

Step 1: Define the best-response correspondence. For mixed strategy profile \(\sigma\), let: \[BR_i(\sigma) = \arg\max_{\sigma_i'} u_i(\sigma_i', \sigma_{-i})\]

Step 2: Define the combined best-response \(BR(\sigma) = \prod_i BR_i(\sigma)\). A fixed point of \(BR\) is a Nash equilibrium.

Step 3: Construct a continuous function whose fixed points correspond to Nash equilibria. Define: \[g_{i,s_i}(\sigma) = \max\{0, u_i(s_i, \sigma_{-i}) - u_i(\sigma)\}\]

Step 4: Define the update function: \[f_{i,s_i}(\sigma) = \frac{\sigma_i(s_i) + g_{i,s_i}(\sigma)}{1 + \sum_{s_i'} g_{i,s_i'}(\sigma)}\]

Step 5: By Brouwer's fixed-point theorem, \(f\) has a fixed point. At a fixed point, if \(\sigma_i(s_i) > 0\) then \(g_{i,s_i}(\sigma) = 0\), meaning \(s_i\) is a best response. Thus the fixed point is a Nash equilibrium. ∎

Notation Guide

  • \(N = \{1, \ldots, n\}\) — Set of players
  • \(S_i\) — Strategy set of player \(i\)
  • \(s_{-i} = (s_1, \ldots, s_{i-1}, s_{i+1}, \ldots, s_n)\) — Strategies of others
  • \(\Delta(S_i)\) — Set of mixed strategies
  • \(u_i(s)\) — Payoff function
  • \(BR_i\) — Best-response correspondence

import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import minimize
from itertools import product

class GameTheory:
    """Game theory computations and equilibrium finding."""
    
    @staticmethod
    def payoff_matrix_game(A, B=None):
        """
        Define a 2-player game.
        A: payoff matrix for player 1 (row player)
        B: payoff matrix for player 2 (column player)
        If B is None, it's a zero-sum game (B = -A).
        """
        if B is None:
            B = -A
        return {'A': A, 'B': B}
    
    @staticmethod
    def expected_payoff(A, p, q):
        """
        Expected payoff for row player when playing p against q.
        p: mixed strategy for row player
        q: mixed strategy for column player
        """
        return p @ A @ q
    
    @staticmethod
    def best_response_row(A, q):
        """
        Best response for row player against column strategy q.
        Returns the set of pure best responses.
        """
        expected_payoffs = A @ q
        max_payoff = np.max(expected_payoffs)
        return np.where(np.isclose(expected_payoffs, max_payoff))[0]
    
    @staticmethod
    def best_response_col(B, p):
        """
        Best response for column player against row strategy p.
        """
        expected_payoffs = p @ B
        max_payoff = np.max(expected_payoffs)
        return np.where(np.isclose(expected_payoffs, max_payoff))[0]
    
    @staticmethod
    def is_nash_equilibrium(A, B, p, q, tol=1e-6):
        """
        Check if (p, q) is a Nash equilibrium.
        """
        # Check if p is best response to q
        br_row = GameTheory.best_response_row(A, q)
        p_support = np.where(p > tol)[0]
        
        # Check if q is best response to p
        br_col = GameTheory.best_response_col(B, p)
        q_support = np.where(q > tol)[0]
        
        is_br_row = all(s in br_row for s in p_support)
        is_br_col = all(s in br_col for s in q_support)
        
        return is_br_row and is_br_col
    
    @staticmethod
    def prisoners_dilemma():
        """
        Prisoner's Dilemma payoff matrices.
        Strategies: 0 = Cooperate, 1 = Defect
        """
        # (T, S, R, P) = (5, 0, 3, 1)
        A = np.array([[3, 0],   # Row player payoffs
                      [5, 1]])
        B = np.array([[3, 5],   # Column player payoffs
                      [0, 1]])
        return A, B
    
    @staticmethod
    def matching_pennies():
        """
        Matching Pennies: zero-sum game with no pure strategy NE.
        """
        A = np.array([[1, -1],
                      [-1, 1]])
        return A, -A
    
    @staticmethod
    def compute_mixed_equilibrium_2x2(A, B):
        """
        Compute mixed Nash equilibrium for 2x2 game.
        """
        # For 2x2, solve for indifference
        # Player 1 plays p = (p1, 1-p1) to make player 2 indifferent
        # Player 2 plays q = (q1, 1-q1) to make player 1 indifferent
        
        # Player 2's indifference: q such that A[0,:] @ q = A[1,:] @ q
        a, b, c, d = A[0,0], A[0,1], A[1,0], A[1,1]
        if abs((a - c) + (d - b)) > 1e-10:
            q1 = (d - b) / ((a - c) + (d - b))
            q1 = np.clip(q1, 0, 1)
        else:
            q1 = 0.5
        
        # Player 1's indifference: p such that p @ B[:,0] = p @ B[:,1]
        w, x, y, z = B[0,0], B[0,1], B[1,0], B[1,1]
        if abs((w - x) + (z - y)) > 1e-10:
            p1 = (z - y) / ((w - x) + (z - y))
            p1 = np.clip(p1, 0, 1)
        else:
            p1 = 0.5
        
        return np.array([p1, 1-p1]), np.array([q1, 1-q1])

Signal Processing: Sampling, Filtering, and Reconstruction

Tags: signal-processing fourier-analysis information-theory

Real-World Motivation

Signal processing underlies modern technology: from the audio compression in streaming services to medical imaging (MRI, CT scans), from wireless communications to radar systems. The Nyquist-Shannon sampling theorem, discovered independently by multiple researchers in the 1920s-1940s, establishes the fundamental limits of digital signal representation, enabling the digital revolution that transformed music, telecommunications, and scientific instrumentation.

Definitions

A continuous-time signal is a function \(x: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{C}\). A discrete-time signal is a sequence \(x[n]\) for \(n \in \mathbb{Z}\).

The Fourier transform of a signal is: \[X(f) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x(t) e^{-2\pi i f t} dt\]

A signal is bandlimited with bandwidth \(B\) if \(X(f) = 0\) for \(|f| > B\).

The sampling rate is \(f_s = 1/T\) where \(T\) is the sampling interval.

Theorem and Proof

Theorem (Nyquist-Shannon Sampling Theorem): Let \(x(t)\) be a continuous-time signal bandlimited to \([-B, B]\). If \(x(t)\) is sampled at rate \(f_s > 2B\), then \(x(t)\) can be perfectly reconstructed from its samples \(x[n] = x(nT)\) where \(T = 1/f_s\): \[x(t) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} x[n] \cdot \text{sinc}\left(\frac{t - nT}{T}\right)\]

Proof:

Step 1: The sampled signal can be represented as: \[x_s(t) = x(t) \cdot \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} \delta(t - nT) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} x(nT) \delta(t - nT)\]

Step 2: In the frequency domain, multiplication becomes convolution: \[X_s(f) = X(f) * \frac{1}{T}\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} \delta\left(f - \frac{k}{T}\right) = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} X\left(f - \frac{k}{T}\right)\]

Step 3: If \(f_s = 1/T > 2B\), the copies of \(X(f)\) don't overlap (no aliasing).

Step 4: Apply an ideal lowpass filter with cutoff \(f_s/2\) to recover \(X(f)\). The impulse response of this filter is \(\text{sinc}(t/T)\), giving the reconstruction formula. ∎

Notation Guide

  • \(x(t)\) — Continuous-time signal
  • \(x[n] = x(nT)\) — Discrete samples
  • \(X(f)\) — Fourier transform
  • \(f_s = 1/T\) — Sampling frequency
  • \(B\) — Bandwidth
  • \(\text{sinc}(x) = \sin(\pi x)/(\pi x)\) — Sinc function
  • \(h[n]\) — Discrete filter impulse response

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy import signal as sig

class SignalProcessing:
    """Digital signal processing fundamentals."""
    
    @staticmethod
    def sample_signal(x_func, t_max, fs):
        """
        Sample a continuous signal at rate fs.
        """
        T = 1 / fs
        n_samples = int(t_max * fs)
        n = np.arange(n_samples)
        t_sampled = n * T
        x_sampled = x_func(t_sampled)
        return t_sampled, x_sampled
    
    @staticmethod
    def sinc_interpolation(t, x_samples, T):
        """
        Reconstruct signal using sinc interpolation.
        x(t) = sum_n x[n] * sinc((t - nT)/T)
        """
        n = np.arange(len(x_samples))
        result = np.zeros_like(t)
        for i, ti in enumerate(t):
            result[i] = np.sum(x_samples * np.sinc((ti - n * T) / T))
        return result
    
    @staticmethod
    def dft(x):
        """
        Discrete Fourier Transform.
        X[k] = sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n] * exp(-2πi * k * n / N)
        """
        N = len(x)
        n = np.arange(N)
        k = n.reshape((N, 1))
        W = np.exp(-2j * np.pi * k * n / N)
        return W @ x
    
    @staticmethod
    def design_lowpass(cutoff, fs, numtaps=101):
        """
        Design FIR lowpass filter using window method.
        """
        nyquist = fs / 2
        normalized_cutoff = cutoff / nyquist
        h = sig.firwin(numtaps, normalized_cutoff)
        return h
    
    @staticmethod
    def apply_filter(x, h):
        """
        Apply FIR filter using convolution.
        """
        return np.convolve(x, h, mode='same')
    
    @staticmethod
    def check_sampling_theorem(B, fs):
        """
        Check if sampling rate satisfies Nyquist criterion.
        """
        nyquist_rate = 2 * B
        if fs >= nyquist_rate:
            return True, f"fs = {fs} Hz ≥ {nyquist_rate} Hz = 2B ✓"
        else:
            return False, f"fs = {fs} Hz < {nyquist_rate} Hz = 2B (aliasing!)"

Operations Research: Linear Programming and the Simplex Method

Tags: operations-research linear-programming optimization

Real-World Motivation

Operations research applies mathematical methods to optimize complex systems. From airline scheduling and supply chain management to portfolio optimization and telecommunications network design, linear programming solves problems with millions of variables and constraints. The simplex method, developed by George Dantzig in 1947, remains one of the most important algorithms of the 20th century, enabling efficient resource allocation across virtually every industry.

Definitions

A linear program in standard form is: \[\begin{aligned} \text{minimize} \quad & c^T x \\ \text{subject to} \quad & Ax = b \\ & x \geq 0 \end{aligned}\]

A feasible solution satisfies all constraints. The feasible region is the set of all feasible solutions.

A basic feasible solution (BFS) is a feasible solution with at most \(m\) non-zero components (where \(A\) is \(m \times n\)), corresponding to a vertex of the feasible polytope.

Theorem and Proof

Theorem (Fundamental Theorem of Linear Programming): For a linear program in standard form:

  1. If a feasible solution exists, a basic feasible solution exists.
  2. If an optimal solution exists, an optimal basic feasible solution exists.

Proof of (1): Let \(x\) be feasible with support \(S = \{j : x_j > 0\}\). If columns \(\{A_j : j \in S\}\) are linearly independent, \(x\) is basic. Otherwise, there exists \(d \neq 0\) with \(\sum_{j \in S} d_j A_j = 0\).

Consider \(x(\epsilon) = x + \epsilon d\). For small \(\epsilon\), \(x(\epsilon)\) remains feasible. Choose \(\epsilon\) so that some \(x_j(\epsilon) = 0\), reducing \(|S|\). Repeat until columns are independent.

Proof of (2): Let \(x^*\) be optimal. If not basic, perturb as above. Show the perturbation direction has \(c^T d = 0\) (otherwise optimality is contradicted). Thus we can move to a basic solution with the same objective value. ∎

Notation Guide

  • \(c \in \mathbb{R}^n\) — Cost vector
  • \(A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}\) — Constraint matrix
  • \(b \in \mathbb{R}^m\) — Right-hand side
  • \(x \in \mathbb{R}^n\) — Decision variables
  • \(B\) — Basis (set of basic variable indices)
  • \(N\) — Non-basic variables
  • \(z = c^T x\) — Objective value

import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import linprog

class LinearProgramming:
    """Linear programming and the simplex method."""
    
    @staticmethod
    def solve_lp_scipy(c, A_ub=None, b_ub=None, A_eq=None, b_eq=None, bounds=None):
        """
        Solve LP using scipy.optimize.linprog.
        """
        result = linprog(c, A_ub=A_ub, b_ub=b_ub, A_eq=A_eq, b_eq=b_eq, 
                         bounds=bounds, method='highs')
        return result
    
    @staticmethod
    def simplex_step(c, A, b, basis):
        """
        Perform one iteration of the simplex method.
        
        basis: list of indices of basic variables
        Returns: new basis, or None if optimal
        """
        m, n = A.shape
        B = A[:, basis]
        c_B = c[basis]
        
        # Compute simplex multipliers (dual variables)
        y = np.linalg.solve(B.T, c_B)
        
        # Compute reduced costs
        reduced_costs = c - A.T @ y
        
        # Check optimality
        if np.all(reduced_costs >= -1e-10):
            return None, "optimal"
        
        # Choose entering variable (most negative reduced cost)
        entering = np.argmin(reduced_costs)
        
        # Compute direction
        d = np.linalg.solve(B, A[:, entering])
        
        # Check unboundedness
        if np.all(d <= 1e-10):
            return None, "unbounded"
        
        # Choose leaving variable (minimum ratio test)
        x_B = np.linalg.solve(B, b)
        ratios = np.array([x_B[i] / d[i] if d[i] > 1e-10 else np.inf 
                          for i in range(m)])
        leaving_idx = np.argmin(ratios)
        leaving = basis[leaving_idx]
        
        # Update basis
        new_basis = basis.copy()
        new_basis[leaving_idx] = entering
        
        return new_basis, f"pivot: {leaving} out, {entering} in"
    
    @staticmethod
    def simplex_method(c, A, b, initial_basis):
        """
        Run simplex method from initial basis.
        """
        basis = initial_basis.copy()
        iterations = 0
        max_iter = 100
        
        while iterations < max_iter:
            new_basis, status = LinearProgramming.simplex_step(c, A, b, basis)
            iterations += 1
            
            if new_basis is None:
                # Compute optimal solution
                B = A[:, basis]
                x_B = np.linalg.solve(B, b)
                x = np.zeros(len(c))
                x[basis] = x_B
                obj = c @ x
                return {
                    'status': status,
                    'x': x,
                    'objective': obj,
                    'basis': basis,
                    'iterations': iterations
                }
            
            basis = new_basis
        
        return {'status': 'max_iterations_reached'}
    
    @staticmethod
    def transportation_problem(supply, demand, costs):
        """
        Solve transportation problem.
        Minimize sum_{i,j} c_{ij} x_{ij}
        subject to: sum_j x_{ij} = supply_i
                    sum_i x_{ij} = demand_j
                    x_{ij} >= 0
        """
        m = len(supply)
        n = len(demand)
        
        # Flatten decision variables
        c = costs.flatten()
        
        # Supply constraints
        A_eq_supply = np.zeros((m, m * n))
        for i in range(m):
            A_eq_supply[i, i*n:(i+1)*n] = 1
        
        # Demand constraints
        A_eq_demand = np.zeros((n, m * n))
        for j in range(n):
            for i in range(m):
                A_eq_demand[j, i*n + j] = 1
        
        A_eq = np.vstack([A_eq_supply, A_eq_demand])
        b_eq = np.concatenate([supply, demand])
        
        return c, A_eq, b_eq

Mathematical Logic: Gödel's Incompleteness Theorems

Tags: mathematical-logic foundations computability

Historical Context

In 1931, Kurt Gödel published his incompleteness theorems, shattering the Hilbert program's goal of finding a complete and consistent set of axioms for all mathematics. Building on work by Whitehead, Russell, and Hilbert himself, Gödel showed that any sufficiently powerful formal system contains true statements that cannot be proved within the system. This result fundamentally changed our understanding of mathematical truth and paved the way for computer science through the study of computability.

Definitions

A formal system consists of a language, axioms, and inference rules for deriving theorems.

A system is consistent if it cannot prove both a statement and its negation.

A system is complete if for every statement \(\phi\), either \(\phi\) or \(\neg\phi\) is provable.

A system is recursively axiomatizable if there is an algorithm to decide whether a formula is an axiom.

Theorem: Gödel's First Incompleteness Theorem

Theorem: Any consistent formal system \(F\) that is strong enough to encode basic arithmetic is incomplete: there exists a statement \(G_F\) (the Gödel sentence) such that neither \(G_F\) nor \(\neg G_F\) is provable in \(F\).

Proof Sketch:

Step 1 (Gödel numbering): Assign a unique natural number to each formula and proof in the system. This allows the system to talk about its own syntax.

Step 2 (Diagonal lemma): For any formula \(\psi(x)\) with one free variable, there exists a sentence \(\phi\) such that: \[F \vdash \phi \leftrightarrow \psi(\ulcorner\phi\urcorner)\] where \(\ulcorner\phi\urcorner\) is the Gödel number of \(\phi\).

Step 3 (Provability predicate): Define \(\text{Prov}_F(x)\) meaning "the formula with Gödel number \(x\) is provable in \(F\)."

Step 4 (Gödel sentence): By the diagonal lemma, there exists \(G_F\) such that: \[F \vdash G_F \leftrightarrow \neg\text{Prov}_F(\ulcorner G_F\urcorner)\] This says "I am not provable."

Step 5: If \(F \vdash G_F\), then \(F\) proves \(\text{Prov}_F(\ulcorner G_F\urcorner)\), hence \(F \vdash \neg G_F\), contradicting consistency. If \(F \vdash \neg G_F\), then \(F\) proves it can prove \(G_F\), but this requires stronger assumptions (\(\omega\)-consistency). ∎

Notation Guide

  • \(\ulcorner\phi\urcorner\) — Gödel number of formula \(\phi\)
  • \(\text{Prov}_F(x)\) — Provability predicate
  • \(F \vdash \phi\) — \(\phi\) is provable in \(F\)
  • \(G_F\) — Gödel sentence for system \(F\)
  • \(\leftrightarrow\) — Logical equivalence
  • \(\neg\) — Negation

class GodelEncoding:
    """Demonstrate Gödel numbering concepts."""
    
    @staticmethod
    def encode_symbol(symbol):
        """
        Assign Gödel numbers to basic symbols.
        """
        encoding = {
            '0': 1, 'S': 2,  # Zero and successor
            '+': 3, '*': 4,  # Operations
            '=': 5, '<': 6,  # Relations
            '(': 7, ')': 8,  # Parentheses
            '¬': 9, '→': 10, # Logical connectives
            '∀': 11, '∃': 12, # Quantifiers
            'x': 13, 'y': 14, 'z': 15, # Variables (prime > 12)
        }
        return encoding.get(symbol, 0)

    def godel_number(formula_string):
        """
        Compute Gödel number of a formula.

        from sympy import prime

        result = 1
        for i, char in enumerate(symbols, 1):
            code = GodelEncoding.encode_symbol(char)
            if code > 0:

        return result

Combinatorics: The Probabilistic Method

Tags: combinatorics probabilistic-method graph-theory

Historical Context

The probabilistic method, pioneered by Paul Erdős in the 1940s-1950s, revolutionized combinatorics by proving the existence of mathematical objects through probability theory without explicitly constructing them. This non-constructive approach solved long-standing problems in graph theory, number theory, and combinatorial geometry. Erdős's insight was that showing an object has positive probability of existing proves its existence, even if we cannot exhibit it.

Definitions

The probabilistic method proves existence of an object with property \(P\) by showing that a random object has property \(P\) with positive probability.

A random graph \(G(n, p)\) has \(n\) vertices and each edge appears independently with probability \(p\).

The Ramsey number \(R(s, t)\) is the smallest \(n\) such that every 2-coloring of edges of \(K_n\) contains a monochromatic \(K_s\) (red) or \(K_t\) (blue).

Theorem: Lower Bound on Ramsey Numbers

Theorem (Erdős, 1947): For \(k \geq 3\): \[R(k, k) > 2^{k/2 - 1}\]

Proof: Consider a random 2-coloring of edges of \(K_n\) where each edge is red or blue with probability \(1/2\).

Step 1: For a fixed set of \(k\) vertices, the probability it forms a monochromatic \(K_k\) is: \[2 \cdot 2^{-\binom{k}{2}} = 2^{1 - \binom{k}{2}}\] (factor of 2 for red or blue).

Step 2: There are \(\binom{n}{k}\) such sets. By union bound: \[P(\text{some monochromatic } K_k) \leq \binom{n}{k} 2^{1 - \binom{k}{2}}\]

Step 3: Using \(\binom{n}{k} < n^k/k!\) and Stirling's approximation, this probability is less than 1 when \(n \leq 2^{k/2 - 1}\).

Step 4: If the probability of having a monochromatic \(K_k\) is less than 1, there exists a coloring with no monochromatic \(K_k\). Thus \(R(k, k) > n\). ∎

Notation Guide

  • \(G(n, p)\) — Erdős-Rényi random graph
  • \(R(s, t)\) — Ramsey number
  • \(K_n\) — Complete graph on \(n\) vertices
  • \(\binom{n}{k}\) — Binomial coefficient
  • \(\mathbb{E}[X]\) — Expected value
  • \(P(A)\) — Probability of event \(A\)

Analytic Number Theory: The Distribution of Primes

Tags: analytic-number-theory prime-numbers riemann-zeta

Historical Context

Analytic number theory applies methods from complex analysis to study integers, particularly primes. The field began with Euler's product formula connecting primes to the zeta function, but flourished after Riemann's 1859 memoir on the distribution of primes. The Prime Number Theorem, proved independently by Hadamard and de la Vallée Poussin in 1896, established that \(\pi(x) \sim x/\log x\). The famous Riemann Hypothesis, still open, would provide the ultimate refinement of this result.

Definitions

The prime counting function is \(\pi(x) = \#\{p \leq x : p \text{ prime}\}\).

The Riemann zeta function for \(\text{Re}(s) > 1\) is: \[\zeta(s) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n^s} = \prod_p \frac{1}{1 - p^{-s}}\]

The von Mangoldt function is: \[\Lambda(n) = \begin{cases} \log p & \text{if } n = p^k \text{ for prime } p \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}\]

The Chebyshev functions are: \[\psi(x) = \sum_{n \leq x} \Lambda(n), \quad \vartheta(x) = \sum_{p \leq x} \log p\]

Theorem: The Prime Number Theorem

Theorem (Hadamard, de la Vallée Poussin, 1896): \[\pi(x) \sim \frac{x}{\log x}\] Equivalently, \(\psi(x) \sim x\) or \(\vartheta(x) \sim x\).

Proof Sketch:

Step 1: Relate \(\psi(x)\) to \(\zeta(s)\) via: \[-\frac{\zeta'(s)}{\zeta(s)} = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{\Lambda(n)}{n^s}\]

Step 2: Apply Perron's formula to express \(\psi(x)\) as a contour integral: \[\psi(x) = \frac{1}{2\pi i} \int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty} \left(-\frac{\zeta'(s)}{\zeta(s)}\right) \frac{x^s}{s} ds\]

Step 3: Move the contour to the left. The main contribution comes from the pole at \(s = 1\) (residue \(x\)).

Step 4: The remaining integral depends on zeros of \(\zeta(s)\). Hadamard and de la Vallée Poussin proved \(\zeta(1 + it) \neq 0\) for all \(t\), which is sufficient to show the error term is \(o(x)\).

Step 5: Partial summation converts from \(\psi(x)\) to \(\pi(x)\). ∎

Notation Guide

  • \(\pi(x)\) — Prime counting function
  • \(\zeta(s)\) — Riemann zeta function
  • \(\Lambda(n)\) — von Mangoldt function
  • \(\psi(x), \vartheta(x)\) — Chebyshev functions
  • \(s = \sigma + it\) — Complex variable
  • \(\text{li}(x) = \int_2^x \frac{dt}{\log t}\) — Logarithmic integral
  • \(\rho\) — Non-trivial zero of \(\zeta(s)\)

        return expi(np.log(x)) - expi(np.log(2))

        """
        total = 0
        for p in primerange(2, int(x) + 1):
            total += float(log(p))
        return total

    def chebyshev_psi(x, max_power=10):
        """

        for p in primerange(2, int(x) + 1):
            # Add contributions from p, p^2, p^3, ...
            pk = p
            while pk <= x:
                total += float(log(p))
                pk *= p

Emmy Noether: Symmetry and Conservation Laws

Tags: noether mathematical-physics abstract-algebra

Historical Context

Emmy Noether (1882-1935) was one of the most influential mathematicians of the 20th century, despite facing severe discrimination as a Jewish woman in Germany. Her 1918 theorem establishing the connection between symmetries and conservation laws revolutionized theoretical physics. In mathematics, she transformed algebra by emphasizing the structural properties of rings and ideals over computational approaches. Einstein called her "the most significant creative mathematical genius thus far produced since the higher education of women began."

Noether's Theorem

Theorem (Noether's First Theorem): If a physical system has a continuous symmetry, then there exists a corresponding conserved quantity.

Specifically, if the action \(S = \int L(q, \dot{q}, t) dt\) is invariant under a continuous transformation \(q(t) \mapsto q(t) + \epsilon \psi(t)\), then: \[\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{q}} \psi\right) = 0\]

giving the conserved quantity \(I = p \psi\) where \(p = \partial L / \partial \dot{q}\).

Noetherian Rings

A ring \(R\) is Noetherian if every ascending chain of ideals stabilizes: \[I_1 \subseteq I_2 \subseteq I_3 \subseteq \cdots \implies \exists N: I_N = I_{N+1} = \cdots\]

Hilbert's Basis Theorem: If \(R\) is Noetherian, then \(R[x]\) is Noetherian.

The Development of Modern Algebra: From Equations to Structures

The transformation of algebra from a discipline focused on solving equations to one studying abstract structures represents one of the most profound shifts in mathematical thinking. This evolution, spanning roughly 1830 to 1930, fundamentally changed how mathematicians understand their subject.

The story begins with the work of Évariste Galois in the 1830s. Galois, who died in a duel at age 20, developed a theory that connected polynomial equations to group theory. His insight was that the solvability of an equation by radicals depends on the structure of its associated permutation group. Though his contemporaries found his work nearly incomprehensible, Liouville recognized its significance and published Galois's papers in 1846.

Meanwhile, British mathematicians were developing their own algebraic tradition. George Boole's "An Investigation of the Laws of Thought" (1854) created algebraic logic, showing that logical reasoning could be reduced to algebraic manipulation. This work would eventually form the foundation of computer science.

The late 19th century saw the emergence of abstract group theory. Felix Klein's Erlangen Program (1872) proposed that geometries could be classified by their transformation groups. Sophus Lie developed the theory of continuous transformation groups, now called Lie groups, which became essential tools in physics.

The turn of the century brought the axiomatization movement. David Hilbert's "Grundlagen der Geometrie" (1899) showed that geometry could be built on purely formal axioms, independent of intuition. This approach was extended to algebra by Ernst Steinitz, whose 1910 paper "Algebraische Theorie der Körper" (Algebraic Theory of Fields) established the abstract theory of fields.

Emmy Noether's work in the 1920s completed this transformation. Her approach emphasized the structural properties of rings and ideals over computational techniques. Her famous declaration "Es steht alles schon bei Dedekind" (It's all already in Dedekind) acknowledged her debt to Richard Dedekind's earlier work, while her methods went far beyond it. Noether's abstract approach initially faced resistance—some called her work "abstract nonsense"—but it eventually became the dominant paradigm.

The culmination of this development was the publication of Bartel van der Waerden's "Moderne Algebra" in 1930. Based on Noether's lectures and Emil Artin's teaching, this textbook presented algebra as the study of structures: groups, rings, fields, and modules. It became the definitive treatment of the subject, shaping algebra education for generations.

This structural view of algebra had profound consequences. It enabled the development of algebraic topology, algebraic geometry, and homological algebra. It provided the language for quantum mechanics and particle physics. And it established a model of mathematical rigor that influenced all subsequent mathematics.

IMO Shortlist Problem

Difficulty: ★★★★☆

Problem: Let \(n\) be a positive integer. A sequence of integers \(a_0, a_1, \ldots, a_n\) is called good if it satisfies:

  • \(a_0 = 0\) and \(a_n = n\)
  • For each \(i = 1, 2, \ldots, n\), we have \(a_i - a_{i-1} \in \{1, 2\}\)

Determine the number of good sequences.

Hint

Let \(k\) be the number of times the difference equals 2. How many 1s must there be? Use a combinatorial argument involving choosing positions.

Solution

Solution: Suppose there are \(k\) instances where \(a_i - a_{i-1} = 2\). Then there are \(n-k\) instances where the difference is 1.

The total increase from \(a_0 = 0\) to \(a_n = n\) is: \[2k + 1 \cdot (n-k) = n + k\]

But this must equal \(n\), so \(k = 0\). Wait—this gives only the all-1s sequence. Let me reconsider...

Actually, there are exactly \(n\) differences (from \(i=1\) to \(n\)), not \(n\) positions. If \(k\) differences equal 2 and \(n-k\) equal 1: \[2k + (n-k) = n + k = n\]

This implies \(k = 0\). So there's exactly one good sequence: the sequence with all differences equal to 1, giving \(a_i = i\) for all \(i\).

Answer: There is exactly \(\boxed{1}\) good sequence.

The Mandelbrot Set: A Window into Complex Dynamics

The Mandelbrot set is defined as the set of complex numbers \(c\) for which the iteration \(z_{n+1} = z_n^2 + c\) (starting with \(z_0 = 0\)) remains bounded. This simple recursive formula generates one of the most intricate and beautiful objects in mathematics.

The set exhibits self-similarity at all scales—zooming in reveals copies of the whole set, twisted and distorted. It is connected (proved by Douady and Hubbard in the 1980s) but its boundary has Hausdorff dimension 2, making it a fractal of extraordinary complexity.

Each point in the complex plane corresponds to a different dynamical system. The main cardioid and bulbs correspond to parameters where the iteration has attracting periodic orbits of different periods. The filaments connecting these regions contain parameters with more complex behavior.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def mandelbrot(c, max_iter=100, escape_radius=2.0):
    """
    Compute the number of iterations until escape for point c.
    Returns the iteration count (normalized for smooth coloring).
    """
    z = 0
    for n in range(max_iter):
        if abs(z) > escape_radius:
            # Smooth coloring: interpolate between iterations
            log_zn = np.log(abs(z))
            nu = np.log(log_zn / np.log(escape_radius)) / np.log(2)
            return n + 1 - nu
        z = z*z + c
    return max_iter  # Did not escape (in set)

def compute_mandelbrot_set(xmin=-2.5, xmax=1.0, ymin=-1.25, ymax=1.25, 
                           width=800, height=600, max_iter=100):
    """
    Compute the Mandelbrot set on a grid.
    """
    x = np.linspace(xmin, xmax, width)
    y = np.linspace(ymin, ymax, height)
    X, Y = np.meshgrid(x, y)
    C = X + 1j*Y
    
    # Vectorized computation
    mandelbrot_set = np.vectorize(lambda c: mandelbrot(c, max_iter))(C)
    
    return mandelbrot_set

def visualize_mandelbrot(mandelbrot_set, filename='mandelbrot.png'):
    """
    Create a visualization of the Mandelbrot set.
    """
    plt.figure(figsize=(12, 9))
    
    # Use a colormap for the exterior; black for the interior
    plt.imshow(mandelbrot_set, extent=[-2.5, 1.0, -1.25, 1.25], 
               cmap='hot', origin='lower', interpolation='bilinear')
    
    plt.colorbar(label='Iterations to escape')
    plt.title('The Mandelbrot Set', fontsize=16)
    plt.xlabel('Re(c)')
    plt.ylabel('Im(c)')
    
    plt.tight_layout()
    plt.savefig(filename, dpi=150, bbox_inches='tight')
    plt.close()
    
    print(f"Mandelbrot set saved to {filename}")

def zoom_mandelbrot(x_center, y_center, zoom_width, filename='mandelbrot_zoom.png',
                    max_iter=500):
    """
    Zoom into a region of the Mandelbrot set.
    
    Interesting locations:
    - Seahorse valley: (-0.75, 0.1), width=0.1
    - Elephant valley: (0.3, 0), width=0.1  
    - Mini-Mandelbrot: (-1.75, 0), width=0.1
    """
    xmin = x_center - zoom_width/2
    xmax = x_center + zoom_width/2
    ymin = y_center - zoom_width/2
    ymax = y_center + zoom_width/2
    
    m_set = compute_mandelbrot_set(xmin, xmax, ymin, ymax, 
                                    width=800, height=800, 
                                    max_iter=max_iter)
    
    plt.figure(figsize=(10, 10))
    plt.imshow(m_set, extent=[xmin, xmax, ymin, ymax],
               cmap='hot', origin='lower', interpolation='bilinear')
    plt.title(f'Mandelbrot Zoom: ({x_center}, {y_center})', fontsize=14)
    plt.xlabel('Re(c)')
    plt.ylabel('Im(c)')
    
    plt.tight_layout()
    plt.savefig(filename, dpi=150, bbox_inches='tight')
    plt.close()
    
    print(f"Zoom saved to {filename}")
