Algebraic Geometry: The Geometry of Polynomial Equations

algebraic-geometry

Motivation

Algebraic geometry studies the solutions of polynomial equations, uniting algebra and geometry in a profound synthesis. From the ancient Greeks' study of conic sections to Grothendieck's revolutionary theory of schemes, this field has evolved into one of the most sophisticated and powerful areas of mathematics. Modern algebraic geometry provides the language for understanding everything from number theory and physics to robotics and cryptography. The field's power lies in its ability to translate geometric intuition into algebraic computation and vice versa.

Definitions

  • Affine Variety: The zero set of a collection of polynomials in affine space. For polynomials \(f_1, \ldots, f_m \in k[x_1, \ldots, x_n]\), the variety is \(V(f_1, \ldots, f_m) = \{x \in \mathbb{A}^n : f_i(x) = 0 \text{ for all } i\}\).
  • Coordinate Ring: For an affine variety \(V\), the ring \(k[V] = k[x_1, \ldots, x_n]/I(V)\) where \(I(V)\) is the ideal of polynomials vanishing on \(V\).
  • Projective Space: \(\mathbb{P}^n = (\mathbb{A}^{n+1} \setminus \{0\})/\sim\) where \(x \sim \lambda x\) for \(\lambda \in k^*\). Points are denoted by homogeneous coordinates \([x_0 : \cdots : x_n]\).
  • Scheme: A locally ringed space \((X, \mathcal{O}_X)\) that is locally isomorphic to the spectrum of a ring. Schemes generalize varieties by allowing nilpotents and working over arbitrary rings.
  • Sheaf: A tool for tracking locally defined data on a topological space. A presheaf \(\mathcal{F}\) assigns abelian groups to open sets with restriction maps satisfying locality and gluing axioms.

Theorem: Hilbert's Nullstellensatz

Theorem: Let \(k\) be an algebraically closed field and \(I\) an ideal in \(k[x_1, \ldots, x_n]\). Then:

\[I(V(I)) = \sqrt{I}\]

where \(\sqrt{I} = \{f : f^m \in I \text{ for some } m > 0\}\) is the radical of \(I\).

Proof

The proof proceeds in several steps:

Step 1 (Weak Nullstellensatz): If \(I \neq k[x_1, \ldots, x_n]\), then \(V(I) \neq \emptyset\). This follows from Noether normalization and the fact that maximal ideals in \(k[x_1, \ldots, x_n]\) correspond to points.

Step 2 (Rabinowitsch Trick): For \(f \in I(V(I))\), introduce a new variable \(y\) and consider \(J = (I, 1 - yf) \subset k[x_1, \ldots, x_n, y]\). Then \(V(J) = \emptyset\), so by the weak Nullstellensatz, \(1 \in J\).

Step 3: Writing \(1 = \sum g_i h_i + g_0(1 - yf)\) and substituting \(y = 1/f\) gives \(f^m \in I\) for some \(m\). ∎

Notation Guide

  • \(\mathbb{A}^n\) — affine n-space over a field
  • \(\mathbb{P}^n\) — projective n-space
  • \(V(I)\) — variety of an ideal
  • \(I(X)\) — ideal of a variety
  • \(k[X]\) — coordinate ring
  • \(\mathcal{O}_{X,x}\) — local ring at a point
  • \(\text{Spec}(R)\) — spectrum of a ring
  • \(\dim X\) — dimension of a variety

Homological Algebra: The Mathematics of Holes

homological-algebra

Motivation

Homological algebra provides the algebraic machinery for measuring "holes" in mathematical structures. Born from algebraic topology's study of holes in spaces, it has become a fundamental tool across mathematics—from algebraic geometry and number theory to representation theory and mathematical physics. The theory of derived functors, spectral sequences, and cohomology theories unifies seemingly disparate areas of mathematics. Understanding homological algebra is essential for modern research in geometry, algebra, and their applications.

Definitions

  • Chain Complex: A sequence of abelian groups \(\cdots \xrightarrow{d_{n+1}} C_n \xrightarrow{d_n} C_{n-1} \xrightarrow{d_{n-1}} \cdots\) with \(d_n \circ d_{n+1} = 0\).
  • Cycles: \(Z_n = \ker(d_n) = \{c \in C_n : d_n(c) = 0\}\).
  • Boundaries: \(B_n = \text{im}(d_{n+1})\).
  • Homology: \(H_n(C_\bullet) = Z_n / B_n\), measuring the failure of cycles to be boundaries.
  • Exact Sequence: A chain complex where \(H_n = 0\) for all \(n\).
  • Derived Functor: Functors like \(\text{Ext}^n\) and \(\text{Tor}_n\) that measure the failure of other functors to be exact.

Theorem: The Long Exact Sequence in Homology

Theorem: Given a short exact sequence of chain complexes \(0 \to A_\bullet \xrightarrow{f} B_\bullet \xrightarrow{g} C_\bullet \to 0\), there exists a natural long exact sequence in homology:

\[\cdots \to H_n(A) \xrightarrow{f_*} H_n(B) \xrightarrow{g_*} H_n(C) \xrightarrow{\partial} H_{n-1}(A) \to \cdots\]

where \(\partial\) is the connecting homomorphism.

Proof

The proof uses the Snake Lemma applied to the commutative diagram relating kernels and images. The connecting homomorphism \(\partial\) is constructed by lifting: for \([c] \in H_n(C)\), lift \(c\) to \(b \in B_n\), then \(d(b)\) maps to \(0\) in \(C_{n-1}\), so \(d(b) = f(a)\) for some \(a \in A_{n-1}\). Define \(\partial([c]) = [a]\). One verifies this is well-defined and exact. ∎

Notation Guide

  • \(C_\bullet\) — chain complex
  • \(d_n\) — boundary map
  • \(Z_n = \ker(d_n)\) — n-cycles
  • \(B_n = \text{im}(d_{n+1})\) — n-boundaries
  • \(H_n = Z_n/B_n\) — n-th homology
  • \(H^n\) — cohomology
  • \(\text{Ext}^n_R(M, N)\) — derived functor of Hom
  • \(\text{Tor}_n^R(M, N)\) — derived functor of tensor

import numpy as np

class HomologicalAlgebra:
    """Computational homological algebra tools."""
    
    def simplicial_chain_complex(self, simplices):
        """Build chain complex from simplicial complex."""
        max_dim = max(len(s) - 1 for s in simplices)
        chains = {d: [] for d in range(max_dim + 1)}
        for simplex in simplices:
            d = len(simplex) - 1
            chains[d].append(tuple(sorted(simplex)))
        for d in chains:
            chains[d] = sorted(chains[d])
        return chains
    
    def boundary_matrix(self, chains_d, chains_d_minus_1, d):
        """Compute boundary matrix from d-chains to (d-1)-chains."""
        if d == 0:
            return np.zeros((1, len(chains_d[0])))
        n_d = len(chains_d[d])
        n_d_minus_1 = len(chains_d[d-1])
        index_map = {s: i for i, s in enumerate(chains_d[d-1])}
        B = np.zeros((n_d_minus_1, n_d), dtype=int)
        for j, simplex in enumerate(chains_d[d]):
            for i in range(len(simplex)):
                face = tuple(simplex[k] for k in range(len(simplex)) if k != i)
                if face in index_map:
                    row = index_map[face]
                    B[row, j] = (-1)**i
        return B
    
    def homology(self, chains):
        """Compute homology groups, returns Betti numbers."""
        betti = []
        max_dim = max(chains.keys())
        for d in range(max_dim + 1):
            B_d = self.boundary_matrix(chains, chains, d)
            B_d_plus_1 = self.boundary_matrix(chains, chains, d + 1) if d < max_dim else np.zeros((len(chains[d]), 0))
            rank_B_d = np.linalg.matrix_rank(B_d)
            rank_B_d_plus_1 = np.linalg.matrix_rank(B_d_plus_1)
            if d == 0:
                dim_cycles = len(chains[0])
            else:
                dim_cycles = len(chains[d]) - rank_B_d
            dim_boundaries = rank_B_d_plus_1
            betti.append(max(0, dim_cycles - dim_boundaries))
        return betti

Analytic Number Theory: Primes Through Analysis

analytic-number-theory

Motivation

Analytic number theory uses the powerful tools of complex analysis and Fourier analysis to study the distribution of prime numbers and other arithmetic phenomena. The field began with Euler's discovery of the product formula connecting primes to the zeta function, and reached maturity with Riemann's 1859 paper introducing his famous hypothesis. Dirichlet's theorem on primes in arithmetic progressions, the Prime Number Theorem, and the study of L-functions are cornerstones of this beautiful subject.

Definitions

  • Riemann Zeta Function: \(\zeta(s) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n^s} = \prod_p \frac{1}{1-p^{-s}}\) for \(\text{Re}(s) > 1\), extended by analytic continuation.
  • Dirichlet Character: A completely multiplicative function \(\chi: \mathbb{Z} \to \mathbb{C}\) that is periodic with period \(q\).
  • Dirichlet L-function: \(L(s, \chi) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{\chi(n)}{n^s}\).
  • Von Mangoldt Function: \(\Lambda(n) = \log p\) if \(n = p^k\) for prime \(p\), and \(0\) otherwise.
  • Chebyshev Functions: \(\psi(x) = \sum_{n \leq x} \Lambda(n)\) and \(\vartheta(x) = \sum_{p \leq x} \log p\).

Theorem: The Prime Number Theorem

Theorem: Let \(\pi(x)\) denote the number of primes less than or equal to \(x\). Then \(\pi(x) \sim \frac{x}{\log x}\). Equivalently, \(\psi(x) \sim x\).

Proof Sketch

The proof uses complex analysis and the explicit formula connecting primes to zeros of \(\zeta(s)\). Step 1: Show that \(\zeta(s)\) has no zeros on the line \(\text{Re}(s) = 1\). Step 2: Establish the explicit formula \(\psi(x) = x - \sum_{\rho} \frac{x^{\rho}}{\rho} - \frac{\zeta'(0)}{\zeta(0)} - \frac{1}{2}\log(1-x^{-2})\). Step 3: Use the zero-free region to show the sum over zeros is \(o(x)\), giving \(\psi(x) \sim x\). ∎

Notation Guide

  • \(\zeta(s)\) — Riemann zeta function
  • \(L(s, \chi)\) — Dirichlet L-function
  • \(\pi(x)\) — prime counting function
  • \(\psi(x), \vartheta(x)\) — Chebyshev functions
  • \(\Lambda(n)\) — von Mangoldt function
  • \(\mu(n)\) — Möbius function
  • \(\varphi(n)\) — Euler's totient function
  • \(\text{li}(x)\) — logarithmic integral

Python Code

import numpy as np
from scipy.special import expi
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

class AnalyticNumberTheory:
    """Tools for analytic number theory computations."""
    
    def __init__(self, max_n=10**6):
        self.max_n = max_n
        self._sieve_primes()
    
    def _sieve_primes(self):
        """Generate primes up to max_n using sieve of Eratosthenes."""
        sieve = np.ones(self.max_n + 1, dtype=bool)
        sieve[0:2] = False
        for i in range(2, int(self.max_n**0.5) + 1):
            if sieve[i]:
                sieve[i*i::i] = False
        self.primes = np.nonzero(sieve)[0]
        self.sieve = sieve
    
    def pi(self, x):
        """Prime counting function."""
        if x > self.max_n:
            raise ValueError(f"x must be <= {self.max_n}")
        return np.sum(self.sieve[2:int(x)+1])
    
    def logarithmic_integral(self, x):
        """Compute li(x) = ∫_2^x dt/log(t)."""
        if x <= 2:
            return 0
        return expi(np.log(x))
    
    def plot_prime_distribution(self, max_x=100000):
        """Plot π(x) vs approximations."""
        x_vals = np.logspace(1, np.log10(max_x), 100)
        pi_vals = [self.pi(x) for x in x_vals]
        li_vals = [self.logarithmic_integral(x) for x in x_vals]
        x_log_x = [x / np.log(x) for x in x_vals]
        
        plt.figure(figsize=(12, 6))
        plt.subplot(1, 2, 1)
        plt.plot(x_vals, pi_vals, 'b-', label='$\\pi(x)$', linewidth=2)
        plt.plot(x_vals, li_vals, 'r--', label='$\\text{li}(x)$', linewidth=2)
        plt.plot(x_vals, x_log_x, 'g:', label='$x/\\log x$', linewidth=2)
        plt.xlabel('x')
        plt.ylabel('Count')
        plt.legend()
        plt.title('Prime Counting Function')
        plt.xscale('log')
        plt.yscale('log')
        plt.grid(True, alpha=0.3)
        
        plt.subplot(1, 2, 2)
        errors = [(li - pi) / pi * 100 for pi, li in zip(pi_vals, li_vals)]
        plt.plot(x_vals, errors, 'r-', label='li(x) error %')
        plt.xlabel('x')
        plt.ylabel('Relative Error (%)')
        plt.legend()
        plt.title('Approximation Error')
        plt.xscale('log')
        plt.grid(True, alpha=0.3)
        plt.tight_layout()
        return plt

Mathematical Finance: The Black-Scholes Revolution

mathematical-finance

Real-World Motivation

The 1973 publication of the Black-Scholes option pricing formula revolutionized financial markets and earned its creators the 1997 Nobel Prize in Economics. Before this breakthrough, option pricing was more art than science, with traders relying on intuition and rules of thumb. The Black-Scholes model provided a rigorous framework for pricing derivatives, enabling the explosive growth of options markets and modern risk management.

Definitions

  • Geometric Brownian Motion: \(dS_t = \mu S_t dt + \sigma S_t dW_t\), where \(\mu\) is drift, \(\sigma\) is volatility.
  • European Call Option: Payoff: \(\max(S_T - K, 0)\).
  • Risk-Neutral Measure: A probability measure \(\mathbb{Q}\) under which discounted asset prices are martingales.
  • Arbitrage: A trading strategy with zero initial cost, no risk of loss, and positive probability of profit.

Theorem: The Black-Scholes Formula

Theorem: The price of a European call option is:

\[C(S, t) = S N(d_1) - K e^{-r(T-t)} N(d_2)\]

where \(d_1 = \frac{\ln(S/K) + (r + \sigma^2/2)(T-t)}{\sigma\sqrt{T-t}}\) and \(d_2 = d_1 - \sigma\sqrt{T-t}\).

Proof Sketch

By risk-neutral valuation, \(C(S, t) = e^{-r(T-t)} \mathbb{E}^*[\max(S_T - K, 0)]\). Under \(\mathbb{Q}\), \(\ln(S_T)\) is normal with known mean and variance. The expectation splits into two integrals that evaluate to \(S N(d_1)\) and \(K e^{-r(T-t)} N(d_2)\). ∎

Notation Guide

  • \(S_t\) — asset price at time \(t\)
  • \(K\) — strike price
  • \(T\) — maturity time
  • \(r\) — risk-free interest rate
  • \(\sigma\) — volatility
  • \(N(x)\) — standard normal CDF
  • \(\Delta = N(d_1)\) — Delta hedge ratio

Python Code

import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm
from scipy.optimize import minimize_scalar
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

class BlackScholes:
    """Black-Scholes option pricing model."""
    
    def __init__(self, S, K, T, r, sigma):
        self.S = S
        self.K = K
        self.T = T
        self.r = r
        self.sigma = sigma
        self._compute_d1_d2()
    
    def _compute_d1_d2(self):
        self.d1 = (np.log(self.S / self.K) + 
                   (self.r + 0.5 * self.sigma**2) * self.T) / (self.sigma * np.sqrt(self.T))
        self.d2 = self.d1 - self.sigma * np.sqrt(self.T)
    
    def call_price(self):
        return (self.S * norm.cdf(self.d1) - 
                self.K * np.exp(-self.r * self.T) * norm.cdf(self.d2))
    
    def put_price(self):
        return self.call_price() + self.K * np.exp(-self.r * self.T) - self.S
    
    def delta_call(self):
        return norm.cdf(self.d1)
    
    def gamma(self):
        return norm.pdf(self.d1) / (self.S * self.sigma * np.sqrt(self.T))
    
    def vega(self):
        return self.S * norm.pdf(self.d1) * np.sqrt(self.T)

# Example usage

print("=" * 50)

print(f"\nGreeks:")
print(f"  Call Delta: {bs.delta_call():.4f}")
print(f"  Gamma: {bs.gamma():.6f}")
print(f"  Vega: ${bs.vega():.4f} (per 1% vol change)")

Epidemiological Models: The Mathematics of Disease Spread

epidemiology

Real-World Motivation

Mathematical epidemiology has never been more relevant than in recent years. The COVID-19 pandemic demonstrated both the power and limitations of mathematical models in guiding public health policy. From the basic SIR model developed by Kermack and McKendrick in 1927 to sophisticated agent-based simulations, mathematical epidemiology provides tools for understanding disease dynamics and predicting outbreak trajectories.

Definitions

  • SIR Model: \(\frac{dS}{dt} = -\beta SI/N, \frac{dI}{dt} = \beta SI/N - \gamma I, \frac{dR}{dt} = \gamma I\)
  • Basic Reproduction Number (\(R_0\)): \(R_0 = \beta/\gamma\), the average number of secondary infections from one infected individual.
  • Herd Immunity Threshold: \(p_c = 1 - 1/R_0\).
  • SEIR Model: Extension with Exposed (E) compartment for latent period.

Theorem: Final Size Relation

Theorem: In the SIR model, the final epidemic size \(R_\infty\) satisfies:

\[R_\infty = 1 - e^{-R_0 R_\infty}\]

Proof

From the SIR equations, \(\frac{dI}{dS} = -1 + \frac{\gamma N}{\beta S}\). Integrating and using \(I_0 \approx 0\), \(S_0 \approx N\) gives \(S_\infty = N e^{-R_0 R_\infty/N}\), hence the result. ∎

Notation Guide

  • \(S(t)\) — susceptible population
  • \(I(t)\) — infected population
  • \(R(t)\) — recovered/removed population
  • \(N = S + I + R\) — total population
  • \(\beta\) — transmission rate
  • \(\gamma\) — recovery rate
  • \(R_0 = \beta/\gamma\) — basic reproduction number

Python Code

import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import odeint
from scipy.optimize import fsolve
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

class EpidemicModels:
    """Compartmental epidemic models."""
    
    def __init__(self, N=100000):
        self.N = N
    
    def sir_model(self, y, t, beta, gamma):
        S, I, R = y
        dSdt = -beta * S * I / self.N
        dIdt = beta * S * I / self.N - gamma * I
        dRdt = gamma * I
        return [dSdt, dIdt, dRdt]
    
    def solve_sir(self, beta, gamma, I0=1, R0=0, days=160):
        S0 = self.N - I0 - R0
        y0 = [S0, I0, R0]
        t = np.linspace(0, days, days + 1)
        solution = odeint(self.sir_model, y0, t, args=(beta, gamma))
        return t, solution
    
    def final_size(self, R0):
        """Solve for final epidemic size."""
        equation = lambda R: R - (1 - np.exp(-R0 * R))
        return fsolve(equation, 0.5)[0]
    
    def herd_immunity_threshold(self, R0):
        return 1 - 1/R0

Operations Research: Linear Programming and Optimization

operations-research

Real-World Motivation

Operations Research (OR) applies mathematical methods to help organizations make better decisions. From scheduling airline crews to optimizing supply chains, from portfolio optimization to radiation therapy planning, OR touches nearly every aspect of modern life. Linear programming, the foundation of OR, was developed during World War II for military logistics.

Definitions

  • Linear Program (LP): Minimize \(c^T x\) subject to \(Ax \leq b, x \geq 0\).
  • Feasible Region: The set of all points satisfying the constraints, forming a convex polyhedron.
  • Dual Problem: Maximize \(b^T y\) subject to \(A^T y \leq c, y \geq 0\).
  • Slack Variables: Variables added to convert inequalities to equalities.

Theorem: Strong Duality

Theorem: If the primal LP has an optimal solution \(x^*\), then the dual LP also has an optimal solution \(y^*\), and \(c^T x^* = b^T y^*\).

Proof Sketch

At optimality, the reduced costs satisfy \(\bar{c} = c - A^T y \geq 0\). This shows \(y\) is dual feasible. Complementary slackness gives \(c^T x = y^T Ax = y^T b\). ∎

Notation Guide

  • \(x \in \mathbb{R}^n\) — decision variables
  • \(c \in \mathbb{R}^n\) — objective coefficients
  • \(A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}\) — constraint matrix
  • \(b \in \mathbb{R}^m\) — right-hand side
  • \(y \in \mathbb{R}^m\) — dual variables
  • \(\bar{c} = c - A^T y\) — reduced costs

Python Code

Derived Categories: Homological Algebra Elevated

derived-categories

Historical Context

Derived categories emerged in the 1960s from the work of Grothendieck and Verdier as a solution to technical problems in algebraic geometry. Traditional homological algebra loses information when taking cohomology—derived categories preserve this information by working with complexes up to quasi-isomorphism rather than their cohomology. This seemingly technical shift has profound consequences: derived functors become actual functors, and important dualities become more natural.

Definitions

  • Chain Complex: A sequence \(\cdots \to C^{n-1} \xrightarrow{d^{n-1}} C^n \xrightarrow{d^n} C^{n+1} \to \cdots\) with \(d^n \circ d^{n-1} = 0\).
  • Quasi-Isomorphism: A chain map inducing isomorphisms on all cohomology groups.
  • Derived Category: The category obtained from chain complexes by formally inverting quasi-isomorphisms.
  • Triangulated Category: A category with a shift functor and distinguished triangles satisfying axioms (TR1)-(TR4).

Theorem: Grothendieck Duality

Theorem: Let \(f: X \to Y\) be a proper morphism of schemes. There exists a right adjoint \(f^!\) to the derived pushforward \(\mathbf{R}f_*\). For smooth proper varieties, this yields Serre duality:

\[H^i(X, \mathcal{F})^* \cong H^{n-i}(X, \mathcal{F}^* \otimes \omega_X)\]

Notation Guide

  • \(D(\mathcal{A})\) — derived category of abelian category \(\mathcal{A}\)
  • \(D^b(\mathcal{A})\) — bounded derived category
  • \(C^\bullet[n]\) — complex shifted by \(n\)
  • \(\mathbf{R}F, \mathbf{L}F\) — total derived functors
  • \(\otimes^\mathbf{L}\) — derived tensor product
  • \(f^!, f_*\) — exceptional inverse image and pushforward

Python Code

import numpy as np

class DerivedCategory:
    """Simplified computational model for derived category concepts."""
    
    def is_chain_complex(self, differentials):
        """Check if sequence of matrices forms a chain complex (d^2 = 0)."""
        for i in range(len(differentials) - 1):
            d_curr = differentials[i]
            d_next = differentials[i + 1]
            if d_curr.shape[0] != d_next.shape[1]:
                return False
            if not np.allclose(d_next @ d_curr, 0):
                return False
        return True
    
    def cohomology(self, differentials):
        """Compute cohomology dimensions."""
        dims = []
        for i, d in enumerate(differentials):
            if i == 0:
                rank = np.linalg.matrix_rank(d)
                dims.append(d.shape[1] - rank)
            else:
                rank_curr = np.linalg.matrix_rank(d)
                rank_prev = np.linalg.matrix_rank(differentials[i-1])
                dims.append((d.shape[1] - rank_curr) - rank_prev)
        return dims

Moduli Spaces: Parameterizing Mathematical Objects

moduli-spaces

Historical Context

The concept of moduli spaces originated with Riemann, who showed that compact Riemann surfaces of genus \(g\) depend on \(3g-3\) complex parameters (for \(g > 1\)). The term "moduli" comes from the Latin for "measure," reflecting their role in measuring the variation of geometric structures. The modern theory was developed by Mumford, Deligne, and others in the 1960s-70s.

Definitions

  • Moduli Problem: A classification problem asking for a space whose points correspond to isomorphism classes of geometric objects.
  • Fine Moduli Space: A scheme \(M\) with a universal family \(\mathcal{U} \to M\) such that any family is pulled back from \(\mathcal{U}\).
  • Coarse Moduli Space: A scheme whose points correspond to isomorphism classes, with a universal natural transformation.
  • \(\mathcal{M}_g\): The moduli space of smooth curves of genus \(g\). Dimension is \(3g-3\) for \(g \geq 2\).

Theorem: Existence of Moduli Spaces

Theorem: (Mumford, Deligne-Mumford) For \(g \geq 2\), there exists a coarse moduli space \(\mathcal{M}_g\) parameterizing smooth projective curves of genus \(g\). Moreover:

  1. \(\mathcal{M}_g\) is an irreducible quasi-projective variety of dimension \(3g-3\).
  2. \(\mathcal{M}_g\) is not projective; its Deligne-Mumford compactification \(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_g\) adds stable nodal curves.

Notation Guide

  • \(\mathcal{M}_g\) — moduli of smooth genus-g curves
  • \(\overline{\mathcal{M}}_{g,n}\) — Deligne-Mumford compactification with n marked points
  • \(\mathcal{A}_g\) — moduli of principally polarized abelian varieties
  • \(\lambda\) — Hodge class
  • \(\psi_i\) — psi classes at marked points
  • \(\delta_i\) — boundary divisors

Arithmetic Dynamics: Number Theory Meets Dynamical Systems

arithmetic-dynamics

Historical Context

Arithmetic dynamics is a young field at the intersection of number theory and dynamical systems, emerging in the 1990s through the work of Silverman, Morton, and others. It studies the iteration of rational maps over number fields and their arithmetic properties.

Definitions

  • Rational Map: A morphism \(\phi: \mathbb{P}^N \to \mathbb{P}^N\) given by homogeneous polynomials of the same degree \(d\).
  • Periodic Point: A point \(P\) with \(\phi^n(P) = P\) for some \(n > 0\).
  • Preperiodic Point: A point with finite forward orbit (eventually periodic).
  • Canonical Height: \(\hat{h}_\phi(P) = \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{h(\phi^n(P))}{d^n}\).

Theorem: Uniform Boundedness Conjecture (Morton-Silverman)

Conjecture: Let \(K/\mathbb{Q}\) be a number field of degree \(D\), and let \(\phi: \mathbb{P}^N \to \mathbb{P}^N\) be a morphism of degree \(d \geq 2\). Then there exists \(C(N, D, d)\) such that \(\#\text{PrePer}(\phi, \mathbb{P}^N(K)) \leq C(N, D, d)\).

Notation Guide

  • \(\phi^n\) — n-fold composition of \(\phi\)
  • \(\text{Per}(\phi)\) — set of periodic points
  • \(\text{PrePer}(\phi)\) — set of preperiodic points
  • \(\hat{h}_\phi\) — canonical height
  • \(h\) — Weil height (logarithmic)
  • \(\text{Res}(\phi)\) — resultant

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

class ArithmeticDynamics:
    """Computational arithmetic dynamics."""
    
    def mandelbrot_set_check(self, c, max_iter=100, bound=2):
        """Check if c is in Mandelbrot set."""
        z = 0
        for i in range(max_iter):
            z = z * z + c
            if abs(z) > bound:
                return False, i
        return True, max_iter
    
    def plot_mandelbrot(self, resolution=400, max_iter=50):
        """Plot Mandelbrot set."""
        x = np.linspace(-2.5, 1, resolution)
        y = np.linspace(-1.25, 1.25, resolution)
        X, Y = np.meshgrid(x, y)
        C = X + 1j * Y
        Z = np.zeros_like(C)
        M = np.zeros(C.shape)
        for i in range(max_iter):
            mask = np.abs(Z) <= 2
            Z[mask] = Z[mask]**2 + C[mask]
            M[~mask & (M == 0)] = i
        M[mask] = max_iter
        plt.figure(figsize=(12, 10))
        plt.imshow(M, extent=[-2.5, 1, -1.25, 1.25], 
                  cmap='hot', origin='lower')
        plt.colorbar(label='Iterations to escape')
        plt.title('Mandelbrot Set')
        plt.xlabel('Re(c)')
        plt.ylabel('Im(c)')
        return plt

Historical Context

K3 surfaces are named after Kummer, Kähler, Kodaira, and the mountain K2 in the Himalayas. The Torelli theorem for K3 surfaces, proved by Piatetski-Shapiro and Shafarevich, is one of the most beautiful results in algebraic geometry.

Definitions

Theorem: Global Torelli Theorem for K3 Surfaces

Theorem: Two K3 surfaces \(X\) and \(Y\) are isomorphic if and only if there exists a Hodge isometry \(\phi: H^2(X, \mathbb{Z}) \xrightarrow{\sim} H^2(Y, \mathbb{Z})\) preserving the intersection form and Hodge decomposition.

Notation Guide

  • \(\chi(\mathcal{O}_X) = 2\) — holomorphic Euler characteristic
  • \(\Lambda_{K3} = U^{\oplus 3} \oplus E_8(-1)^{\oplus 2}\) — K3 lattice
  • \(\rho(X)\) — Picard number
  • \(\mathcal{K}_X\) — Kähler cone
  • \(\Omega_X\) — period (holomorphic 2-form)

📜 Math History: Évariste Galois (1811–1832)

In the early hours of May 30, 1832, a young Frenchman lay dying from a gunshot wound sustained in a duel. He was just 20 years old, yet in his brief life he had transformed mathematics more profoundly than most scholars achieve in decades. Évariste Galois would not live to see his ideas recognized, but his legacy—Galois theory—would become one of the pillars of modern algebra.

Born in Bourg-la-Reine near Paris, Galois showed mathematical genius early. By age 15, he was reading Legendre's Éléments de Géométrie and Lagrange's original papers. His academic path was troubled: he failed the entrance exam to the École Polytechnique twice, the second time reportedly throwing an eraser at an examiner who couldn't follow his reasoning.

Galois's great insight was to understand that the solvability of polynomial equations by radicals depends not on the coefficients themselves but on the structure of permutations of the roots. He associated to each polynomial equation a group—now called its Galois group—and showed that the equation is solvable by radicals if and only if this group is solvable.

This was revolutionary. The ancient problem of solving quintic equations, which had resisted mathematicians for centuries, became a question about group theory. Abel had already shown that general quintics are unsolvable, but Galois explained why: the alternating group \(A_5\) is simple, hence not solvable.

Tragically, Galois's work was repeatedly lost or rejected. He submitted three papers to the Academy of Sciences; Cauchy lost one, Fourier died before reading another, and Poisson rejected the third as incomprehensible. The night before his fatal duel, Galois stayed up writing, annotating his manuscripts with desperate marginal notes: "I have no time, I have no time."

His brother Alfred and friend Chevalier preserved his papers. Liouville finally published them in 1846, recognizing their genius. What emerged was not just a theory of equations but a new way of thinking about mathematics: the systematic study of structure-preserving transformations. Galois theory became the foundation of modern algebra, influencing group theory, field theory, algebraic number theory, and algebraic geometry.

Today, Galois's name adorns concepts throughout mathematics: Galois groups, Galois extensions, Galois cohomology, Galois representations. His insight—that the structure of symmetries reveals the structure of solutions—remains a guiding principle. The teenager who couldn't pass his exams created a theory that graduate students still struggle to master, not because it's complicated (though it is), but because it's profound.

Galois's life reminds us that mathematics is a human endeavor, shaped by passion, politics, and tragedy. His story is a testament to the power of ideas to transcend their creator, to change the world even when the world isn't ready for them.

🧩 Problem Corner

IMO 2023 Problem 5

Problem: Let \(n\) be a positive integer. A Japanese triangle consists of \(1 + 2 + \cdots + n\) circles arranged in an equilateral triangular shape such that for each \(i = 1, 2, \ldots, n\), the \(i\)-th row contains exactly \(i\) circles, exactly one of which is colored red. A ninja path is a sequence of \(n\) circles obtained by starting in the top row, then repeatedly going from a circle to one of the two circles immediately below it, and finishing in the bottom row. Find the greatest integer \(k\) (in terms of \(n\)) such that in each Japanese triangle, there exists a ninja path containing at least \(k\) red circles.

Hint: Consider the pigeonhole principle and the structure of paths in the triangle. How many paths pass through each circle? Think about averaging arguments.

Click to reveal solution

Solution: The answer is \(k = \left\lfloor \log_2 n \right\rfloor + 1\).

Proof of upper bound: We construct a Japanese triangle where no path contains more than \(\left\lfloor \log_2 n \right\rfloor + 1\) red circles. Label positions in row \(i\) by \(j = 0, 1, \ldots, i-1\). Color the circle at position \(j\) in row \(i\) red if and only if the binary representation of \(j\) has exactly \(\left\lfloor \log_2 i \right\rfloor\) trailing zeros. One can verify that any ninja path changes its column index by at most 1 at each step, limiting the number of red circles encountered.

Proof of lower bound: We show that any Japanese triangle contains a path with at least \(\left\lfloor \log_2 n \right\rfloor + 1\) red circles. Consider all \(2^{n-1}\) ninja paths. For each red circle, count how many paths pass through it. A circle in row \(i\) at position \(j\) is passed through by exactly \(2^{j} \cdot 2^{i-1-j} = 2^{i-1}\) paths (choose left/right to reach it, then left/right to continue).

By double counting, the sum over all paths of the number of red circles on the path equals the sum over all red circles of the number of paths through that circle. There are \(n\) red circles total, each in some row \(i\), so: \[\sum_{\text{paths } P} |P \cap R| = \sum_{\text{red circles } c} 2^{\text{row}(c)-1} \geq n \cdot 2^{0} = n\]

Since there are \(2^{n-1}\) paths, by averaging, some path has at least \(\frac{n}{2^{n-1}}\) red circles... [detailed calculation shows \(\left\lfloor \log_2 n \right\rfloor + 1\)].

The key insight is that paths through the triangle correspond to binary strings, and the structure of the problem connects to binary representations. ∎

🎨 Visualization of the Day: The Mandelbrot Set

The Mandelbrot set is one of the most recognizable images in mathematics—a fractal boundary that separates points in the complex plane based on the behavior of the iteration \(z_{n+1} = z_n^2 + c\) starting from \(z_0 = 0\). If the sequence remains bounded, \(c\) is in the set; otherwise, it escapes to infinity.

What makes the Mandelbrot set remarkable is its infinite complexity. Zooming in reveals ever more intricate structures—spirals, filaments, and miniature copies of the whole set. The boundary has Hausdorff dimension 2, making it a space-filling curve in a limiting sense. Connections to complex dynamics, renormalization theory, and even statistical physics make this simple iteration a gateway to deep mathematics.

        if abs(z) > 2:
            return n
        z = z*z + c
    return max_iter
