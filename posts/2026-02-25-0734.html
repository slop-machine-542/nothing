1. Sheaf Theory: Local-to-Global Mathematics

Tags: sheaf-theory algebraic-geometry topology

Motivation

How do we study mathematical objects that are defined locally but have global properties? Sheaf theory provides the framework for passing from local data to global conclusions. Originally developed for complex analysis and algebraic geometry, sheaves have become fundamental across mathematics—from topology and differential geometry to logic and number theory. A sheaf assigns algebraic structures (groups, rings, etc.) to open sets of a topological space in a way that allows consistent patching of local information. This enables powerful techniques like sheaf cohomology, which measures the obstructions to solving global problems locally.

Definitions

  • Presheaf: A presheaf \( \mathcal{F} \) of abelian groups on a topological space \( X \) assigns to each open set \( U \subseteq X \) an abelian group \( \mathcal{F}(U) \), and to each inclusion \( V \subseteq U \) a restriction homomorphism \( \rho_{UV}: \mathcal{F}(U) \to \mathcal{F}(V) \), satisfying:
    • \( \rho_{UU} = \text{id}_{\mathcal{F}(U)} \)
    • \( \rho_{VW} \circ \rho_{UV} = \rho_{UW} \) for \( W \subseteq V \subseteq U \)
  • Sheaf: A presheaf is a sheaf if it satisfies:
    • Locality: If \( \{U_i\} \) covers \( U \) and \( s, t \in \mathcal{F}(U) \) with \( s|_{U_i} = t|_{U_i} \) for all \( i \), then \( s = t \)
    • Gluing: If \( \{U_i\} \) covers \( U \) and sections \( s_i \in \mathcal{F}(U_i) \) agree on overlaps (\( s_i|_{U_i \cap U_j} = s_j|_{U_i \cap U_j} \)), then there exists \( s \in \mathcal{F}(U) \) with \( s|_{U_i} = s_i \)
  • Stalk: The stalk of \( \mathcal{F} \) at \( x \in X \) is \( \mathcal{F}_x = \varinjlim_{U \ni x} \mathcal{F}(U) \), the direct limit over open neighborhoods of \( x \).
  • Sheafification: The sheafification \( \mathcal{F}^+ \) of a presheaf is the "closest" sheaf to \( \mathcal{F} \), satisfying a universal property.

Theorem: Exponential Sequence on Complex Manifolds

Theorem: Let \( X \) be a complex manifold. There is a short exact sequence of sheaves:

\[ 0 \longrightarrow \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\cdot 2\pi i} \mathcal{O}_X \xrightarrow{\exp} \mathcal{O}_X^* \longrightarrow 0 \]

where \( \mathcal{O}_X \) is the sheaf of holomorphic functions and \( \mathcal{O}_X^* \) is the sheaf of non-vanishing holomorphic functions.

Corollary: The long exact sequence in cohomology yields:

\[ \cdots \to H^1(X, \mathbb{Z}) \to H^1(X, \mathcal{O}_X) \to \text{Pic}(X) \xrightarrow{c_1} H^2(X, \mathbb{Z}) \to \cdots \]

where \( \text{Pic}(X) \cong H^1(X, \mathcal{O}_X^*) \) is the Picard group of holomorphic line bundles.

Proof Sketch

To verify exactness of the exponential sequence:

  1. Injectivity: The map \( \mathbb{Z} \to \mathcal{O}_X \) is injective (constant integer functions are distinct).
  2. A holomorphic function \( f \) satisfies \( \exp(f) = 1 \) iff \( f = 2\pi i n \) for some integer \( n \).
  3. Surjectivity: Locally, any non-vanishing holomorphic function \( g \) has a logarithm: since \( g \neq 0 \), we can define \( \log(g) \) on simply connected domains.

The long exact sequence follows from standard homological algebra. The connecting homomorphism \( \text{Pic}(X) \to H^2(X, \mathbb{Z}) \) is the first Chern class map, which classifies line bundles topologically.

Notation Guide

  • \( \mathcal{F}(U) \) — Sections of sheaf \( \mathcal{F} \) over open set \( U \)
  • \( s|_V \) or \( \rho_{UV}(s) \) — Restriction of section \( s \) to \( V \)
  • \( \mathcal{F}_x \) — Stalk of sheaf at point \( x \)
  • \( \Gamma(U, \mathcal{F}) \) — Alternative notation for global sections
  • \( H^i(X, \mathcal{F}) \) — Sheaf cohomology groups
  • \( \text{Pic}(X) \) — Picard group of line bundles

2. Homotopy Theory: Higher-Dimensional Paths

Tags: homotopy-theory algebraic-topology higher-categories

Motivation

While the fundamental group captures one-dimensional holes in a space, higher homotopy groups detect higher-dimensional voids. The classification of spaces up to homotopy equivalence is one of the central problems of algebraic topology. Homotopy theory has profound connections to algebraic geometry (via motivic homotopy theory), mathematical physics (string theory and quantum field theory), and even logic (homotopy type theory). The study of homotopy types—spaces considered up to weak homotopy equivalence—has led to the development of model categories and (∞,1)-categories, providing frameworks for abstract homotopy theory.

Definitions

  • Homotopy: Maps \( f, g: X \to Y \) are homotopic (written \( f \simeq g \)) if there exists \( H: X \times [0,1] \to Y \) with \( H(x,0) = f(x) \) and \( H(x,1) = g(x) \).
  • Homotopy Equivalence: Spaces \( X \) and \( Y \) are homotopy equivalent if there exist maps \( f: X \to Y \) and \( g: Y \to X \) with \( g \circ f \simeq \text{id}_X \) and \( f \circ g \simeq \text{id}_Y \).
  • n-th Homotopy Group:\u003c/strong> \( \pi_n(X, x_0) = [(S^n, s_0), (X, x_0)] \), homotopy classes of based maps from the n-sphere.
  • Path Space:\u003c/strong> \( PX = X^{[0,1]} \), the space of all paths in \( X \) with the compact-open topology.
  • Loop Space:\u003c/strong> \( \Omega X \), the subspace of \( PX \) consisting of loops based at \( x_0 \).
  • Suspension:\u003c/strong> \( \Sigma X = X \times [0,1] / (X \times \{0\} \cup X \times \{1\} \cup \{x_0\} \times [0,1]) \).

Theorem: Homotopy Excision (Blakers-Massey)

Theorem: Let \( X = A \cup B \) where \( A \) and \( B \) are open in \( X \). Suppose \( (A, A \cap B) \) is \( p \)-connected and \( (B, A \cap B) \) is \( q \)-connected. Then the inclusion-induced map:

\[ \pi_i(A, A \cap B) \longrightarrow \pi_i(X, B) \]

is an isomorphism for \( i < p + q \) and a surjection for \( i = p + q \).

Proof Sketch

The proof uses cellular approximation and the homotopy extension property:

  1. First reduce to the case where \( (A, A \cap B) \) and \( (B, A \cap B) \) are relative CW complexes.
  2. Use cellular approximation to show any map \( (D^i, S^{i-1}) \to (X, B) \) can be homotoped into \( A \) when \( i < p + q \).
  3. The connectivity assumptions ensure that cells in dimensions \( \leq p \) in \( A \) and \( \leq q \) in \( B \) don't create obstructions below dimension \( p + q \).
  4. The key insight is that homotopies can be constructed by working cell-by-cell, using the product structure of cells.

Notation Guide

  • \( f \simeq g \) — f is homotopic to g
  • \( [X, Y] \) — Set of homotopy classes of maps
  • \( \pi_n(X) \) — n-th homotopy group
  • \( \Omega X \) — Loop space of X
  • \( \Sigma X \) — Suspension of X
  • \( X \wedge Y \) — Smash product
  • \( X \simeq Y \) — Homotopy equivalence

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from mpl_toolkits.mplot3d import Axes3D

class HomotopyPath:
    """Represents a homotopy between two paths."""
    
    def __init__(self, path1, path2, space_dim=2):
        self.path1 = path1  # Starting path
        self.path2 = path2  # Ending path
        self.space_dim = space_dim
    
    def evaluate(self, t, s):
        """
        Evaluate the homotopy H(s, t).
        s: parameter along path [0, 1]
        t: homotopy parameter [0, 1] (0 = path1, 1 = path2)
        """
        # Linear homotopy (works when paths are homotopic in convex set)
        return (1 - t) * self.path1(s) + t * self.path2(s)
    
    def plot_homotopy(self, num_t=10, num_s=100):
        """Visualize the homotopy as a surface."""
        fig = plt.figure(figsize=(12, 5))
        
        # 3D plot of homotopy surface
        ax1 = fig.add_subplot(121, projection='3d')
        
        s_vals = np.linspace(0, 1, num_s)
        t_vals = np.linspace(0, 1, num_t)
        S, T = np.meshgrid(s_vals, t_vals)
        
        # For 2D paths, plot x-coordinate evolution
        X = np.zeros_like(S)
        Y = np.zeros_like(S)
        
        for i, t in enumerate(t_vals):
            for j, s in enumerate(s_vals):
                point = self.evaluate(t, s)
                X[i, j] = point[0]
                Y[i, j] = point[1]
        
        ax1.plot_surface(S, T, X, alpha=0.7, cmap='viridis')
        ax1.set_xlabel('Path parameter s')
        ax1.set_ylabel('Homotopy parameter t')
        ax1.set_zlabel('x-coordinate')
        ax1.set_title('Homotopy Surface (x-coordinate)')
        
        # 2D plot showing path evolution
        ax2 = fig.add_subplot(122)
        colors = plt.cm.viridis(np.linspace(0, 1, num_t))
        
        for i, t in enumerate(t_vals):
            path_points = np.array([self.evaluate(t, s) for s in s_vals])
            ax2.plot(path_points[:, 0], path_points[:, 1], 
                    color=colors[i], alpha=0.7, linewidth=2)
        
        # Highlight start and end paths
        path1_points = np.array([self.path1(s) for s in s_vals])
        path2_points = np.array([self.path2(s) for s in s_vals])
        ax2.plot(path1_points[:, 0], path1_points[:, 1], 
                'b-', linewidth=3, label='Path 1 (t=0)')
        ax2.plot(path2_points[:, 0], path2_points[:, 1], 
                'r-', linewidth=3, label='Path 2 (t=1)')
        
        ax2.set_xlabel('x')
        ax2.set_ylabel('y')
        ax2.set_title('Path Evolution')
        ax2.legend()
        ax2.axis('equal')
        ax2.grid(True, alpha=0.3)
        
        plt.tight_layout()
        return fig

def circle_path(s, radius=1, center=(0, 0)):
    """Parameterize a circle."""
    angle = 2 * np.pi * s
    return np.array([
        center[0] + radius * np.cos(angle),
        center[1] + radius * np.sin(angle)
    ])

def line_path(s, start=(1, 0), end=(1, 0)):
    """Parameterize a line segment."""
    return np.array([
        start[0] + s * (end[0] - start[0]),
        start[1] + s * (end[1] - start[1])
    ])

3. Harmonic Analysis: Decomposing Functions into Waves

Tags: harmonic-analysis fourier-analysis functional-analysis

Motivation

Harmonic analysis studies the representation of functions or signals as superpositions of basic waves. Originating from Fourier's study of heat conduction, it has grown into a vast field with applications in partial differential equations, signal processing, quantum mechanics, and number theory. The central insight—that complex phenomena can be understood by decomposing them into simpler oscillatory components—has proven extraordinarily powerful. Modern harmonic analysis extends classical Fourier theory to groups, manifolds, and fractals, providing tools for understanding everything from prime number distribution to image compression.

Definitions

  • Fourier Transform: For \( f \in L^1(\mathbb{R}^n) \):

    \[ \hat{f}(\xi) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^n} f(x) e^{-2\pi i x \cdot \xi} dx \]

  • Inverse Fourier Transform:

    \[ f(x) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^n} \hat{f}(\xi) e^{2\pi i x \cdot \xi} d\xi \]

  • Plancherel's Theorem: The Fourier transform extends to a unitary operator on \( L^2(\mathbb{R}^n) \), i.e., \( \|f\|_{L^2} = \|\hat{f}\|_{L^2} \).
  • Hardy-Littlewood Maximal Function: For \( f \in L^1_{\text{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^n) \):

    \[ Mf(x) = \sup_{r > 0} \frac{1}{|B(x,r)|} \int_{B(x,r)} |f(y)| dy \]

  • Singular Integral: An operator of the form \( Tf(x) = \int K(x,y) f(y) dy \) where \( K \) is singular along the diagonal.

Theorem: Boundedness of the Hilbert Transform

Theorem: The Hilbert transform \( H \), defined for \( f \in L^p(\mathbb{R}) \) (\( 1 \leq p < \infty \)) by:

\[ Hf(x) = \frac{1}{\pi} \text{p.v.} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{f(y)}{x-y} dy \]

extends to a bounded linear operator on \( L^p(\mathbb{R}) \) for \( 1 < p < \infty \). Specifically:

\[ \|Hf\|_{L^p} \leq C_p \|f\|_{L^p} \]

where \( C_p = \tan\left(\frac{\pi}{2p}\right) \) for \( 1 < p \leq 2 \) and \( C_p = \cot\left(\frac{\pi}{2p}\right) \) for \( 2 \leq p < \infty \).

Proof Sketch

The proof uses complex analysis and interpolation:

  1. For \( p = 2 \), use the Fourier transform: \( \widehat{Hf}(\xi) = -i \text{sgn}(\xi) \hat{f}(\xi) \), so \( \|Hf\|_{L^2} = \|f\|_{L^2} \).
  2. For \( p = 1 \), the Hilbert transform is not bounded, but satisfies a weak-type estimate.
  3. Use the Marcinkiewicz interpolation theorem to get boundedness for \( 1 < p < 2 \).
  4. For \( p > 2 \), use duality: \( H \) is self-adjoint up to sign, so boundedness follows from the \( p < 2 \) case.

Notation Guide

  • \( \hat{f} \) or \( \mathcal{F}(f) \) — Fourier transform
  • \( \check{f} \) — Inverse Fourier transform
  • \( Hf \) — Hilbert transform
  • \( Mf \) — Maximal function
  • \( \text{p.v.} \) — Principal value
  • \( \mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^n) \) — Schwartz space of rapidly decreasing functions

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from numpy.fft import fft, ifft, fftshift, ifftshift, fftfreq

def fourier_transform_1d(signal, dt=0.01):
    """
    Compute the Fourier transform of a 1D signal.
    """
    N = len(signal)
    freqs = fftfreq(N, dt)
    spectrum = fftshift(fft(signal)) * dt
    return fftshift(freqs), spectrum

def inverse_fourier_transform_1d(spectrum, dt=0.01):
    """
    Compute the inverse Fourier transform.
    """
    N = len(spectrum)
    signal = ifft(ifftshift(spectrum)) / dt
    return signal

def hilbert_transform(signal, dt=0.01):
    """
    Compute the Hilbert transform using FFT.
    Hf = F^{-1}[-i * sgn(ξ) * F[f]]
    """
    N = len(signal)
    spectrum = fft(signal)
    freqs = fftfreq(N, dt)
    
    # Multiply by -i * sgn(ξ)
    # Note: fftfreq gives negative frequencies second half
    multiplier = -1j * np.sign(freqs)
    
    hilbert_spectrum = spectrum * multiplier
    hilbert_signal = ifft(hilbert_spectrum).real
    
    return hilbert_signal

import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import linprog

def nash_equilibrium(A, B):
    """Find Nash equilibrium for 2-player game."""
    m, n = A.shape
    c = np.zeros(m + 1)
    c[-1] = -1
    A_ub = np.hstack([-B.T, np.ones((n, 1))])
    b_ub = np.zeros(n)
    A_eq = np.zeros((1, m + 1))
    A_eq[0, :m] = 1
    b_eq = np.array([1])
    bounds = [(0, None) for _ in range(m)] + [(None, None)]
    result = linprog(c, A_ub=A_ub, b_ub=b_ub, A_eq=A_eq, b_eq=b_eq, bounds=bounds)
    return result.x[:m], result.x[-1]

def needleman_wunsch(seq1, seq2, match=1, mismatch=-1, gap=-2):
    m, n = len(seq1), len(seq2)
    F = [[0] * (n + 1) for _ in range(m + 1)]
    for i in range(m + 1):
        F[i][0] = gap * i
    for j in range(n + 1):
        F[0][j] = gap * j
    for i in range(1, m + 1):
        for j in range(1, n + 1):
            score = match if seq1[i-1] == seq2[j-1] else mismatch
            F[i][j] = max(F[i-1][j-1] + score, F[i-1][j] + gap, F[i][j-1] + gap)
    return F[m][n]

def pagerank(adj, alpha=0.85):
    n = adj.shape[0]
    degrees = np.sum(adj, axis=1)
    P = np.zeros_like(adj, dtype=float)
    for i in range(n):
        if degrees[i] > 0:
            P[i, :] = adj[i, :] / degrees[i]
    r = np.ones(n) / n
    for _ in range(100):
        r = alpha * P.T @ r + (1 - alpha) / n
    return r

def barnsley_fern(n=50000):
    x, y = [0], [0]
    probs = [0.01, 0.85, 0.07, 0.07]
    transforms = [
        [0, 0, 0, 0.16, 0, 0],
        [0.85, 0.04, -0.04, 0.85, 0, 1.6],
        [0.2, -0.26, 0.23, 0.22, 0, 1.6],
        [-0.15, 0.28, 0.26, 0.24, 0, 0.44]
    ]
    for _ in range(n):
        r = np.random.random()
        idx = 0 if r < 0.01 else 1 if r < 0.86 else 2 if r < 0.93 else 3
        a, b, c, d, e, f = transforms[idx]
        x.append(a * x[-1] + b * y[-1] + e)
        y.append(c * x[-2] + d * y[-1] + f)
    return np.array(x), np.array(y)

🧩 Problem Corner

Functional Equation

Find all \( f: \mathbb{Z}^+ \to \mathbb{Z}^+ \) such that: \( f(mn) = \text{lcm}(m, n) \cdot \gcd(f(m), f(n)) \)

Hint

Consider what happens when \( m = n \) and when \( n = 1 \).

Solution

Setting \( m = n \) gives \( f(n^2) = n \cdot f(n) \). Setting \( n = 1 \) gives \( f(m) = m \cdot \gcd(f(m), f(1)) \), implying \( m \mid f(m) \). The solution is \( f(n) = cn \) for any constant \( c \in \mathbb{Z}^+ \).
