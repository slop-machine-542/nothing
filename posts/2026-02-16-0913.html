1. Algebraic Geometry: Bézout's Theorem

Introduction

Algebraic geometry studies the geometry of solutions to polynomial equations. One of its most fundamental results is Bézout's Theorem, which gives a precise count of the intersection points of two algebraic curves.

Unlike Euclidean geometry where two lines typically intersect at one point, algebraic curves can intersect in surprising ways. Bézout's theorem accounts for this complexity by incorporating the degrees of the curves and counting intersections with appropriate multiplicities.

Notation Guide

  • \\(\\mathbb{A}^2\\) or \\(\\mathbb{C}^2\\) — the affine plane (2-dimensional space over a field)
  • \\(\\mathbb{P}^2\\) — the projective plane (compactification of affine space)
  • \\(V(f) = \\{(x,y) : f(x,y) = 0\\}\\) — the zero set (variety) of polynomial \\(f\\)
  • \\(\\deg(f)\\) — degree of polynomial \\(f\\) (highest total degree of any term)
  • \\(I_p(C, D)\\) — intersection multiplicity of curves \\(C\\) and \\(D\\) at point \\(p\\)
Bézout's Theorem

Let \\(C\\) and \\(D\\) be two projective plane curves defined by homogeneous polynomials \\(F\\) and \\(G\\) of degrees \\(m\\) and \\(n\\) respectively, with no common components. Then:

\\[\\sum_{p \\in C \\cap D} I_p(C, D) = m \\cdot n\\]

That is, the number of intersection points, counted with multiplicity, equals the product of the degrees.

Proof Sketch (using resultants)

Step 1: Work in projective space \\(\\mathbb{P}^2\\) to ensure all intersections are captured (including "points at infinity").

Step 2: For curves \\(F(x,y,z) = 0\\) and \\(G(x,y,z) = 0\\), eliminate one variable using the resultant. The resultant \\(\\text{Res}_z(F, G)\\) is a polynomial in \\(x\\) and \\(y\\) that vanishes precisely when \\(F\\) and \\(G\\) have a common root in \\(z\\).

Notation: The resultant is a determinant of the Sylvester matrix, which has size \\((m+n) \\times (m+n)\\).

Step 3: The degree of \\(\\text{Res}_z(F, G)\\) in \\(x\\) and \\(y\\) is \\(m \\cdot n\\).

Step 4: By the fundamental theorem of algebra, this polynomial has exactly \\(m \\cdot n\\) roots (counted with multiplicity) in \\(\\mathbb{P}^1\\).

Step 5: Each root corresponds to an intersection point of \\(C\\) and \\(D\\), with multiplicity equal to the intersection multiplicity. ∎

2. Differential Geometry: The Gauss-Bonnet Theorem

Introduction

Differential geometry studies geometric properties of curves and surfaces using calculus. The Gauss-Bonnet Theorem is one of its most profound results, connecting local curvature to global topology.

This theorem reveals a deep relationship between geometry (curvature) and topology (the Euler characteristic). It states that the total Gaussian curvature of a compact surface equals \\(2\\pi\\) times its Euler characteristic.

Notation Guide

  • \\(M\\) — a compact 2-dimensional surface (manifold)
  • \\(K\\) — Gaussian curvature at a point (product of principal curvatures)
  • \\(\\kappa_g\\) — geodesic curvature of a curve on the surface
  • \\(dA\\) — area element on the surface
  • \\(ds\\) — arc length element
  • \\(\\chi(M)\\) — Euler characteristic of \\(M\\)
  • \\(\\partial M\\) — boundary of \\(M\\) (if any)
The Gauss-Bonnet Theorem

Let \\(M\\) be a compact, oriented surface with boundary \\(\\partial M\\). Then:

\\[\\int_M K \\, dA + \\int_{\\partial M} \\kappa_g \\, ds = 2\\pi \\chi(M)\\]

For a closed surface (no boundary):

\\[\\int_M K \\, dA = 2\\pi \\chi(M)\\]

where \\(\\chi(M) = V - E + F\\) is the Euler characteristic.

Proof Sketch (for compact surfaces without boundary)

Step 1: Triangulate the surface \\(M\\) into triangles \\(T_1, \\ldots, T_n\\).

Step 2: For each triangle, apply the local Gauss-Bonnet formula:

\\[\\int_{T_i} K \\, dA + \\int_{\\partial T_i} \\kappa_g \\, ds + \\sum_{j=1}^3 (\\pi - \\alpha_{ij}) = 2\\pi\\]

where \\(\\alpha_{ij}\\) are the interior angles.

Step 3: Sum over all triangles. The boundary integrals cancel (each edge appears twice with opposite orientation).

Step 4: The angle sum becomes:

\\[\\sum_{i=1}^n \\sum_{j=1}^3 (\\pi - \\alpha_{ij}) = 3\\pi n - \\sum_{\\text{all angles}} \\alpha_{ij}\\]

Step 5: At each vertex, the angles sum to \\(2\\pi\\). With \\(V\\) vertices:

\\[3\\pi n - 2\\pi V = 3\\pi(2E/3) - 2\\pi V = 2\\pi E - 2\\pi V\\]

(using \\(3n = 2E\\) since each edge belongs to 2 triangles).

Step 6: Since \\(n = F\\) (number of faces):

\\[\\int_M K \\, dA + 2\\pi F - 2\\pi V = 2\\pi F\\]

\\[\\int_M K \\, dA = 2\\pi(V - E + F) = 2\\pi \\chi(M)\\] ∎

Python Implementation

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def gaussian_curvature_sphere(R):
    """
    Gaussian curvature of a sphere of radius R.
    
    For a sphere: K = 1/R² (constant everywhere)
    
    Notation: K is the Gaussian curvature (product of principal curvatures k₁ and k₂)
    """
    return 1 / R**2

def total_curvature_sphere(R):
    """
    Total curvature of a sphere = ∫ K dA = K × Area = (1/R²) × (4πR²) = 4π
    
    By Gauss-Bonnet: ∫ K dA = 2π χ(S²)
    For sphere: χ(S²) = 2, so ∫ K dA = 4π ✓
    """
    K = gaussian_curvature_sphere(R)
    area = 4 * np.pi * R**2
    return K * area

def euler_characteristic_genus(g):
    """
    Euler characteristic of a closed orientable surface of genus g.
    
    χ = 2 - 2g
    
    Notation:
    - g is the genus (number of "holes")
    - Sphere: g = 0, χ = 2
    - Torus: g = 1, χ = 0
    - Double torus: g = 2, χ = -2
    """
    return 2 - 2*g

def gauss_bonnet_verification():
    """
    Verify Gauss-Bonnet theorem for various surfaces.
    
    Theorem: ∫_M K dA = 2π χ(M) for closed surfaces
    """
    print("Gauss-Bonnet Theorem Verification")
    print("=" * 60)
    print("Theorem: ∫_M K dA = 2π χ(M)")
    print("-" * 60)
    
    # Sphere (genus 0)
    print("\n1. Sphere (genus g = 0)")
    print("-" * 40)
    R = 1.0
    chi_sphere = euler_characteristic_genus(0)
    total_K = total_curvature_sphere(R)
    expected = 2 * np.pi * chi_sphere
    
    print(f"Radius R = {R}")
    print(f"Gaussian curvature K = 1/R² = {gaussian_curvature_sphere(R):.4f} (constant)")
    print(f"Surface area = 4πR² = {4*np.pi*R**2:.4f}")
    print(f"Total curvature ∫K dA = {total_K:.4f}")
    print(f"Euler characteristic χ = 2 - 2(0) = {chi_sphere}")
    print(f"2πχ = {expected:.4f}")
    print(f"Verification: ∫K dA = 2πχ? {np.isclose(total_K, expected)}")
    
    # Torus (genus 1)
    print("\n2. Torus (genus g = 1)")
    print("-" * 40)
    chi_torus = euler_characteristic_genus(1)
    
    print(f"Euler characteristic χ = 2 - 2(1) = {chi_torus}")
    print(f"2πχ = {2*np.pi*chi_torus:.4f}")
    print(f"For a torus: ∫K dA = 0 (positive and negative curvature cancel)")
    print(f"Verification: 0 = 2π(0) = 0 ✓")
    
    # Summary table
    print("\n" + "=" * 60)
    print("Summary: Gauss-Bonnet for Surfaces of Different Genus")

3. Functional Analysis: The Hahn-Banach Theorem

Introduction

Functional analysis extends the methods of linear algebra and calculus to infinite-dimensional spaces. The Hahn-Banach Theorem is one of its cornerstone results, guaranteeing the existence of linear functionals with certain properties.

This theorem allows us to extend bounded linear functionals from subspaces to the whole space while preserving their norm. It has profound consequences including the separation of convex sets and the existence of dual space elements.

Notation Guide

  • \\(X\\) — a normed vector space (often Banach space)
  • \\(p: X \\to \\mathbb{R}\\) — a sublinear functional: \\(p(x+y) \\leq p(x) + p(y)\\) and \\(p(\\alpha x) = \\alpha p(x)\\) for \\(\\alpha \\geq 0\\)
  • \\(M \\subseteq X\\) — a subspace of \\(X\\)
  • \\(f: M \\to \\mathbb{R}\\) — a linear functional on \\(M\\)
  • \\(\\|f\\|\\) — operator norm of \\(f\\)
  • \\(X^*\\) — the dual space (space of continuous linear functionals on \\(X\\))
Hahn-Banach Theorem (Real Version)

Let \\(X\\) be a real vector space, \\(p: X \\to \\mathbb{R}\\) a sublinear functional, \\(M \\subseteq X\\) a subspace, and \\(f: M \\to \\mathbb{R}\\) a linear functional satisfying:

\\[f(x) \\leq p(x) \\quad \\text{for all } x \\in M\\]

Then there exists a linear extension \\(F: X \\to \\mathbb{R}\\) such that:

  1. \\(F(x) = f(x)\\) for all \\(x \\in M\\) (extension property)
  2. \\(F(x) \\leq p(x)\\) for all \\(x \\in X\\) (domination property)
Hahn-Banach Theorem (Normed Space Version)

Let \\(X\\) be a normed space, \\(M \\subseteq X\\) a subspace, and \\(f \\in M^*\\) (continuous linear functional on \\(M\\)). Then there exists \\(F \\in X^*\\) such that:

  1. \\(F|_M = f\\) (\\(F\\) extends \\(f\\))
  2. \\(\\|F\\|_{X^*} = \\|f\\|_{M^*}\\) (norm preservation)
Proof Sketch (via Zorn's Lemma)

Step 1: Define the set of all extensions:

\\[\\mathcal{E} = \\{(Y, g) : M \\subseteq Y \\subseteq X \\text{ subspace}, g: Y \\to \\mathbb{R} \\text{ linear}, g|_M = f, g \\leq p \\text{ on } Y\\}\\]

Step 2: Define partial order: \\((Y_1, g_1) \\leq (Y_2, g_2)\\) if \\(Y_1 \\subseteq Y_2\\) and \\(g_2|_{Y_1} = g_1\\).

Step 3: Every chain has an upper bound (take union). By Zorn's Lemma, there exists a maximal element \\((Y_{\\max}, g_{\\max})\\).

Step 4: Claim: \\(Y_{\\max} = X\\). If not, pick \\(x_0 \\in X \\setminus Y_{\\max}\\) and extend \\(g_{\\max}\\) to \\(\\text{span}(Y_{\\max} \\cup \\{x_0\\})\\).

Step 5: Define \\(g(y + \\alpha x_0) = g_{\\max}(y) + \\alpha c\\) for appropriate \\(c\\). We need:

\\[g_{\\max}(y) + c \\leq p(y + x_0) \\quad \\text{and} \\quad g_{\\max}(y) - c \\leq p(y - x_0)\\]

Step 6: Such \\(c\\) exists because for all \\(y_1, y_2 \\in Y_{\\max}\\):

\\[g_{\\max}(y_1) + g_{\\max}(y_2) = g_{\\max}(y_1 + y_2) \\leq p(y_1 + y_2) \\leq p(y_1 - x_0) + p(y_2 + x_0)\\]

So \\(\\sup_{y_2} [g_{\\max}(y_2) - p(y_2 + x_0)] \\leq \\inf_{y_1} [p(y_1 - x_0) - g_{\\max}(y_1)]\\), and any \\(c\\) in between works. ∎

1. Statistics: The Central Limit Theorem

Introduction

The Central Limit Theorem (CLT) is one of the most remarkable results in probability and statistics. It states that the sum (or average) of a large number of independent random variables, regardless of their original distribution, will be approximately normally distributed.

This explains why the normal distribution appears so frequently in nature and measurement errors, and it forms the foundation for much of statistical inference.

Notation Guide

  • \\(X_1, X_2, \\ldots, X_n\\) — independent, identically distributed (i.i.d.) random variables
  • \\(\\mu = \\mathbb{E}[X_i]\\) — expected value (mean)
  • \\(\\sigma^2 = \\text{Var}(X_i)\\) — variance
  • \\(S_n = X_1 + \\cdots + X_n\\) — sum of \\(n\\) random variables
  • \\(\\bar{X}_n = S_n/n\\) — sample mean
  • \\(Z_n = \\frac{S_n - n\\mu}{\\sigma\\sqrt{n}}\\) — standardized sum
  • \\(\\xrightarrow{d}\\) — convergence in distribution
  • \\(\\mathcal{N}(0,1)\\) — standard normal distribution
The Central Limit Theorem (Lindeberg-Lévy)

Let \\(X_1, X_2, \\ldots\\) be i.i.d. random variables with \\(\\mathbb{E}[X_i] = \\mu\\) and \\(\\text{Var}(X_i) = \\sigma^2 < \\infty\\). Then:

\\[\\frac{S_n - n\\mu}{\\sigma\\sqrt{n}} = \\frac{\\bar{X}_n - \\mu}{\\sigma/\\sqrt{n}} \\xrightarrow{d} \\mathcal{N}(0,1)\\]

Equivalently, for large \\(n\\):

\\[\\bar{X}_n \\approx \\mathcal{N}\\left(\\mu, \\frac{\\sigma^2}{n}\\right)\\]

Proof Sketch (using characteristic functions)

Step 1: Define the standardized variable:

\\[Z_n = \\frac{S_n - n\\mu}{\\sigma\\sqrt{n}} = \\sum_{i=1}^n \\frac{X_i - \\mu}{\\sigma\\sqrt{n}} = \\sum_{i=1}^n Y_{n,i}\\]

where \\(Y_{n,i} = \\frac{X_i - \\mu}{\\sigma\\sqrt{n}}\\).

Step 2: The characteristic function of \\(Z_n\\) is:

\\[\\varphi_{Z_n}(t) = \\mathbb{E}[e^{itZ_n}] = \\prod_{i=1}^n \\mathbb{E}[e^{itY_{n,i}}] = \\left(\\varphi_{Y_{n,1}}(t)\\right)^n\\]

Notation: \\(\\varphi_X(t) = \\mathbb{E}[e^{itX}]\\) is the characteristic function of \\(X\\).

Step 3: Taylor expand \\(\\varphi_{X_1 - \\mu}(s)\\) around \\(s = 0\\):

\\[\\varphi_{X_1 - \\mu}(s) = 1 + is\\mathbb{E}[X_1 - \\mu] - \\frac{s^2}{2}\\mathbb{E}[(X_1 - \\mu)^2] + o(s^2) = 1 - \\frac{\\sigma^2 s^2}{2} + o(s^2)\\]

Step 4: Substitute \\(s = \\frac{t}{\\sigma\\sqrt{n}}\\):

\\[\\varphi_{Y_{n,1}}(t) = 1 - \\frac{t^2}{2n} + o\\left(\\frac{1}{n}\\right)\\]

Step 5: Therefore:

\\[\\varphi_{Z_n}(t) = \\left(1 - \\frac{t^2}{2n} + o\\left(\\frac{1}{n}\\right)\\right)^n \\to e^{-t^2/2}\\]

Step 6: Since \\(e^{-t^2/2}\\) is the characteristic function of \\(\\mathcal{N}(0,1)\\), by the Continuity Theorem, \\(Z_n \\xrightarrow{d} \\mathcal{N}(0,1)\\). ∎

Python Implementation

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy import stats

def central_limit_theorem_demo():
    """
    Demonstrate the Central Limit Theorem.
    
    Theorem: The sum (or mean) of n i.i.d. random variables
    converges to a normal distribution as n → ∞.
    """
    print("Central Limit Theorem Demonstration")
    print("=" * 60)
    print("Theorem: (Sₙ - nμ)/(σ√n) → N(0,1) as n → ∞")
    print("-" * 60)
    
    # Different distributions to demonstrate universality
    distributions = {
        'Uniform(0,1)': lambda n: np.random.uniform(0, 1, n),
        'Exponential(1)': lambda n: np.random.exponential(1, n),
        'Bernoulli(0.3)': lambda n: np.random.binomial(1, 0.3, n),
        'Discrete {1,2,3,4,5}': lambda n: np.random.choice([1,2,3,4,5], n)
    }
    
    sample_sizes = [1, 5, 30, 100]
    
    for dist_name, dist_func in distributions.items():
        print(f"\n{dist_name}:")
        print("-" * 40)
        
        for n in sample_sizes:
            # Generate many sample means
            num_samples = 10000
            sample_means = []
            
            for _ in range(num_samples):
                samples = dist_func(n)
                sample_means.append(np.mean(samples))
            
            sample_means = np.array(sample_means)
            
            # Standardize
            standardized = (sample_means - np.mean(sample_means)) / np.std(sample_means)
            
            # Test normality (simplified)
            _, p_value = stats.normaltest(standardized)
            
            print(f"  n = {n:3d}: mean = {np.mean(sample_means):.4f}, "
                  f"std = {np.std(sample_means):.4f}, normality p-value = {p_value:.4f}")

# Run demonstration
central_limit_theorem_demo()

2. Numerical Analysis: The Fast Fourier Transform

Introduction

The Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) is one of the most important algorithms in numerical computing. It computes the Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) in \\(O(n \\log n)\\) time instead of \\(O(n^2)\\), enabling applications from signal processing to polynomial multiplication.

Notation Guide

  • \\(\\omega_n = e^{2\\pi i / n}\\) — primitive \\(n\\)-th root of unity
  • \\(\\omega_n^k = e^{2\\pi i k / n}\\) — \\(k\\)-th power of \\(\\omega_n\\)
  • \\(X_k\\) — \\(k\\)-th DFT coefficient
  • \\(x_j\\) — \\(j\\)-th time-domain sample
  • \\(i = \\sqrt{-1}\\) — imaginary unit
The Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT)

Given a sequence \\(x = (x_0, x_1, \\ldots, x_{n-1})\\), the DFT is:

\\[X_k = \\sum_{j=0}^{n-1} x_j \\omega_n^{jk}, \\quad k = 0, 1, \\ldots, n-1\\]

where \\(\\omega_n = e^{-2\\pi i / n}\\) (note: some conventions use \\(+\\) instead of \\(-\\)).

The Fast Fourier Transform (Cooley-Tukey)

For \\(n = 2^m\\), the DFT can be computed in \\(O(n \\log n)\\) operations using the divide-and-conquer approach:

\\[X_k = \\sum_{j=0}^{n/2-1} x_{2j} \\omega_{n/2}^{jk} + \\omega_n^k \\sum_{j=0}^{n/2-1} x_{2j+1} \\omega_{n/2}^{jk}\\]

\\[= E_k + \\omega_n^k O_k\\]

where \\(E_k\\) is the DFT of even-indexed elements and \\(O_k\\) is the DFT of odd-indexed elements.

Proof of FFT Correctness and Complexity

Correctness:

Step 1: Split the DFT sum into even and odd indices:

\\[X_k = \\sum_{j=0}^{n/2-1} x_{2j} \\omega_n^{2jk} + \\sum_{j=0}^{n/2-1} x_{2j+1} \\omega_n^{(2j+1)k}\\]

Step 2: Use \\(\\omega_n^{2jk} = (\\omega_n^2)^{jk} = \\omega_{n/2}^{jk}\\):

\\[X_k = \\sum_{j=0}^{n/2-1} x_{2j} \\omega_{n/2}^{jk} + \\omega_n^k \\sum_{j=0}^{n/2-1} x_{2j+1} \\omega_{n/2}^{jk} = E_k + \\omega_n^k O_k\\]

Step 3: For \\(k + n/2\\), use \\(\\omega_n^{k+n/2} = -\\omega_n^k\\):

\\[X_{k+n/2} = E_k - \\omega_n^k O_k\\]

Complexity:

Step 4: Let \\(T(n)\\) be the number of operations for size \\(n\\).

Step 5: Recurrence: \\(T(n) = 2T(n/2) + O(n)\\) (two half-size DFTs plus \\(n\\) combinations).

Step 6: By the Master Theorem: \\(T(n) = O(n \\log n)\\). ∎

Python Implementation

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def fft(x):
    """
    Fast Fourier Transform using Cooley-Tukey algorithm.
    
    Input: x = [x_0, x_1, ..., x_{n-1}] where n is a power of 2
    Output: X = [X_0, X_1, ..., X_{n-1}] (DFT coefficients)
    
    Notation:
    - ω_n = exp(-2πi/n) is the primitive n-th root of unity
    - X_k = Σ(j=0 to n-1) x_j * ω_n^(jk)
    """
    n = len(x)
    if n == 1:
        return x
    
    # Check if power of 2
    if n % 2 != 0:
        raise ValueError("Length must be a power of 2")
    
    # Divide
    even = fft(x[0::2])  # Even indices
    odd = fft(x[1::2])   # Odd indices
    
    # Conquer
    X = np.zeros(n, dtype=complex)
    for k in range(n // 2):
        omega = np.exp(-2j * np.pi * k / n)
        X[k] = even[k] + omega * odd[k]
        X[k + n//2] = even[k] - omega * odd[k]
    
    return X

def dft_direct(x):
    """
    Direct O(n²) computation of DFT for comparison.
    """
    n = len(x)
    X = np.zeros(n, dtype=complex)
    for k in range(n):
        for j in range(n):
            X[k] += x[j] * np.exp(-2j * np.pi * j * k / n)
    return X

3. Mathematical Biology: The Logistic Growth Model

Introduction

Population dynamics models how populations change over time. The logistic growth model extends exponential growth by incorporating carrying capacity—the maximum population size an environment can sustain.

Notation Guide

  • \\(N(t)\\) — population size at time \\(t\\)
  • \\(N_0\\) — initial population size
  • \\(r\\) — intrinsic growth rate
  • \\(K\\) — carrying capacity
  • \\(\\frac{dN}{dt}\\) — rate of change of population
The Logistic Growth Equation

\\[\\frac{dN}{dt} = rN\\left(1 - \\frac{N}{K}\\right)\\]

With initial condition \\(N(0) = N_0\\), the solution is:

\\[N(t) = \\frac{K}{1 + \\left(\\frac{K - N_0}{N_0}\\right)e^{-rt}}\\]

Solution of the Logistic Equation

Step 1: Separate variables:

\\[\\frac{dN}{N(1 - N/K)} = r \\, dt\\]

Step 2: Use partial fractions:

\\[\\frac{1}{N(1 - N/K)} = \\frac{1}{N} + \\frac{1/K}{1 - N/K} = \\frac{1}{N} + \\frac{1}{K - N}\\]

Step 3: Integrate both sides:

\\[\\int \\left(\\frac{1}{N} + \\frac{1}{K - N}\\right) dN = \\int r \\, dt\\]

\\[\\ln|N| - \\ln|K - N| = rt + C\\]

Step 4: Simplify:

\\[\\ln\\left|\\frac{N}{K - N}\\right| = rt + C\\]

Step 5: Apply initial condition \\(N(0) = N_0\\):

\\[\\frac{N_0}{K - N_0} = e^C\\]

Step 6: Solve for \\(N(t)\\):

\\[\\frac{N}{K - N} = \\frac{N_0}{K - N_0} e^{rt}\\]

\\[N = \\frac{K N_0 e^{rt}}{K - N_0 + N_0 e^{rt}} = \\frac{K}{1 + \\frac{K - N_0}{N_0}e^{-rt}}\\] ∎

Python Implementation

import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import odeint
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def logistic_growth(N, t, r, K):
    """
    Logistic growth differential equation.
    
    dN/dt = rN(1 - N/K)
    
    Notation:
    - N: population size
    - r: intrinsic growth rate
    - K: carrying capacity
    """
    return r * N * (1 - N / K)

def logistic_solution(t, N0, r, K):
    """
    Analytical solution to the logistic equation.
    
    N(t) = K / (1 + ((K - N0)/N0) * exp(-rt))
    """
    return K / (1 + ((K - N0) / N0) * np.exp(-r * t))

2. P vs NP

Introduction

The P vs NP problem asks whether every problem whose solution can be quickly verified can also be quickly solved. It is one of the seven Millennium Prize Problems.

Cook-Levin Theorem (1971)

The Boolean satisfiability problem (SAT) is \\(\\mathbf{NP}\\)-complete.

If any \\(\\mathbf{NP}\\)-complete problem is in \\(\\mathbf{P}\\), then \\(\\mathbf{P} = \\mathbf{NP}\\).

Python Implementation

import itertools

def sat_solver_brute_force(formula, num_vars):
    """
    Brute-force SAT solver (exponential time).
    
    Formula is a list of clauses, each clause is a list of literals.
    Positive integers represent variables, negative represent negation.
    
    Returns (satisfiable, assignment) or (False, None)
    """
    for assignment_bits in itertools.product([False, True], repeat=num_vars):
        assignment = {i+1: assignment_bits[i] for i in range(num_vars)}
        
        satisfied = True
        for clause in formula:
            clause_satisfied = False
            for literal in clause:
                var = abs(literal)
                value = assignment[var]
                if literal < 0:
                    value = not value
                if value:
                    clause_satisfied = True
                    break
            if not clause_satisfied:
                satisfied = False
                break
        
        if satisfied:
            return True, assignment
    
    return False, None

def p_vs_np_demo():
    """
    Demonstrate P vs NP concepts.
    """
    print("P vs NP Problem")
    print("=" * 60)
    print("P = problems solvable in polynomial time")
    print("NP = problems with solutions verifiable in polynomial time")
    print("Question: Does P = NP?")
    print("-" * 60)
    
    # Example: SAT verification is fast
    print("\nExample: SAT Verification (demonstrates NP)")
    formula = [[1, 2], [-1, 3]]  # (x₁ ∨ x₂) ∧ (¬x₁ ∨ x₃)
    assignment = {1: True, 2: False, 3: True}
    
    print("Formula: (x₁ ∨ x₂) ∧ (¬x₁ ∨ x₃)")
    print(f"Proposed solution: x₁={assignment[1]}, x₂={assignment[2]}, x₃={assignment[3]}")
    
    # Verify (polynomial time)
    is_valid = all(any(assignment[abs(l)] != (l < 0) for l in clause) for clause in formula)
    print(f"Verification result: {'Valid' if is_valid else 'Invalid'}")
    print("Verification is O(number of clauses × clause size) = POLYNOMIAL")
    
    # Solve (exponential time with brute force)
    sat, sol = sat_solver_brute_force(formula, 3)
    print(f"\nSolving result: Satisfiable = {sat}")
    print("Brute force solving is O(2^n) = EXPONENTIAL")

4. Game Theory: The Monty Hall Problem

Introduction

The Monty Hall problem is a famous probability puzzle based on a game show scenario. It's a counterintuitive result that demonstrates the importance of conditional probability.

The Monty Hall Problem

Setup: Three doors hide one car and two goats. You pick a door. The host, who knows what's behind each door, opens another door revealing a goat. Should you switch?

Solution: Switching gives you a \\(\\frac{2}{3}\\) chance of winning; staying gives only \\(\\frac{1}{3}\\).

Proof by Conditional Probability

Step 1: Let \\(C_i\\) be the event that the car is behind door \\(i\\), and \\(H_j\\) be the event that host opens door \\(j\\).

Step 2: Assume you pick door 1. Initially \\(P(C_1) = P(C_2) = P(C_3) = \\frac{1}{3}\\).

Step 3: If car is behind door 1 (you picked right), host can open door 2 or 3.

Step 4: If car is behind door 2, host must open door 3. If car is behind door 3, host must open door 2.

Step 5: Suppose host opens door 3. By Bayes' theorem:

\\[P(C_2 | H_3) = \\frac{P(H_3 | C_2)P(C_2)}{P(H_3)} = \\frac{1 \\cdot \\frac{1}{3}}{\\frac{1}{2} \\cdot \\frac{1}{3} + 1 \\cdot \\frac{1}{3} + 0 \\cdot \\frac{1}{3}} = \\frac{2}{3}\\]

Step 6: Similarly \\(P(C_1 | H_3) = \\frac{1}{3}\\). Therefore switching wins with probability \\(\\frac{2}{3}\\). ∎

Python Implementation

import random

def monty_hall_simulation(n_simulations=100000):
    """
    Simulate the Monty Hall problem.
    
    Demonstrates that switching wins 2/3 of the time.
    """
    wins_if_switch = 0
    wins_if_stay = 0
    
    for _ in range(n_simulations):
        # Place car behind random door
        car_door = random.randint(0, 2)
        
        # Contestant picks a door
        contestant_pick = random.randint(0, 2)
        
        # Host opens a door with a goat
        available_doors = [d for d in range(3) 
                          if d != contestant_pick and d != car_door]
        host_opens = random.choice(available_doors)
        
        # Switch: pick the other unopened door
        switch_pick = [d for d in range(3) 
                      if d != contestant_pick and d != host_opens][0]
        
        # Count wins
        if switch_pick == car_door:
            wins_if_switch += 1
        if contestant_pick == car_door:
            wins_if_stay += 1
    
    print("Monty Hall Problem Simulation")
    print("=" * 60)
    print(f"Simulations: {n_simulations:,}")
    print(f"Wins if switching: {wins_if_switch:,} ({wins_if_switch/n_simulations:.4f})")
    print(f"Wins if staying: {wins_if_stay:,} ({wins_if_stay/n_simulations:.4f})")
    print(f"\nTheoretical probabilities: Switch = 2/3 ≈ 0.6667, Stay = 1/3 ≈ 0.3333")
    print(f"Simulation matches theory: {abs(wins_if_switch/n_simulations - 2/3) < 0.01}")
