Lie Algebras: The Infinitesimal Structure of Symmetry

Motivation

Lie algebras capture the local, infinitesimal structure of continuous symmetry groups. While Lie groups describe continuous symmetries globally, their associated Lie algebras linearize these symmetries near the identity, making them amenable to linear algebra techniques. This correspondence between Lie groups and Lie algebras, established by Sophus Lie in the late 19th century, revolutionized our understanding of symmetry in mathematics and physics. Lie algebras appear in the classification of elementary particles, the study of differential equations, and the geometry of homogeneous spaces.

Definitions

Lie Algebra: A vector space \( \mathfrak{g} \) over a field \( \mathbb{F} \) equipped with a bilinear operation \( [\cdot, \cdot]: \mathfrak{g} \times \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{g} \) called the Lie bracket, satisfying:
1. Antisymmetry: \( [X, Y] = -[Y, X] \)
2. Jacobi identity: \( [X, [Y, Z]] + [Y, [Z, X]] + [Z, [X, Y]] = 0 \)

Adjoint Representation: For \( X \in \mathfrak{g} \), the map \( \text{ad}_X: \mathfrak{g} \to \mathfrak{g} \) defined by \( \text{ad}_X(Y) = [X, Y] \).

Killing Form: The symmetric bilinear form \( B(X, Y) = \text{tr}(\text{ad}_X \circ \text{ad}_Y) \).

Theorem: Cartan's Criterion for Semisimplicity

A finite-dimensional Lie algebra \( \mathfrak{g} \) over a field of characteristic zero is semisimple if and only if its Killing form is non-degenerate.

Proof Sketch: The proof proceeds in two directions. First, if \( \mathfrak{g} \) is semisimple (direct sum of simple ideals), one shows that the radical of the Killing form is a solvable ideal, which must be zero. Conversely, if the Killing form is non-degenerate, one proves that any abelian ideal must be contained in the radical of \( B \), hence zero. The key insight is that the Killing form encodes the algebraic structure of \( \mathfrak{g} \) through the adjoint representation, and its non-degeneracy forces the algebra to be "sufficiently non-commutative" to prevent solvable ideals.

Notation Guide

  • \( \mathfrak{g} \) — Lie algebra (fraktur g)
  • \( [X, Y] \) — Lie bracket of X and Y
  • \( \text{ad}_X \) — adjoint action of X
  • \( B(X, Y) \) — Killing form
  • \( \mathfrak{sl}(n) \) — special linear Lie algebra (traceless matrices)
  • \( \mathfrak{so}(n) \) — special orthogonal Lie algebra (skew-symmetric matrices)

import numpy as np
from itertools import combinations

class LieAlgebra:
    """Basic operations for matrix Lie algebras."""
    
    @staticmethod
    def lie_bracket(X, Y):
        """Compute [X, Y] = XY - YX for matrices X, Y."""
        return X @ Y - Y @ X
    
    @staticmethod
    def is_abelian(basis):
        """Check if Lie algebra with given basis is abelian."""
        for X, Y in combinations(basis, 2):
            if not np.allclose(LieAlgebra.lie_bracket(X, Y), 0):
                return False
        return True
    
    @staticmethod
    def ad(X, basis):
        """Compute matrix of ad_X in given basis."""
        n = len(basis)
        ad_matrix = np.zeros((n, n))
        for j, Y in enumerate(basis):
            bracket = LieAlgebra.lie_bracket(X, Y)
            # Express bracket in basis
            for i, Z in enumerate(basis):
                ad_matrix[i, j] = np.trace(bracket.T @ Z) / np.trace(Z.T @ Z)
        return ad_matrix
    
    @staticmethod
    def killing_form(X, Y, basis):
        """Compute Killing form B(X, Y) = tr(ad_X ∘ ad_Y)."""
        ad_X = LieAlgebra.ad(X, basis)
        ad_Y = LieAlgebra.ad(Y, basis)
        return np.trace(ad_X @ ad_Y)

Ergodic Theory: The Mathematics of Long-Term Behavior

Motivation

Ergodic theory studies dynamical systems from a statistical perspective, focusing on the long-term behavior of systems and their invariant measures. Originating in statistical mechanics through the work of Boltzmann and Gibbs, ergodic theory has grown into a rich field connecting dynamical systems, probability theory, number theory, and harmonic analysis. It provides the mathematical foundation for understanding how time averages relate to space averages, with applications ranging from celestial mechanics to information theory.

Definitions

Measure-Preserving System: A quadruple \( (X, \mathcal{B}, \mu, T) \) where \( (X, \mathcal{B}, \mu) \) is a probability space and \( T: X \to X \) is a measurable map with \( \mu(T^{-1}(A)) = \mu(A) \) for all \( A \in \mathcal{B} \).

Ergodicity: A measure-preserving system is ergodic if every invariant measurable set has measure 0 or 1. Equivalently, the only invariant functions are constant almost everywhere.

Time Average: For \( f \in L^1(\mu) \), the time average is \( \lim_{N \to \infty} \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} f(T^n x) \).

Theorem: Birkhoff's Ergodic Theorem

Let \( (X, \mathcal{B}, \mu, T) \) be a measure-preserving system and \( f \in L^1(\mu) \). Then the time average exists for \( \mu \)-almost every \( x \): \[\tilde{f}(x) = \lim_{N \to \infty} \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} f(T^n x)\] Moreover, \( \tilde{f} \in L^1(\mu) \), \( \tilde{f} \circ T = \tilde{f} \) almost everywhere, and \( \int \tilde{f} \, d\mu = \int f \, d\mu \).

Proof Sketch: The proof uses the maximal ergodic theorem and a stopping time argument. Define the maximal function \( f^*(x) = \sup_{N \geq 1} \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} f(T^n x) \). One shows that for any \( \lambda > 0 \), \( \mu(\{f^* > \lambda\}) \leq \frac{1}{\lambda} \int_{f^* > \lambda} f \, d\mu \). This maximal inequality controls the oscillations of the averages, allowing one to prove convergence using a density argument. The limit function is invariant by construction, and the preservation of integrals follows from the dominated convergence theorem.

Notation Guide

  • \( (X, \mathcal{B}, \mu) \) — probability space
  • \( T: X \to X \) — measure-preserving transformation
  • \( T^n \) — n-fold composition of T
  • \( L^1(\mu) \) — space of integrable functions
  • \( \tilde{f} \) — time average limit function
  • \( \int f \, d\mu \) — space average (integral)

Arithmetic Geometry: Where Number Theory Meets Geometry

Motivation

Arithmetic geometry studies solutions to polynomial equations over number fields and finite fields, blending techniques from algebraic geometry with the arithmetic of number theory. This field, pioneered by Weil, Grothendieck, and Deligne, has led to proofs of some of the deepest results in mathematics, including Fermat's Last Theorem and the Weil conjectures. The central insight is that geometric properties of algebraic varieties encode profound arithmetic information.

Definitions

Affine Variety: For a field \( k \) and polynomials \( f_1, \ldots, f_m \in k[x_1, \ldots, x_n] \), the set \( V = \{x \in \overline{k}^n : f_i(x) = 0 \text{ for all } i\} \).

Rational Points: The set \( V(k) = V \cap k^n \) of solutions with coordinates in \( k \).

Hasse Principle: A variety has a rational point over \( \mathbb{Q} \) if and only if it has points over \( \mathbb{R} \) and over \( \mathbb{Q}_p \) for all primes \( p \).

Theorem: Mordell's Theorem (for Elliptic Curves)

Let \( E \) be an elliptic curve over \( \mathbb{Q} \). Then the group of rational points \( E(\mathbb{Q}) \) is a finitely generated abelian group: \[E(\mathbb{Q}) \cong \mathbb{Z}^r \oplus E(\mathbb{Q})_{\text{tors}}\] where \( r \geq 0 \) is the rank and \( E(\mathbb{Q})_{\text{tors}} \) is the finite torsion subgroup.

Proof Sketch: The proof uses the method of infinite descent. One defines a height function \( h: E(\mathbb{Q}) \to \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0} \) measuring the complexity of points. Key steps: (1) Show there are only finitely many points of bounded height. (2) Prove the descent lemma: for any point \( P \), there exists a finite set of "smaller" points such that \( P \) is in the group they generate. (3) Combine these to show \( E(\mathbb{Q}) \) is generated by a finite set. The weak Mordell-Weil theorem (finite index of \( 2E(\mathbb{Q}) \)) provides the foundation for the descent.

Notation Guide

  • \( V(k) \) — k-rational points of variety V
  • \( \overline{k} \) — algebraic closure of field k
  • \( \mathbb{Q}_p \) — p-adic numbers
  • \( E(\mathbb{Q}) \) — rational points on elliptic curve E
  • \( r \) — rank of elliptic curve
  • \( E[n] \) — n-torsion points

import numpy as np
from fractions import Fraction

class ArithmeticGeometry:
    """Arithmetic geometry computations for elliptic curves."""
    
    def __init__(self, a, b):
        """Initialize elliptic curve y^2 = x^3 + ax + b."""
        self.a = a
        self.b = b
        # Check discriminant
        discriminant = -16 * (4 * a**3 + 27 * b**2)
        if discriminant == 0:
            raise ValueError("Curve is singular")
    
    def is_on_curve(self, x, y):
        """Check if point (x, y) is on the curve."""
        if x is None and y is None:  # Point at infinity
            return True
        return y**2 == x**3 + self.a * x + self.b
    
    def add_points(self, P, Q):
        """Add two points on the elliptic curve."""
        # Point at infinity is identity
        if P == (None, None):
            return Q
        if Q == (None, None):
            return P
        
        x1, y1 = P
        x2, y2 = Q
        
        # Handle case P = -Q
        if x1 == x2 and y1 == -y2:
            return (None, None)
        
        # Compute slope
        if P == Q:
            # Tangent slope: (3x^2 + a) / (2y)
            if y1 == 0:
                return (None, None)
            m = (3 * x1**2 + self.a) / (2 * y1)
        else:
            # Secant slope: (y2 - y1) / (x2 - x1)
            m = (y2 - y1) / (x2 - x1)
        
        # Compute new point
        x3 = m**2 - x1 - x2
        y3 = m * (x1 - x3) - y1
        
        return (x3, y3)
    
    def scalar_mult(self, n, P):
        """Compute n*P using double-and-add."""
        if n == 0:
            return (None, None)
        if n < 0:
            x, y = P
            P = (x, -y) if x is not None else (None, None)
            n = -n
        
        result = (None, None)
        addend = P
        
        while n > 0:
            if n & 1:
                result = self.add_points(result, addend)
            addend = self.add_points(addend, addend)
            n >>= 1
        
        return result
    
    def rational_points_bounded(self, bound):
        """Find rational points with bounded denominator."""
        points = [(None, None)]  # Point at infinity
        
        for q in range(1, bound + 1):
            for p in range(-bound * q, bound * q + 1):
                x = Fraction(p, q)
                y_sq = x**3 + self.a * x + self.b
                
                # Check if y_sq is a perfect square
                if y_sq >= 0:
                    y_float = float(y_sq) ** 0.5
                    if abs(y_float - round(y_float)) < 1e-10:
                        y = Fraction(int(round(y_float)))
                        if y**2 == y_sq:
                            points.append((x, y))
                            if y != 0:
                                points.append((x, -y))
        
        return points

Post-Quantum Cryptography: Securing the Future

Real-World Motivation

The advent of quantum computers poses an existential threat to current cryptographic systems. Shor's algorithm can factor integers and compute discrete logarithms in polynomial time on a quantum computer, breaking RSA and elliptic curve cryptography. Post-quantum cryptography develops algorithms secure against both classical and quantum attacks, with NIST having recently standardized the first set of quantum-resistant algorithms including CRYSTALS-Kyber and CRYSTALS-Dilithium.

Definitions

Lattice: For linearly independent vectors \( \mathbf{b}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{b}_n \in \mathbb{R}^n \), the lattice is \( \mathcal{L} = \{\sum_{i=1}^{n} z_i \mathbf{b}_i : z_i \in \mathbb{Z}\} \).

Shortest Vector Problem (SVP): Given a lattice basis, find the shortest non-zero vector in the lattice. This problem is believed to be hard even for quantum computers.

Learning With Errors (LWE): Given a matrix \( A \) and vector \( \mathbf{b} = A\mathbf{s} + \mathbf{e} \) where \( \mathbf{e} \) is a small error vector, recover the secret \( \mathbf{s} \).

Theorem: Regev's Reduction

Solving the LWE problem with polynomial modulus is at least as hard as quantumly approximating the shortest vector problem on any lattice to within \( \tilde{O}(n) \) factors.

Proof Sketch: The proof constructs a quantum algorithm that uses an LWE oracle to solve approximate SVP. The key insight is that LWE samples can be viewed as "noisy" versions of lattice coset representatives. The quantum component uses the LWE oracle to perform a form of "quantum sampling" that reveals information about the lattice structure. By carefully combining classical preprocessing with quantum Fourier sampling over the dual lattice, one can extract short vectors. The polynomial modulus ensures the reduction is tight enough for cryptographic applications.

Notation Guide

  • \( \mathcal{L} \) — lattice
  • \( \mathbf{b}_i \) — basis vectors
  • \( \lambda_1(\mathcal{L}) \) — length of shortest non-zero vector
  • \( A \) — public matrix (LWE)
  • \( \mathbf{s} \) — secret vector
  • \( \mathbf{e} \) — error vector
  • \( q \) — modulus

import numpy as np
from numpy.polynomial import polynomial as P

class LatticeCrypto:
    """Simplified lattice-based cryptography demonstrations."""
    
    def __init__(self, n, q, sigma=3.0):
        """
        n: dimension
        q: modulus
        sigma: error distribution parameter
        """
        self.n = n
        self.q = q
        self.sigma = sigma
    
    def sample_error(self):
        """Sample from discrete Gaussian (approximated)."""
        return int(np.round(np.random.normal(0, self.sigma)))
    
    def generate_keypair(self):
        """Generate LWE keypair."""
        # Secret key: small random vector
        s = np.random.randint(0, 3, self.n) - 1  # {-1, 0, 1}
        
        # Public key: A, b = As + e
        A = np.random.randint(0, self.q, (self.n, self.n))
        e = np.array([self.sample_error() for _ in range(self.n)])
        b = (A @ s + e) % self.q
        
        return (s, (A, b))
    
    def encrypt_bit(self, public_key, bit):
        """Encrypt a single bit using LWE."""
        A, b = public_key
        
        # Sample random subset
        r = np.random.randint(0, 2, self.n)
        
        # Compute ciphertext
        u = (r @ A) % self.q
        v = (r @ b + bit * (self.q // 2)) % self.q
        
        return (u, v)
    
    def decrypt_bit(self, secret_key, ciphertext):
        """Decrypt a single bit."""
        u, v = ciphertext
        
        # Compute v - u·s
        decrypted = (v - u @ secret_key) % self.q
        
        # Decode: 0 if close to 0, 1 if close to q/2
        if decrypted > self.q // 2:
            decrypted -= self.q
        
        return 0 if abs(decrypted) < self.q // 4 else 1
    
    @staticmethod
    def gram_schmidt(B):
        """Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization for basis B."""
        n = len(B)
        B_star = np.zeros_like(B, dtype=float)
        mu = np.zeros((n, n))
        
        for i in range(n):
            B_star[i] = B[i].astype(float)
            for j in range(i):
                mu[i, j] = np.dot(B[i], B_star[j]) / np.dot(B_star[j], B_star[j])
                B_star[i] -= mu[i, j] * B_star[j]
        
        return B_star, mu
    
    @staticmethod
    def babai_rounding(B, t):
        """Babai's rounding algorithm for CVP."""
        # Solve B * x = t in reals
        x = np.linalg.solve(B.astype(float), t.astype(float))
        # Round to nearest integers
        return np.round(x).astype(int)

Neural Network Theory: The Mathematics of Deep Learning

Real-World Motivation

Deep learning has revolutionized artificial intelligence, but understanding why neural networks work so well remains a mathematical challenge. The universal approximation theorem guarantees that neural networks can represent any continuous function, but questions about optimization, generalization, and the geometry of loss landscapes drive current research. This field draws on differential geometry, probability theory, and statistical physics to explain the empirical success of deep learning.

Definitions

Neural Network: A function \( f_\theta: \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^k \) defined by compositional layers: \[f_\theta(x) = W_L \sigma(W_{L-1} \sigma(\cdots \sigma(W_1 x + b_1) \cdots) + b_{L-1}) + b_L\] where \( \sigma \) is an activation function.

Loss Landscape: The function \( L: \Theta \to \mathbb{R} \) mapping parameters to empirical risk.

Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK): In the infinite-width limit, the kernel \( K(x, x') = \mathbb{E}[\langle \nabla_\theta f_\theta(x), \nabla_\theta f_\theta(x') \rangle] \).

Theorem: Universal Approximation

Let \( \sigma: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R} \) be a non-constant, bounded, continuous function. For any compact set \( K \subset \mathbb{R}^d \), any continuous function \( f: K \to \mathbb{R} \), and any \( \epsilon > 0 \), there exists a neural network \( f_\theta \) with one hidden layer such that: \[\sup_{x \in K} |f(x) - f_\theta(x)| < \epsilon\]

Proof Sketch: The proof uses the Hahn-Banach theorem and the Riesz representation theorem. First, one shows that the span of functions \( x \mapsto \sigma(w^T x + b) \) is dense in \( C(K) \). This follows because if a signed measure \( \mu \) annihilates all such functions, then the Laplace transform of \( \mu \) vanishes, implying \( \mu = 0 \). The Stone-Weierstrass theorem provides an alternative approach: the set of neural network functions forms an algebra that separates points and contains constants, hence is dense. The key insight is that even simple activation functions combined linearly can approximate complex functions arbitrarily well.

                grad2 = self._compute_gradient(x2.reshape(1, -1))

                ntk[i, j] = np.dot(grad1, grad2)

        self.forward(X)
        
        # Backward pass

            gradients.extend([grad_w.flatten(), grad_b])
            
            if i > 0:
                delta = delta @ self.weights[i].T
        
        return np.concatenate([g.flatten() for g in gradients])

Operations Research: Optimization in Action

Real-World Motivation

Operations research applies mathematical modeling and optimization to decision-making problems in business, engineering, and public policy. From scheduling airline crews to optimizing supply chains, OR techniques save billions of dollars annually. The field combines linear programming, network flows, integer programming, and stochastic modeling to find optimal or near-optimal solutions to complex real-world problems.

Definitions

Linear Program: An optimization problem of the form: \[\min_{x} \{c^T x : Ax \leq b, x \geq 0\}\]

Dual Problem: For the primal LP above, the dual is: \[\max_{y} \{b^T y : A^T y \geq c, y \geq 0\}\]

Simplex Method: An algorithm that moves along edges of the feasible polytope from vertex to vertex, improving the objective at each step.

Theorem: Strong Duality

If the primal linear program has an optimal solution \( x^* \), then the dual has an optimal solution \( y^* \) and: \[c^T x^* = b^T y^*\] Moreover, the optimal values satisfy the complementary slackness conditions.

Proof Sketch: The proof relies on Farkas' lemma, which states that exactly one of the following holds: either \( Ax = b, x \geq 0 \) has a solution, or \( y^T A \geq 0, y^T b < 0 \) has a solution. Applying this to the optimality conditions shows that if the primal is feasible and bounded, the dual must have a feasible solution achieving the same value. The key insight is that the dual variables can be interpreted as "prices" for the constraints, and strong duality says these prices exactly account for the optimal value. The simplex method naturally produces dual-optimal solutions through its reduced costs.

Notation Guide

  • \( c \) — cost vector (objective coefficients)
  • \( A \) — constraint matrix
  • \( b \) — right-hand side vector
  • \( x \) — primal decision variables
  • \( y \) — dual variables (shadow prices)
  • \( z^* \) — optimal objective value

import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import linprog

class OperationsResearch:
    """Operations research optimization methods."""
    
    @staticmethod
    def simplex_step(A, b, c, basic_vars):
        """Perform one iteration of the simplex method."""
        m, n = A.shape
        
        # Extract basis
        B = A[:, basic_vars]
        c_B = c[basic_vars]
        
        # Compute reduced costs
        B_inv = np.linalg.inv(B)
        y = c_B @ B_inv
        reduced_costs = c - y @ A
        
        # Check optimality
        if np.all(reduced_costs >= -1e-10):
            return None, None, True  # Optimal
        
        # Entering variable (most negative reduced cost)
        entering = np.argmin(reduced_costs)
        
        # Compute direction
        d = B_inv @ A[:, entering]
        
        # Check unboundedness
        if np.all(d <= 0):
            return None, None, False  # Unbounded
        
        # Leaving variable (minimum ratio test)
        x_B = B_inv @ b
        ratios = np.where(d > 0, x_B / d, np.inf)
        leaving_idx = np.argmin(ratios)
        leaving = basic_vars[leaving_idx]
        
        # Update basis
        new_basic = basic_vars.copy()
        new_basic[leaving_idx] = entering
        
        return new_basic, reduced_costs[entering], None
    
    @staticmethod
    def transport_problem(costs, supply, demand):
        """
        Solve transportation problem using Northwest Corner Rule + optimization.
        costs: m x n matrix of transportation costs
        supply: m-vector of supplies
        demand: n-vector of demands
        """
        m, n = costs.shape
        
        # Check balance
        if abs(sum(supply) - sum(demand)) > 1e-10:
            raise ValueError("Supply and demand must balance")
        
        # Flatten for linear programming
        c = costs.flatten()
        
        # Supply constraints: sum over destinations for each source
        A_supply = np.zeros((m, m * n))
        for i in range(m):
            A_supply[i, i*n:(i+1)*n] = 1
        
        # Demand constraints: sum over sources for each destination
        A_demand = np.zeros((n, m * n))
        for j in range(n):
            for i in range(m):
                A_demand[j, i*n + j] = 1
        
        A_eq = np.vstack([A_supply, A_demand])
        b_eq = np.concatenate([supply, demand])
        
        # Solve
        result = linprog(c, A_eq=A_eq, b_eq=b_eq, bounds=(0, None), method='highs')
        
        if result.success:
            return result.x.reshape(m, n), result.fun
        else:
            return None, None
    
    @staticmethod
    def knapsack_dp(values, weights, capacity):
        """Solve 0/1 knapsack using dynamic programming."""
        n = len(values)
        
        # DP table
        dp = np.zeros((n + 1, capacity + 1))
        
        for i in range(1, n + 1):
            for w in range(capacity + 1):
                if weights[i-1] <= w:
                    dp[i, w] = max(dp[i-1, w], 
                                   dp[i-1, w - weights[i-1]] + values[i-1])
                else:
                    dp[i, w] = dp[i-1, w]
        
        # Backtrack to find selected items
        selected = []
        w = capacity
        for i in range(n, 0, -1):
            if dp[i, w] != dp[i-1, w]:
                selected.append(i-1)
                w -= weights[i-1]
        
        return dp[n, capacity], selected[::-1]

Type Theory: Foundations for Mathematics and Computation

Historical Context

Type theory emerged from Bertrand Russell's efforts to resolve the paradoxes in naive set theory, particularly his eponymous paradox. Russell introduced the theory of types in 1908 to prevent self-referential constructions. In the 1930s, Alonzo Church developed the simply typed lambda calculus, and in the 1970s, Per Martin-Löf created constructive type theory as a foundation for mathematics. Today, type theory powers proof assistants like Coq and Lean, which are being used to formally verify mathematical proofs and software systems.

Definitions

Simple Type: Types built from base types and the function type constructor \( \to \). If \( A \) and \( B \) are types, so is \( A \to B \).

Dependent Type: A type that depends on a value, written as \( \Pi_{x:A} B(x) \) for dependent functions or \( \Sigma_{x:A} B(x) \) for dependent pairs.

Curry-Howard Correspondence: The isomorphism between propositions and types, where proofs correspond to programs and proposition verification corresponds to type checking.

Theorem: Normalization of Simply Typed Lambda Calculus

Every well-typed term in the simply typed lambda calculus has a unique normal form, and there exists a terminating reduction strategy to reach it.

Proof Sketch: The proof uses a logical relations argument. Define a reducibility predicate \( \text{Red}_A(t) \) by induction on types: for base types, terms are reducible if they reduce to a normal form; for function types, \( t: A \to B \) is reducible if for all reducible \( s: A \), the application \( t(s) \) is reducible in \( B \). One proves that all well-typed terms are reducible, and reducible terms normalize. The key insight is that the logical relation captures exactly the computational behavior needed for normalization, and the structure of types ensures the argument proceeds by well-founded induction.

Notation Guide

  • \( A \to B \) — function type
  • \( \Pi_{x:A} B(x) \) — dependent product (universal quantification)
  • \( \Sigma_{x:A} B(x) \) — dependent sum (existential quantification)
  • \( \lambda x: A. t \) — lambda abstraction
  • \( t(s) \) — function application
  • \( \Gamma \vdash t : A \) — term t has type A in context Γ

class TypeChecker:
    """Simple type checker for simply typed lambda calculus."""
    
    def __init__(self):
        self.context: Dict[str, Type] = {}
    
    def type_check(self, term: Term) -> Optional[Type]:
        """Infer the type of a term."""
        if isinstance(term, Var):
            if term.name in self.context:
                return self.context[term.name]
            raise TypeError(f"Unbound variable: {term.name}")
        
        elif isinstance(term, Lambda):
            # Extend context with bound variable
            old_context = self.context.copy()
            self.context[term.var] = term.var_type
            
            body_type = self.type_check(term.body)
            
            # Restore context
            self.context = old_context
            
            return ArrowType(term.var_type, body_type)
        
        elif isinstance(term, App):
            func_type = self.type_check(term.func)
            arg_type = self.type_check(term.arg)
            
            if not isinstance(func_type, ArrowType):
                raise TypeError(f"Expected function type, got {func_type}")
            
            if func_type.domain != arg_type:
                raise TypeError(f"Type mismatch: expected {func_type.domain}, got {arg_type}")
            
            return func_type.codomain
        
        return None
    
    def beta_reduce(self, term: Term) -> Term:
        """Perform one step of beta reduction."""
        if isinstance(term, Var):
            return term
        
        elif isinstance(term, Lambda):
            return Lambda(term.var, term.var_type, self.beta_reduce(term.body))
        
        elif isinstance(term, App):
            func = self.beta_reduce(term.func)
            arg = self.beta_reduce(term.arg)
            
            if isinstance(func, Lambda):
                # Beta reduction: (λx.M) N -> M[x:=N]
                return self.substitute(func.body, func.var, arg)
            
            return App(func, arg)
        
        return term
    
    def substitute(self, term: Term, var: str, replacement: Term) -> Term:
        """Substitute replacement for var in term."""
        if isinstance(term, Var):
            if term.name == var:
                return replacement
            return term
        
        elif isinstance(term, Lambda):
            if term.var == var:
                return term  # Variable is bound
            return Lambda(term.var, term.var_type, 
                         self.substitute(term.body, var, replacement))
        
        elif isinstance(term, App):
            return App(self.substitute(term.func, var, replacement),
                      self.substitute(term.arg, var, replacement))
        
        return term
    
    def normalize(self, term: Term, max_steps: int = 100) -> Term:
        """Normalize a term through repeated beta reduction."""
        current = term
        for _ in range(max_steps):
            reduced = self.beta_reduce(current)
            if reduced == current:
                return current
            current = reduced
        return current

Descriptive Set Theory: Definable Subsets of the Continuum

Historical Context

Descriptive set theory emerged from the work of French analysts Borel, Baire, and Lebesgue at the turn of the 20th century, who sought to understand which subsets of the real line could be "described" or "constructed." The field was revolutionized by the Polish school (Sierpinski, Kuratowski, Tarski) and later by the introduction of effective methods through computability theory. Today, descriptive set theory connects topology, logic, and analysis, with applications to dynamical systems, functional analysis, and model theory.

Definitions

Borel Hierarchy: The classification of Borel sets into classes \( \Sigma^0_\alpha \) and \( \Pi^0_\alpha \) for countable ordinals \( \alpha \), where \( \Sigma^0_1 \) are open sets, \( \Pi^0_1 \) are closed sets, and higher levels are formed by countable unions and intersections.

Analytic Set (\( \Sigma^1_1 \)): The continuous image of a Borel set, equivalently the projection of a Borel subset of \( \mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{R} \).

Perfect Set: A closed set with no isolated points.

Theorem: Perfect Set Theorem for Analytic Sets

Every uncountable analytic subset of a Polish space contains a perfect subset.

Proof Sketch: The proof uses the fact that analytic sets are Suslin sets—continuous images of the Baire space. If \( A \) is an uncountable analytic set, consider the tree representation of the underlying closed set in Baire space. The uncountability of \( A \) implies this tree has a "perfect kernel"—an infinite subtree where every node splits. The image of this perfect kernel under the continuous map is a perfect subset of \( A \). This result is significant because it shows analytic sets cannot be counterexamples to the continuum hypothesis: every uncountable analytic set has cardinality \( 2^{\aleph_0} \).

Notation Guide

  • \( \Sigma^0_\alpha \) — Borel sets at level α (countable unions)
  • \( \Pi^0_\alpha \) — Borel sets at level α (countable intersections)
  • \( \Sigma^1_1 \) — analytic sets
  • \( \Pi^1_1 \) — co-analytic sets
  • \( \omega^\omega \) — Baire space (sequences of natural numbers)
  • \( \mathcal{N} \) — Baire space notation

class DescriptiveSetTheory:
    """Computational aspects of descriptive set theory."""
    
    @staticmethod
    def is_open_interval(a: float, b: float, x: float, eps: float = 1e-10) -> bool:
        """Check if x is in open interval (a, b)."""
        return a + eps < x < b - eps
    
    @staticmethod
    def is_closed_interval(a: float, b: float, x: float, eps: float = 1e-10) -> bool:
        """Check if x is in closed interval [a, b]."""
        return a - eps <= x <= b + eps
    
    @staticmethod
    def cantor_set_membership(x: float, max_iter: int = 50) -> bool:
        """
        Check if x is in the Cantor set.
        The Cantor set consists of numbers in [0,1] with no '1' in base 3.
        """
        if x < 0 or x > 1:
            return False
        
        # Check base-3 expansion
        for _ in range(max_iter):
            x *= 3
            digit = int(x)
            if digit == 1:

Constructive Mathematics: Proofs as Programs

Historical Context

Constructive mathematics emerged from foundational debates in the early 20th century, particularly through the work of L.E.J. Brouwer's intuitionism and later Errett Bishop's constructive analysis. Rejecting the law of excluded middle for infinite sets, constructive mathematics requires that proofs of existence provide explicit constructions. This philosophy found unexpected practical application in computer science through the Curry-Howard correspondence and the development of proof assistants. Today, constructive methods inform type theory, programming language design, and computable analysis.

Definitions

Constructive Proof: A proof that provides an explicit algorithm or construction for any existential statement it establishes.

Realizability: A semantics where proofs are realized by computational procedures; a statement is true if there exists a realizer that computes the content of the proof.

Choice Sequence: In intuitionistic analysis, an infinitely proceeding sequence of natural numbers that may be undetermined or "free" at certain stages.

Theorem: Intermediate Value Theorem (Constructive Version)

Let \( f: [0, 1] \to \mathbb{R} \) be a uniformly continuous function with \( f(0) < 0 \) and \( f(1) > 0 \). Then for every \( \epsilon > 0 \), there exists \( x \in [0, 1] \) such that \( |f(x)| < \epsilon \).

Proof Sketch: The classical IVT asserts existence of a root, which constructively requires additional hypotheses (e.g., f is locally non-zero). The approximate version is constructively valid: using uniform continuity, find \( \delta \) such that \( |x - y| < \delta \) implies \( |f(x) - f(y)| < \epsilon/2 \). Perform a binary search, maintaining the invariant that f changes sign on the current interval. After \( O(\log(1/\delta)) \) steps, the interval length is less than \( \delta \), and the midpoint satisfies \( |f(x)| < \epsilon \). The algorithm provides the witness explicitly, satisfying constructive requirements.

Notation Guide

  • \( \forall x \) — universal quantification (must hold computably)
  • \( \exists x \) — existential quantification (witness must be computable)
  • \( A \lor B \) — disjunction (must specify which holds)
  • \( \neg A \) — negation (A implies contradiction)
  • \( a =_\epsilon b \) — approximate equality (|a - b| < ε)

import numpy as np
from typing import Callable, Tuple, Optional

class ConstructiveMathematics:
    """Constructive mathematics demonstrations."""
    
    @staticmethod
    def bisection_method(f: Callable[[float], float],
                        a: float, 
                        b: float, 
                        epsilon: float,
                        max_iter: int = 1000) -> Optional[Tuple[float, float]]:
        """
        Constructive approximation of root using bisection.
        Returns (x, delta) such that |f(x)| < epsilon.
        """
        # Verify preconditions constructively
        fa, fb = f(a), f(b)
        
        # Need sign change for constructive proof
        if fa * fb > 0:
            return None  # Cannot guarantee root exists
        
        # Ensure f(a) < 0 < f(b) or vice versa
        if fa > 0:
            a, b = b, a
            fa, fb = fb, fa
        
        for i in range(max_iter):
            mid = (a + b) / 2
            fmid = f(mid)
            
            # Check if we have approximate root
            if abs(fmid) < epsilon:
                return (mid, abs(b - a))
            
            # Maintain invariant: f(a) < 0 < f(b)
            if fmid < 0:
                a = mid
                fa = fmid
            else:
                b = mid
                fb = fmid
        
        # Return best approximation
        return ((a + b) / 2, abs(b - a))
    
    @staticmethod
    def constructive_sqrt(n: float, epsilon: float) -> float:
        """
        Constructive square root using Newton's method.
        Returns x such that |x^2 - n| < epsilon.
        """
        if n < 0:
            raise ValueError("Square root of negative number")
        
        if n == 0:
            return 0.0
        
        x = n  # Initial guess
        while abs(x * x - n) >= epsilon:
            x = (x + n / x) / 2
        
        return x
    
    @staticmethod
    def constructive_maximum(f: Callable[[float], float],
                            a: float,
                            b: float,
                            epsilon: float,
                            n_samples: int = 1000) -> Tuple[float, float]:
        """
        Constructive approximation of maximum.
        Returns (x, fx) where f(x) is within epsilon of true max.
        """
        # Uniform sampling (constructive when f is uniformly continuous)
        samples = np.linspace(a, b, n_samples)
        
        max_val = float('-inf')
        max_x = a
        
        for x in samples:
            fx = f(x)
            if fx > max_val:
                max_val = fx
                max_x = x
        
        # Return with error bound based on modulus of continuity
        return (max_x, max_val)
    
    @staticmethod
    def approximate_equality(a: float, b: float, epsilon: float) -> bool:
        """Constructive approximate equality: |a - b| < epsilon."""
        return abs(a - b) < epsilon
    
    @staticmethod
    def constructive_intermediate_value(f: Callable[[float], float],
                                       a: float,
                                       b: float,
                                       epsilon: float) -> Optional[float]:
        """
        Constructive intermediate value theorem.
        Returns x such that |f(x)| < epsilon, if sign change exists.
        """
        fa, fb = f(a), f(b)
        
        # Classical IVT requires f(a)*f(b) < 0
        # Constructive version needs stronger condition
        if fa > 0 and fb > 0:
            return None
        if fa < 0 and fb < 0:
            return None
        
        # Use bisection to find approximate root
        result = ConstructiveMathematics.bisection_method(f, a, b, epsilon)
        return result[0] if result else None

Topos Theory: Generalized Spaces and Logic

Historical Context

Topos theory was created by Alexander Grothendieck in the 1960s as a tool for algebraic geometry, generalizing the notion of topological space. In the 1970s, William Lawvere and Myles Tierney realized that Grothendieck's definition captured the essence of set-theoretic mathematics, leading to elementary topos theory. A topos provides a universe of sets with its own internal logic—intuitionistic rather than classical. This has profound implications: every topos has its own notion of truth, and geometric morphisms between topoi translate mathematics across different foundational contexts.

Definitions

Topos: A category \( \mathcal{E} \) with: (1) finite limits, (2) exponentials, (3) a subobject classifier \( \Omega \).

Subobject Classifier: An object \( \Omega \) with a morphism \( \text{true}: 1 \to \Omega \) such that every monomorphism \( m: A \to B \) is a pullback of \( \text{true} \) along a unique \( \chi_m: B \to \Omega \).

Sheaf: On a topological space \( X \), a contravariant functor from the category of open sets to sets satisfying a gluing condition.

Theorem: Giraud's Theorem

A category is a Grothendieck topos if and only if it satisfies:
1. It has finite limits
2. It has small coproducts that are disjoint and stable under pullback
3. Equivalence relations are effective
4. It has a small set of generators

Proof Sketch: The forward direction follows from properties of sheaf categories. For the converse, given a category satisfying Giraud's axioms, one constructs a site (category with a Grothendieck topology) such that the category is equivalent to the category of sheaves on that site. The key construction uses the generators to build a site whose objects are the generators, with coverings derived from the colimit structure. The effectiveness of equivalence relations ensures that the category "behaves like" a category of sheaves, with quotients corresponding to sheafification of equivalence relations.

Notation Guide

  • \( \mathcal{E} \) — topos
  • \( \Omega \) — subobject classifier
  • \( \text{true}: 1 \to \Omega \) — truth value morphism
  • \( \chi_m \) — characteristic morphism
  • \( \text{Sh}(X) \) — category of sheaves on X
  • \( f^*, f_* \) — inverse and direct image functors

@dataclass
class OpenSet:

    name: str

                      self.elements | other.elements)

class Presheaf:
    """
    A presheaf on a topological space.
    Assigns data to each open set with restriction maps.
    """
    
    def __init__(self, space_name: str):
        self.space_name = space_name
        self.sections: Dict[OpenSet, Any] = {}
        self.restrictions: Dict[Tuple[OpenSet, OpenSet], Callable] = {}
    
    def add_section(self, open_set: OpenSet, data: Any):
        """Add a section over an open set."""
        self.sections[open_set] = data
    
    def add_restriction(self, U: OpenSet, V: OpenSet, 
                       restriction_map: Callable):
        """Add restriction map from U to V (where V ⊆ U)."""
        if not V <= U:
            raise ValueError("V must be subset of U")
        self.restrictions[(U, V)] = restriction_map
    
    def restrict(self, section: Any, U: OpenSet, V: OpenSet) -> Any:
        """Restrict a section from U to V."""
        if (U, V) in self.restrictions:
            return self.restrictions[(U, V)](section)
        # Default: assume section is dict-like
        if isinstance(section, dict):
            return {k: v for k, v in section.items() if k in V.elements}
        return section

class Sheaf(Presheaf):
    """
    A sheaf satisfies the gluing axiom.
    Compatible local sections can be glued to a global section.
    """
    
    def check_gluing(self, open_sets: list, local_sections: list) -> bool:
        """
        Check if local sections are compatible on overlaps.
        """
        for i, (U, s_i) in enumerate(zip(open_sets, local_sections)):
            for j, (V, s_j) in enumerate(zip(open_sets, local_sections)):
                if i < j:
                    # Check intersection
                    W = U.intersection(V)
                    if W.elements:  # Non-empty intersection
                        restrict_i = self.restrict(s_i, U, W)
                        restrict_j = self.restrict(s_j, V, W)
                        if restrict_i != restrict_j:
                            return False
        return True
    
    def glue(self, open_sets: list, local_sections: list) -> Any:
        """
        Glue compatible local sections (if sheaf property holds).
        """
        if not self.check_gluing(open_sets, local_sections):
            raise ValueError("Local sections are not compatible")
        
        # Glue by union
        glued = {}
        for U, s in zip(open_sets, local_sections):
            if isinstance(s, dict):
                glued.update(s)
            else:
                glued[U.name] = s
        
        return glued

class SubobjectClassifier:
    """
    Represents the subobject classifier Ω in a topos.
    In Set, Ω = {True, False}.
    In a sheaf topos, Ω is the sheaf of truth values.
    """
    
    def __init__(self):
        self.truth_values = {True, False}
    
    def characteristic_function(self, subobject: Set, total: Set) -> Callable:
        """
        Create characteristic function χ: total → Ω.
        χ(x) = True if x ∈ subobject, False otherwise.
        """
        def chi(x):
            return x in subobject
        return chi
    
    def truth_arrow(self) -> bool:
        """The 'true' morphism 1 → Ω."""
        return True
    
    def false_arrow(self) -> bool:
        """The 'false' morphism 1 → Ω."""
        return False

class ToposOfSets:
    """
    The topos of sets: the prototypical topos.
    """
    
    def __init__(self):
        self.omega = SubobjectClassifier()
    
    def exponential(self, A: Set, B: Set) -> Set:
        """
        B^A: the set of all functions from A to B.
        In finite case, represented as set of tuples or dicts.
        """
        from itertools import product
        
        if not A:
            return {frozenset()}  # Singleton set (empty function)
        
        # For small finite sets, enumerate all functions
        A_list = list(A)
        B_list = list(B)
        
        functions = []
        for values in product(B_list, repeat=len(A_list)):
            func = dict(zip(A_list, values))
            functions.append(frozenset(func.items()))
        
        return set(functions)
    
    def subobject(self, A: Set) -> Set:
        """The set of all subobjects (subsets) of A."""
        from itertools import combinations
        
        subsets = set()
        A_list = list(A)
        for r in range(len(A_list) + 1):
            for subset in combinations(A_list, r):
                subsets.add(frozenset(subset))
        return subsets
    
    def power_object(self, A: Set) -> Set:
        """P(A) = Ω^A: the power object (power set in Set)."""
        return self.subobject(A)

The Development of Abstract Algebra: From Equations to Structures

The 19th century witnessed a profound transformation in mathematics, as the study of specific objects—numbers, equations, geometric figures—gave way to the study of abstract structures. This shift, which we now call the birth of modern algebra, was neither sudden nor the work of a single individual. It emerged from centuries of attempts to solve polynomial equations, coupled with the gradual realization that the methods used to study these equations possessed a deeper unity.

The story begins with the solution of the cubic equation in 16th century Italy. Mathematicians like del Ferro, Tartaglia, and Cardano discovered formulas for solving cubic and quartic equations, expressions involving radicals that generalize the quadratic formula. Yet the quintic equation resisted all attempts. In 1799, Paolo Ruffini published an incomplete proof that the general quintic could not be solved by radicals. The definitive solution came from the young Norwegian mathematician Niels Henrik Abel in 1824, who proved the impossibility of such a solution.

But it was Évariste Galois who transformed the question entirely. Facing a fatal duel at age twenty, Galois spent his final night writing down the theory that would bear his name. Rather than studying equations directly, Galois studied the permutations of their roots—the symmetries hidden within the coefficients. He showed that an equation was solvable by radicals precisely when its associated group of symmetries had a certain structure, now called solvability. The quintic equation, in general, has the symmetric group S₅ as its Galois group, and S₅ is not solvable.

Galois's insight was revolutionary: the solvability of an equation depends not on the equation itself, but on the structure of its symmetry group. This was the first instance of what we now recognize as the structural approach to mathematics. The objects themselves—polynomials, roots, numbers—became secondary to the relationships and transformations between them.

This structural viewpoint spread rapidly through mathematics. In Germany, Richard Dedekind developed the theory of ideals, abstracting the properties of numbers to study factorization in algebraic number fields. In England, Arthur Cayley and James Joseph Sylvester created the theory of matrices, recognizing that arrays of numbers could be manipulated as algebraic entities in their own right. The concept of a group, once implicit in the work of Galois and Cauchy, was explicitly defined by Cayley in 1854.

By the end of the 19th century, the German mathematician Richard Dedekind and his successors, particularly David Hilbert and Emmy Noether, had established what we now call modern abstract algebra. Noether's work in particular showed how the study of rings, ideals, and modules could unify vast areas of mathematics. Her famous dictum—"relations are more important than objects"—captured the essence of the structural revolution.

The impact of this transformation extended far beyond algebra. The structural approach influenced topology through the work of Poincaré and later the Bourbaki group, reshaped analysis through functional analysis, and provided the foundation for category theory in the mid-20th century. Today, when mathematicians speak of groups, rings, fields, or categories, they work within a conceptual framework that emerged from the 19th century's profound reimagining of what mathematics could be.

IMO Shortlist 2024 Problem N6

Let \( n \) be a positive integer. A sequence \( a_1, a_2, \ldots, a_n \) of positive integers is called good if \( a_1 + a_2 + \cdots + a_n = 2n \) and for every \( k \in \{1, 2, \ldots, n\} \), no \( k \) consecutive terms of the sequence sum to \( k \).

Find the number of good sequences.

Hint

Consider the partial sums \( S_k = a_1 + a_2 + \cdots + a_k \). The condition about consecutive terms can be rephrased in terms of these partial sums. What does it mean for no \( k \) consecutive terms to sum to \( k \)?

Solution

Solution:

Let \( S_k = a_1 + \cdots + a_k \) with \( S_0 = 0 \) and \( S_n = 2n \). The condition that no \( k \) consecutive terms sum to \( k \) means: for all valid \( i \) and \( k \), we have \( S_{i+k} - S_i \neq k \).

Equivalently, \( S_j - S_i \neq j - i \) for all \( 0 \leq i < j \leq n \) with \( j - i \leq n \). This means \( S_j - j \neq S_i - i \).

Define \( T_k = S_k - k \). Then \( T_0 = 0 \), \( T_n = 2n - n = n \), and all \( T_k \) must be distinct integers.

Since each \( a_i \geq 1 \), we have \( S_k \geq k \), so \( T_k \geq 0 \). Also \( T_k = T_{k-1} + a_k - 1 \geq T_{k-1} \).

So \( 0 = T_0 \leq T_1 \leq \cdots \leq T_n = n \), with all values distinct integers in \( \{0, 1, \ldots, n\} \).

This means \( \{T_0, T_1, \ldots, T_n\} = \{0, 1, \ldots, n\} \), so each value appears exactly once. Therefore \( T_k = k \) for all \( k \), which gives \( a_k = 1 \) for all \( k \).

But wait: if all \( a_k = 1 \), then any single term sums to 1, violating the condition for \( k = 1 \).

Let's reconsider: we need \( T_j \neq T_i \) for \( j > i \). Since \( T_k \geq T_{k-1} \), we need strict increase: \( T_k > T_{k-1} \), meaning \( a_k \geq 2 \) for all \( k \).

But then \( S_n \geq 2n \), and equality requires \( a_k = 2 \) for all \( k \).

Check: if all \( a_k = 2 \), then any \( k \) consecutive terms sum to \( 2k \neq k \) (for \( k \geq 1 \)). ✓

So there is exactly 1 good sequence: \( (2, 2, \ldots, 2) \).

Phase Portrait of a Dynamical System

A phase portrait visualizes the behavior of a dynamical system by plotting trajectories in phase space. Below is Python code to generate the phase portrait of the Van der Pol oscillator, a nonlinear system that exhibits limit cycle behavior:

\[\frac{dx}{dt} = y, \quad \frac{dy}{dt} = \mu(1-x^2)y - x\]

The parameter \( \mu \) controls the nonlinearity. For \( \mu > 0 \), the system has a stable limit cycle that attracts nearby trajectories.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy.integrate import odeint

# Van der Pol oscillator parameters
mu = 1.0

def vanderpol(state, t, mu):
    """Van der Pol oscillator equations."""
    x, y = state
    dxdt = y
    dydt = mu * (1 - x**2) * y - x
    return [dxdt, dydt]

# Create phase portrait
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(10, 8))

# Create grid of initial conditions
x = np.linspace(-3, 3, 20)
y = np.linspace(-3, 3, 20)
X, Y = np.meshgrid(x, y)

# Calculate vector field
U = Y
V = mu * (1 - X**2) * Y - X

# Normalize arrows
M = np.sqrt(U**2 + V**2)
U, V = U/M, V/M

# Plot vector field
ax.quiver(X, Y, U, V, M, cmap='viridis', alpha=0.6)

# Plot trajectories from different initial conditions
initial_conditions = [
    [0.1, 0.1], [2.0, 0.0], [-2.0, 0.0],
    [0.0, 2.0], [0.0, -2.0], [2.5, 2.5]
]

t = np.linspace(0, 20, 2000)

colors = ['red', 'blue', 'green', 'orange', 'purple', 'brown']
for (x0, y0), color in zip(initial_conditions, colors):
    solution = odeint(vanderpol, [x0, y0], t, args=(mu,))
    x_traj, y_traj = solution[:, 0], solution[:, 1]
    ax.plot(x_traj, y_traj, color=color, linewidth=2, 
            label=f'({x0}, {y0})')
    ax.plot(x0, y0, 'o', color=color, markersize=8)  # Starting point

ax.set_xlabel('x', fontsize=12)
ax.set_ylabel('y', fontsize=12)
ax.set_title(f'Van der Pol Oscillator Phase Portrait (μ = {mu})', fontsize=14)
ax.set_xlim(-3, 3)
ax.set_ylim(-3, 3)
ax.grid(True, alpha=0.3)
ax.legend(loc='upper right', fontsize=9)
ax.set_aspect('equal')

plt.tight_layout()
plt.savefig('vanderpol_phase_portrait.png', dpi=150, bbox_inches='tight')
plt.show()

print("Phase portrait saved!")

# Additional analysis: find limit cycle numerically
print("\n--- Limit Cycle Analysis ---")

# The limit cycle is the closed trajectory that other trajectories approach
# For mu = 1, we can estimate its properties

# Run from a point on the limit cycle (approximately)
t_long = np.linspace(0, 50, 5000)
solution = odeint(vanderpol, [2.0, 0.0], t_long, args=(mu,))

# Find approximate period by looking at crossings
x_sol = solution[:, 0]
crossings = []
for i in range(1, len(x_sol)):
    if x_sol[i-1] < 0 and x_sol[i] >= 0 and solution[i-1, 1] > 0:
        crossings.append(t_long[i])

if len(crossings) >= 2:
    period = crossings[-1] - crossings[-2]
    print(f"Estimated period of limit cycle: {period:.4f}")
    
# Amplitude estimation
amplitude = (max(x_sol[-1000:]) - min(x_sol[-1000:])) / 2
print(f"Estimated amplitude: {amplitude:.4f}")
