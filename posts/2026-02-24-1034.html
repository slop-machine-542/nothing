1. Algebraic K-Theory: Invariants of Rings

Tags: algebraic-k-theory homological-algebra ring-theory

Motivation

Algebraic K-theory emerged from attempts to generalize the Riemann-Roch theorem and to understand the obstruction to certain geometric constructions. Grothendieck introduced \(K_0\) in the late 1950s to formulate his version of Riemann-Roch. Bass and others developed \(K_1\) and established fundamental exact sequences. The subject took a revolutionary turn when Quillen introduced higher K-theory in 1972, providing a unified framework and powerful computational tools. Today, algebraic K-theory connects number theory, algebraic geometry, and topology, with applications ranging from the study of zeta functions to the classification of manifolds.

Definitions

Let \(R\) be a ring (associative with unit). The Grothendieck group \(K_0(R)\) is defined as follows: let \(\mathcal{P}(R)\) be the category of finitely generated projective left \(R\)-modules. Form the free abelian group on isomorphism classes \([P]\) of objects in \(\mathcal{P}(R)\), then quotient by the subgroup generated by relations \([P \oplus Q] - [P] - [Q]\).

The Whitehead group \(K_1(R)\) is: \[K_1(R) = GL(R)^{ab} = GL(R)/[GL(R), GL(R)]\] where \(GL(R) = \bigcup_{n \geq 1} GL_n(R)\) is the infinite general linear group.

For a commutative ring \(R\), the special Whitehead group is: \[SK_1(R) = \ker(\det: K_1(R) \to R^\times)\]

The relative K-group \(K_0(R, I)\) for an ideal \(I \subseteq R\) classifies projective modules with specified reduction modulo \(I\).

Theorem and Proof

Theorem (Bass's Fundamental Theorem): Let \(R\) be a ring and \(R[t]\) the polynomial ring. Then there is a natural splitting: \[K_1(R[t]) \cong K_1(R) \oplus NK_1(R)\] where \(NK_1(R) = \ker(K_1(R[t]) \to K_1(R))\) (evaluation at \(t = 0\)). Moreover, if \(R\) is regular, then \(NK_1(R) = 0\).

Proof Sketch: The proof proceeds in several stages:

  1. Horrocks' Theorem: For a local ring \(R\), any finitely generated projective \(R[t]\)-module is free. This is the key algebraic input.
  2. Localization: Consider the multiplicative set \(S = \{t^n\}\) in \(R[t]\). The localization \(R[t]_S = R[t, t^{-1}]\).
  3. Mayer-Vietoris: There is a cartesian square: \[\begin{array}{ccc} R[t] & \to & R[t, t^{-1}] \\ \downarrow & & \downarrow \\ R & \to & R[t^{-1}]_{(t^{-1})} \end{array}\]
  4. Exact Sequence: This yields a long exact sequence in K-theory. The key computation shows that the boundary map connects \(K_0\) and \(K_1\).
  5. Regularity: When \(R\) is regular, the homological dimension of \(R[t]\)-modules is controlled, forcing \(NK_1(R) = 0\).
The fundamental theorem is crucial for understanding the behavior of K-theory under polynomial extensions and forms the basis for the construction of negative K-groups. ∎

Notation Guide

  • \(K_n(R)\) — n-th K-group of ring \(R\)
  • \(GL_n(R)\) — General linear group of invertible \(n \times n\) matrices
  • \(E_n(R)\) — Elementary subgroup generated by transvections
  • \(\mathcal{P}(R)\) — Category of finitely generated projective modules
  • \(R^\times\) — Group of units of \(R\)
  • \(\tilde{K}_0(R)\) — Reduced K_0 (kernel of rank map)

2. Étale Cohomology: Cohomology for Algebraic Varieties

Tags: etale-cohomology algebraic-geometry weil-conjectures

Motivation

The Weil conjectures, formulated in 1949, predicted deep properties of the zeta functions of algebraic varieties over finite fields. To prove these conjectures, Grothendieck needed a cohomology theory for algebraic varieties that would work over any field, not just the complex numbers. Singular cohomology requires a topology, which is not available for varieties over finite fields. Grothendieck's revolutionary insight was to use the étale topology, where "open sets" are étale morphisms rather than Zariski open subsets. This theory, developed with Artin and others in SGA 4, provided the necessary tools and led to Deligne's proof of the last Weil conjecture in 1974.

Definitions

An étale morphism \(f: X \to Y\) of schemes is a morphism that is:

  • Flat (preserves algebraic dependencies)
  • Unramified (tangent spaces inject)
Equivalently, \(f\) is smooth of relative dimension 0.

The étale site \(X_{et}\) of a scheme \(X\) has:

  • Objects: Étale morphisms \(U \to X\)
  • Morphisms: Morphisms over \(X\)
  • Covers: Surjective families of étale morphisms

An étale sheaf \(\mathcal{F}\) on \(X\) is a contravariant functor from \(X_{et}\) to abelian groups (or sets, etc.) satisfying the sheaf condition for étale covers.

The étale cohomology groups \(H^i_{et}(X, \mathcal{F})\) are the right derived functors of the global sections functor \(\Gamma(X, -)\) on the category of étale sheaves.

For a prime \(\ell\) different from the characteristic, the \(\ell\)-adic cohomology is: \[H^i(X, \mathbb{Q}_\ell) = \left(\varprojlim_n H^i_{et}(X, \mathbb{Z}/\ell^n\mathbb{Z})\right) \otimes_{\mathbb{Z}_\ell} \mathbb{Q}_\ell\]

Theorem and Proof

Theorem (Grothendieck's Comparison Theorem): Let \(X\) be a smooth scheme over \(\mathbb{C}\) and let \(X^{an}\) be the associated complex analytic space. Then for any finite abelian group \(\Lambda\) and any \(i \geq 0\): \[H^i_{et}(X, \Lambda) \cong H^i_{sing}(X^{an}, \Lambda)\]

Proof Sketch: The proof uses several key ingredients:

  1. Artin Comparison: First establish the result for \(X = \mathbb{A}^n\) using explicit computation. Both cohomologies vanish in positive degrees for affine space.
  2. Proper Base Change: For proper morphisms, étale cohomology commutes with base change. This allows reduction to the proper case.
  3. Resolution of Singularities: Use Hironaka's theorem to reduce to the smooth case via blow-ups and excision.
  4. GAGA: Serre's GAGA principle connects coherent sheaves in algebraic and analytic geometry.
  5. Spectral Sequences: Use the Leray spectral sequence for the comparison morphism and show it degenerates.
The key insight is that étale covers of a complex variety are "finer" than analytic covers in a way that exactly compensates for the coarser topology. The exponential map connects the discrete (étale) and continuous (analytic) worlds. ∎

Notation Guide

  • \(X_{et}\) — Étale site of \(X\)
  • \(H^i_{et}(X, \mathcal{F})\) — Étale cohomology groups
  • \(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}(1)\) — Sheaf of n-th roots of unity
  • \(\mathbb{Q}_\ell\) — Field of \(\ell\)-adic numbers
  • \(R^i f_*\) — Higher direct image functors
  • \(D^b_c(X, \mathbb{Q}_\ell)\) — Derived category of constructible sheaves

3. Random Matrix Theory: Statistical Properties of Eigenvalues

Tags: random-matrix-theory probability statistical-mechanics

Motivation

Random matrix theory (RMT) studies the statistical properties of matrices with random entries. Originating in statistics in the 1920s-30s, it gained prominence in the 1950s when Wigner used it to model energy levels of heavy nuclei. The key insight was that complex quantum systems exhibit universal statistical behavior that can be captured by random matrices. Since then, RMT has found applications in quantum chaos, number theory (connections to the Riemann zeta function), wireless communications, and machine learning. The field reveals deep connections between probability theory, integrable systems, and exactly solvable models.

Definitions

The Gaussian Unitary Ensemble (GUE) consists of \(n \times n\) Hermitian matrices \(H\) with probability density: \[P(H) \propto \exp\left(-\frac{n}{2}\text{Tr}(H^2)\right)\]

The Gaussian Orthogonal Ensemble (GOE) consists of real symmetric matrices with: \[P(H) \propto \exp\left(-\frac{n}{4}\text{Tr}(H^2)\right)\]

The empirical spectral distribution of a matrix \(H\) with eigenvalues \(\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_n\) is: \[\mu_H = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \delta_{\lambda_i}\]

The Wigner semicircle law describes the limiting distribution of eigenvalues for Wigner matrices: \[\rho(x) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{4 - x^2} \cdot \mathbf{1}_{[-2,2]}(x)\]

The level spacing distribution \(P(s)\) gives the probability density of gaps between adjacent eigenvalues.

Theorem and Proof

Theorem (Wigner's Semicircle Law): Let \(H_n\) be an \(n \times n\) Wigner matrix: \(H_n = \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}(A + A^*)\) where \(A\) has independent entries with mean 0, variance 1, and bounded moments. Then the empirical spectral distribution of \(H_n\) converges weakly almost surely to the semicircle distribution:

\[\mu_{H_n} \xrightarrow{w} \rho_{sc}(x)\,dx = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{4-x^2}\,\mathbf{1}_{|x|\leq 2}\,dx\]

Proof Sketch: We outline the moment method:

  1. Moment Computation: The \(k\)-th moment of the semicircle distribution is: \[m_k = \int_{-2}^{2} x^k \rho_{sc}(x)\,dx = \begin{cases} C_{k/2} & k \text{ even} \\ 0 & k \text{ odd} \end{cases}\] where \(C_n = \frac{1}{n+1}\binom{2n}{n}\) is the Catalan number.
  2. Trace Formula: For the empirical measure: \[\int x^k \,d\mu_{H_n} = \frac{1}{n}\text{Tr}(H_n^k) = \frac{1}{n^{1+k/2}}\text{Tr}((A+A^*)^k)\]
  3. Graph Expansion: Expand the trace as a sum over closed walks. Non-vanishing terms correspond to pairings of edges (since entries have mean 0).
  4. Catalan Structure: The contributing walks are in bijection with Dyck paths, counted by Catalan numbers.
  5. Convergence: Show variance goes to 0, giving almost sure convergence.
For the GUE, stronger results hold via the determinantal structure and orthogonal polynomials. ∎

Notation Guide

  • \(\text{GUE}(n)\) — Gaussian Unitary Ensemble
  • \(\text{GOE}(n)\) — Gaussian Orthogonal Ensemble
  • \(\rho_{sc}\) — Semicircle density
  • \(C_n\) — n-th Catalan number
  • \(P(s)\) — Level spacing distribution
  • \(\beta\) — Dyson index (1 for GOE, 2 for GUE, 4 for GSE)
  • \(K_n(x,y)\) — Christoffel-Darboux kernel

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy import stats
from scipy.special import gammaln

class RandomMatrixTheory:
    """Computational tools for random matrix theory."""
    
    @staticmethod
    def generate_gue(n):
        """Generate n×n GUE matrix."""
        # H = (A + A†)/√(2n) where A has complex Gaussian entries
        A = (np.random.randn(n, n) + 1j * np.random.randn(n, n)) / np.sqrt(2)
        H = (A + A.conj().T) / np.sqrt(2 * n)
        return H
    
    @staticmethod
    def generate_goe(n):
        """Generate n×n GOE matrix."""
        # H = (A + A^T)/√(2n) where A has real Gaussian entries
        A = np.random.randn(n, n)
        H = (A + A.T) / np.sqrt(2 * n)
        return H
    
    @staticmethod
    def generate_wigner(n):
        """Generate n×n Wigner matrix."""
        # Upper triangular: N(0,1), diagonal: N(0,2)
        A = np.random.randn(n, n)
        H = (A + A.T) / np.sqrt(2)
        # Scale diagonal
        np.fill_diagonal(H, np.random.randn(n))
        return H / np.sqrt(n)
    
    @staticmethod
    def semicircle_pdf(x):
        """Semicircle probability density."""
        x = np.asarray(x)
        result = np.zeros_like(x, dtype=float)
        mask = np.abs(x) <= 2
        result[mask] = np.sqrt(4 - x[mask]**2) / (2 * np.pi)
        return result
    
    @staticmethod
    def wigner_surmise(s, beta=1):
        """
        Wigner surmise for level spacing distribution.
        beta=1: GOE, beta=2: GUE, beta=4: GSE
        """
        a = beta / 2
        C = (2**a * gamma(a + 1) / gamma(2*a + 1))**(a + 1)
        # Simplified form
        if beta == 1:
            return (np.pi * s / 2) * np.exp(-np.pi * s**2 / 4)
        elif beta == 2:
            return (32 * s**2 / np.pi**2) * np.exp(-4 * s**2 / np.pi)
        elif beta == 4:
            return (2**18 * s**4 / (3**6 * np.pi**3)) * np.exp(-64 * s**2 / (9 * np.pi))
        return None
    
    @staticmethod
    def compute_level_spacings(eigenvalues):
        """Compute normalized level spacings."""
        # Sort eigenvalues
        ev = np.sort(eigenvalues)
        # Compute gaps
        gaps = np.diff(ev)
        # Normalize by mean spacing
        mean_gap = np.mean(gaps)
        return gaps / mean_gap
    
    @staticmethod
    def marchenko_pastur_pdf(x, lambda_param):
        """
        Marchenko-Pastur distribution for sample covariance matrices.
        lambda = n/p (aspect ratio)
        """
        a = (1 - np.sqrt(lambda_param))**2
        b = (1 + np.sqrt(lambda_param))**2
        
        x = np.asarray(x)
        result = np.zeros_like(x, dtype=float)
        mask = (x >= a) & (x <= b)
        
        result[mask] = np.sqrt((b - x[mask]) * (x[mask] - a)) / (2 * np.pi * lambda_param * x[mask])
        return result

1. Information Geometry: The Geometry of Statistical Manifolds

Tags: information-geometry differential-geometry statistics

Real-World Motivation

Information geometry provides a powerful framework for understanding statistical inference by viewing families of probability distributions as Riemannian manifolds. This geometric perspective, pioneered by Chentsov and Amari, reveals deep structure in maximum likelihood estimation, natural gradient descent, and statistical efficiency. Applications include neural network training (natural gradient methods), machine learning (information bottleneck), evolutionary biology (Fisher's fundamental theorem), and statistical physics. The field connects statistics with differential geometry, offering intuitive visualizations and efficient algorithms for high-dimensional parameter spaces.

Definitions

A statistical manifold is a parametric family of probability distributions \(\mathcal{M} = \{p(x; \theta) : \theta \in \Theta\}\) where \(\Theta \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n\) is the parameter space.

The Fisher information metric is the Riemannian metric on \(\mathcal{M}\) defined by: \[g_{ij}(\theta) = E_{p(x;\theta)}\left[\frac{\partial \log p(x;\theta)}{\partial \theta^i} \frac{\partial \log p(x;\theta)}{\partial \theta^j}\right] = -E_{p(x;\theta)}\left[\frac{\partial^2 \log p(x;\theta)}{\partial \theta^i \partial \theta^j}\right]\]

The Kullback-Leibler divergence (relative entropy) is: \[D_{KL}(p \| q) = \int p(x) \log\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\,dx\]

The exponential connection (e-connection) and mixture connection (m-connection) are dual affine connections on \(\mathcal{M}\) defined via the decomposition: \[\partial_i \partial_j \log p = \Gamma_{ij}^{(e)k} \partial_k \log p + h_{ij}\] where \(h_{ij}\) is orthogonal to the tangent space.

Theorem and Proof

Theorem (Chentsov's Uniqueness Theorem): The Fisher information metric is (up to a constant factor) the unique Riemannian metric on statistical manifolds that is invariant under sufficient statistics and Markov embeddings.

Proof Sketch: The proof proceeds by analyzing invariance requirements:

  1. Sufficient Statistics: If \(T(x)\) is a sufficient statistic for \(\theta\), then \(I_T(\theta) = I_X(\theta)\) (Rao-Blackwell). This requires the metric to respect the data processing inequality.
  2. Markov Embeddings: Consider embeddings of the form \(p(x;\theta) \mapsto \sum_y p(x|y)q(y;\theta)\). The metric must be consistent under such coarse-grainings.
  3. Monotonicity: The Fisher information satisfies \(I_Y(\theta) \leq I_X(\theta)\) for any Markov kernel \(p(y|x)\), with equality iff the kernel is sufficient.
  4. Characterization: Any metric satisfying these conditions must be proportional to the Fisher metric. The key step uses the fact that the Fisher metric is the Hessian of the KL divergence: \[g_{ij}(\theta) = \partial_i \partial_j D_{KL}(p(x;\theta) \| p(x;\theta'))|_{\theta'=\theta}\]
The uniqueness follows from the fact that these invariance properties completely determine the metric on the tangent space at each point. ∎

2. Game Theory: Strategic Interaction and Equilibrium

Tags: game-theory economics nash-equilibrium

Real-World Motivation

Game theory studies mathematical models of strategic interaction among rational decision-makers. Founded by von Neumann and Morgenstern in 1944, it has become fundamental to economics, political science, biology, and computer science. Applications range from auction design and market mechanisms to evolutionary dynamics and network routing. The Nash equilibrium concept provides a solution concept for non-cooperative games, while cooperative game theory addresses coalition formation and fair division. Modern applications include algorithmic game theory, mechanism design for online platforms, and multi-agent reinforcement learning.

Definitions

A normal-form game is a tuple \(G = (N, (A_i)_{i \in N}, (u_i)_{i \in N})\) where:

  • \(N = \{1, \ldots, n\}\) is the set of players
  • \(A_i\) is the set of actions for player \(i\)
  • \(u_i: A \to \mathbb{R}\) is the utility function for player \(i\), where \(A = \prod_{i} A_i\)

A mixed strategy for player \(i\) is a probability distribution \(\sigma_i \in \Delta(A_i)\). The expected utility is: \[u_i(\sigma) = \sum_{a \in A} u_i(a) \prod_{j \in N} \sigma_j(a_j)\]

A Nash equilibrium is a strategy profile \(\sigma^* = (\sigma_1^*, \ldots, \sigma_n^*)\) such that for all players \(i\) and all alternative strategies \(\sigma_i'\): \[u_i(\sigma_i^*, \sigma_{-i}^*) \geq u_i(\sigma_i', \sigma_{-i}^*)\]

A strategy \(\sigma_i\) is a best response to \(\sigma_{-i}\) if: \[\sigma_i \in \arg\max_{\sigma_i'} u_i(\sigma_i', \sigma_{-i})\]

Theorem and Proof

Theorem (Nash's Existence Theorem): Every finite normal-form game has at least one Nash equilibrium in mixed strategies.

Proof: We use Kakutani's fixed point theorem. Define the best response correspondence \(BR: \Sigma \rightrightarrows \Sigma\) by: \[BR(\sigma) = \prod_{i \in N} BR_i(\sigma_{-i})\] where \(BR_i(\sigma_{-i}) = \arg\max_{\sigma_i'} u_i(\sigma_i', \sigma_{-i})\).

A Nash equilibrium is exactly a fixed point of \(BR\). We verify Kakutani's conditions:

  1. Non-empty: \(BR(\sigma)\) is non-empty because \(\Delta(A_i)\) is compact and \(u_i\) is continuous.
  2. Convex-valued: \(BR_i(\sigma_{-i})\) is convex because \(u_i(\cdot, \sigma_{-i})\) is linear in \(\sigma_i\).
  3. Upper hemicontinuous: The best response correspondence has closed graph because \(u_i\) is continuous.
  4. Domain: \(\Sigma = \prod_i \Delta(A_i)\) is compact and convex.
By Kakutani's fixed point theorem, \(BR\) has a fixed point, which is a Nash equilibrium. ∎

Notation Guide

  • \(N\) — Set of players
  • \(A_i\) — Action set for player \(i\)
  • \(\sigma_i\) — Mixed strategy for player \(i\)
  • \(\sigma_{-i}\) — Strategies of all players except \(i\)
  • \(u_i\) — Utility function for player \(i\)
  • \(BR_i\) — Best response correspondence
  • \(\Delta(S)\) — Set of probability distributions on \(S\)
  • \(\epsilon\)-NE — \(\epsilon\)-approximate Nash equilibrium

3. Computational Algebraic Geometry: Algorithms for Polynomial Systems

Tags: computational-algebraic-geometry groebner-bases symbolic-computation

Real-World Motivation

Computational algebraic geometry provides algorithms for solving systems of polynomial equations, with applications in robotics (kinematics), computer vision (structure from motion), cryptography (algebraic attacks), and systems biology (chemical reaction networks). The development of Gröbner bases by Buchberger in 1965 revolutionized the field, providing a general method for solving polynomial systems. Modern techniques include numerical algebraic geometry (homotopy continuation) for finding approximate solutions and tropical geometry for studying asymptotic behavior. These methods bridge pure mathematics with practical computation, enabling the solution of previously intractable problems.

Definitions

Let \(k\) be a field and \(R = k[x_1, \ldots, x_n]\) the polynomial ring. An ideal \(I \subseteq R\) is a subset closed under addition and multiplication by elements of \(R\).

The variety of an ideal \(I\) is: \[V(I) = \{x \in k^n : f(x) = 0 \text{ for all } f \in I\}\]

A monomial order \(\prec\) is a total order on monomials satisfying:

  • \(1 \prec x^\alpha\) for all \(\alpha \neq 0\)
  • If \(x^\alpha \prec x^\beta\) then \(x^{\alpha+\gamma} \prec x^{\beta+\gamma}\)

The leading term \(\text{LT}(f)\) of a polynomial \(f\) is its largest monomial with respect to \(\prec\).

A Gröbner basis for an ideal \(I\) is a finite set \(G = \{g_1, \ldots, g_m\} \subseteq I\) such that \(\langle \text{LT}(g_1), \ldots, \text{LT}(g_m) \rangle = \langle \text{LT}(I) \rangle\).

Theorem and Proof

Theorem (Buchberger's Criterion): A set \(G = \{g_1, \ldots, g_m\}\) is a Gröbner basis for \(I = \langle G \rangle\) if and only if for all \(i \neq j\), the S-polynomial reduces to 0 modulo \(G\): \[S(g_i, g_j) = \frac{\text{lcm}(\text{LM}(g_i), \text{LM}(g_j))}{\text{LT}(g_i)} g_i - \frac{\text{lcm}(\text{LM}(g_i), \text{LM}(g_j))}{\text{LT}(g_j)} g_j \xrightarrow{G} 0\]

Proof Sketch: We prove both directions:

  1. (\(\Rightarrow\)): If \(G\) is a Gröbner basis, then \(S(g_i, g_j) \in I\). Since the leading terms of \(G\) generate the leading term ideal, any element of \(I\) reduces to 0 by the division algorithm.
  2. (\(\Leftarrow\)): Suppose all S-polynomials reduce to 0. Let \(f \in I\). We can write \(f = \sum_i h_i g_i\). Among all such representations, choose one where \(\max_i \text{multideg}(h_i g_i)\) is minimal (call it \(\delta\)).
  3. If \(\text{multideg}(f) = \delta\), then \(\text{LT}(f)\) is divisible by some \(\text{LT}(g_i)\), so we're done.
  4. Otherwise, cancellation occurs. The leading terms of some \(h_i g_i\) and \(h_j g_j\) cancel. This cancellation can be expressed using S-polynomials, which by assumption reduce to 0.
  5. This gives a new representation with smaller \(\delta\), contradicting minimality. ∎

Notation Guide

  • \(k[x_1, \ldots, x_n]\) — Polynomial ring over field \(k\)
  • \(V(I)\) — Affine variety of ideal \(I\)
  • \(I(V)\) — Ideal of variety \(V\)
  • \(\text{LT}(f)\) — Leading term of polynomial \(f\)
  • \(\text{LM}(f)\) — Leading monomial
  • \(\text{LC}(f)\) — Leading coefficient
  • \(S(f, g)\) — S-polynomial of \(f\) and \(g\)
  • \(f \xrightarrow{G} r\) — Reduction of \(f\) to remainder \(r\) modulo \(G\)

        """Lexicographic order on monomials."""

            if p1 != p2:

        """
        Multivariate polynomial division.
        Returns (quotient_list, remainder)
        
        Each polynomial is [(coeff, monomial_dict), ...]
        """
        quotients = [[] for _ in divisors]
        remainder = []
        
        # Sort dividend terms by descending order
        p = sorted(dividend, key=lambda t: 
            ComputationalAlgebraicGeometry.monomial_degree(t[1]), reverse=True)
        
        while p:
            coeff, mono = p[0]
            divided = False
            
            for i, div in enumerate(divisors):
                if not div:
                    continue
                div_lt = div[0]  # Leading term (already sorted)
                div_coeff, div_mono = div_lt
                
                # Check if div_mono divides mono
                if all(mono.get(v, 0) >= div_mono.get(v, 0) for v in div_mono):
                    # Perform division
                    new_coeff = coeff / div_coeff
                    new_mono = {v: mono.get(v, 0) - div_mono.get(v, 0) 
                               for v in set(mono) | set(div_mono)}
                    new_mono = {k: v for k, v in new_mono.items() if v > 0}
                    
                    quotients[i].append((new_coeff, new_mono))
                    
                    # Subtract new_term * divisor from p
                    for dc, dm in div:
                        prod_mono = {v: new_mono.get(v, 0) + dm.get(v, 0) 
                                    for v in set(new_mono) | set(dm)}
                        prod_mono = {k: v for k, v in prod_mono.items() if v > 0}

                                    p.pop(j)
                                found = True
                                break
                        if not found:

    def solve_linear_system(matrix, rhs):
        """Solve linear system using Gaussian elimination."""
        A = np.array(matrix, dtype=float)
        b = np.array(rhs, dtype=float)
        
        n = len(b)
        # Forward elimination
        for i in range(n):
            # Partial pivoting
            max_row = i + np.argmax(np.abs(A[i:, i]))
            if abs(A[max_row, i]) < 1e-10:
                continue
            A[[i, max_row]] = A[[max_row, i]]
            b[[i, max_row]] = b[[max_row, i]]
            
            for j in range(i + 1, n):
                factor = A[j, i] / A[i, i]
                A[j, i:] -= factor * A[i, i:]
                b[j] -= factor * b[i]
        
        # Back substitution
        x = np.zeros(n)
        for i in range(n - 1, -1, -1):
            if abs(A[i, i]) < 1e-10:
                x[i] = 0
            else:
                x[i] = (b[i] - np.dot(A[i, i+1:], x[i+1:])) / A[i, i]
        
        return x
    
    @staticmethod
    def newton_method_system(f, J, x0, tol=1e-6, max_iter=100):
        """
        Newton's method for solving nonlinear system f(x) = 0.
        f: function returning vector
        J: function returning Jacobian matrix
        """
        x = np.array(x0, dtype=float)
        
        for i in range(max_iter):
            fx = np.array(f(x))
            Jx = np.array(J(x))
            
            # Solve J(x) * delta = -f(x)
            try:
                delta = np.linalg.solve(Jx, -fx)
            except np.linalg.LinAlgError:
                return None
            
            x = x + delta
            
            if np.linalg.norm(delta) < tol:
                return x
        
        return x

1. Elliptic Curves and the Birch-Swinnerton-Dyer Conjecture

Tags: elliptic-curves number-theory millennium-problems

Historical Context

Elliptic curves have been studied since the 19th century, with roots in the study of elliptic integrals. The modern arithmetic theory began with Poincaré's 1901 theorem that the rational points form a finitely generated abelian group. Mordell proved this in 1922, and Weil generalized it to abelian varieties. The Birch-Swinnerton-Dyer (BSD) conjecture, formulated in the 1960s based on computational evidence, connects the algebraic rank of the Mordell-Weil group to the analytic behavior of the L-function. It is one of the seven Millennium Prize Problems with a $1 million reward for its solution.

Definitions

An elliptic curve over a field \(k\) is a smooth projective curve of genus 1 with a specified base point \(O\). In Weierstrass form: \[E: y^2 = x^3 + ax + b\] with discriminant \(\Delta = -16(4a^3 + 27b^2) \neq 0\).

The Mordell-Weil group \(E(\mathbb{Q})\) is the group of rational points on \(E\), with the chord-tangent law for addition.

The L-function of \(E\) over \(\mathbb{Q}\) is: \[L(E, s) = \prod_{p \nmid N} \frac{1}{1 - a_p p^{-s} + p^{1-2s}} \prod_{p \mid N} \frac{1}{1 - a_p p^{-s}}\] where \(N\) is the conductor and \(a_p = p + 1 - \#E(\mathbb{F}_p)\).

The analytic rank is the order of vanishing of \(L(E, s)\) at \(s = 1\).

Theorem and Proof

Theorem (Mordell-Weil): Let \(E\) be an elliptic curve over a number field \(K\). Then \(E(K)\) is a finitely generated abelian group: \[E(K) \cong E(K)_{tors} \oplus \mathbb{Z}^r\] where \(E(K)_{tors}\) is the finite torsion subgroup and \(r\) is the algebraic rank.

Proof Sketch: The proof has two main parts:

  1. Weak Mordell-Weil: Show that \(E(K)/2E(K)\) is finite. This uses Kummer theory, Hilbert's Theorem 90, and the theory of heights. The key is constructing a homomorphism to a finite group via reduction modulo primes.
  2. Descent: Use the weak result plus the theory of heights to deduce finite generation. The canonical height \(\hat{h}: E(K) \to \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\) satisfies:
    • \(\hat{h}(P) = 0\) iff \(P\) is torsion
    • \(\hat{h}(nP) = n^2\hat{h}(P)\)
    • \(\{P : \hat{h}(P) \leq B\}\) is finite for all \(B\)
    These properties allow a descent argument: if \(E(K)/2E(K)\) is generated by \(Q_1, \ldots, Q_m\), then any point can be written as \(P = \sum n_i Q_i + 2R\). The height of \(R\) is controlled, leading to finite generation.
The full proof requires careful analysis of local behavior and the theory of heights. ∎

class EllipticCurves:
    """Computational tools for elliptic curves."""
    
    @staticmethod
    def discriminant(a, b):
        """Compute discriminant of y² = x³ + ax + b."""
        return -16 * (4 * a**3 + 27 * b**2)
    
    @staticmethod
    def is_singular(a, b):
        """Check if curve is singular."""
        return EllipticCurves.discriminant(a, b) == 0
    
    @staticmethod
    def point_addition(P, Q, a, modulus=None):
        """
        Add two points on elliptic curve y² = x³ + ax + b.
        P, Q are tuples (x, y) or None for point at infinity.
        """
        # Point at infinity is identity
        if P is None:
            return Q
        if Q is None:
            return P
        
        x1, y1 = P
        x2, y2 = Q
        
        # Handle modular arithmetic
        def mod(x):
            return x if modulus is None else x % modulus
        
        # Check if P = -Q
        if x1 == x2 and mod(y1 + y2) == 0:
            return None
        
        # Compute slope
        if x1 == x2 and y1 == y2:
            # Point doubling
            if y1 == 0:
                return None
            num = mod(3 * x1**2 + a)
            den = mod(2 * y1)
        else:
            # Point addition
            num = mod(y2 - y1)
            den = mod(x2 - x1)
        
        # Modular inverse
        if modulus is not None:
            try:
                den_inv = pow(int(den), -1, modulus)
                m = mod(num * den_inv)
            except ValueError:
                return None
        else:
            m = num / den
        
        # Compute result
        x3 = mod(m**2 - x1 - x2)
        y3 = mod(m * (x1 - x3) - y1)
        
        return (x3, y3)
    
    @staticmethod
    def scalar_multiplication(n, P, a, modulus=None):
        """Compute nP using double-and-add."""
        if n == 0 or P is None:
            return None
        if n < 0:
            x, y = P
            P = (x, -y if modulus is None else (-y) % modulus)
            n = -n
        
        result = None
        addend = P
        
        while n > 0:
            if n & 1:
                result = EllipticCurves.point_addition(result, addend, a, modulus)
            addend = EllipticCurves.point_addition(addend, addend, a, modulus)
            n >>= 1
        
        return result
    
    @staticmethod
    def count_points_finite_field(a, b, p):
        """
        Count points on E(F_p) by enumeration.
        Includes point at infinity.
        """
        count = 1  # Point at infinity
        
        for x in range(p):
            rhs = (x**3 + a*x + b) % p
            # Count solutions to y² = rhs (mod p)
            for y in range(p):
                if (y*y) % p == rhs:
                    count += 1
        
        return count
    
    @staticmethod
    def ap_coefficient(a, b, p):
        """Compute a_p = p + 1 - #E(F_p)."""
        return p + 1 - EllipticCurves.count_points_finite_field(a, b, p)

2. Algebraic Number Theory: Rings of Integers and Ideal Factorization

Tags: algebraic-number-theory dedekind-domains class-field-theory

Historical Context

Algebraic number theory emerged from attempts to prove Fermat's Last Theorem in the 19th century. Kummer's 1847 discovery that unique factorization fails in cyclotomic fields led to the invention of ideal numbers, later formalized by Dedekind as ideals. The theory of rings of integers, discriminants, and class numbers was developed by Dedekind, Kronecker, and Weber. Hilbert's 1897 Zahlbericht synthesized the field and posed fundamental questions. Class field theory, developed by Takagi, Artin, and Hasse in the early 20th century, provides a complete description of abelian extensions. Modern developments include Iwasawa theory and the Langlands program.

Definitions

A number field \(K\) is a finite extension of \(\mathbb{Q}\). The degree is \([K : \mathbb{Q}] = \dim_{\mathbb{Q}} K\).

The ring of integers \(\mathcal{O}_K\) is the integral closure of \(\mathbb{Z}\) in \(K\): \[\mathcal{O}_K = \{\alpha \in K : \exists \text{ monic } f \in \mathbb{Z}[x] \text{ with } f(\alpha) = 0\}\]

The discriminant of \(K\) is \(\text{disc}(K) = \det(\text{Tr}_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(\omega_i \omega_j))\) where \(\{\omega_i\}\) is an integral basis.

The ideal class group is \(\text{Cl}(K) = \text{Frac}(\mathcal{O}_K)/\text{Prin}(\mathcal{O}_K)\), measuring the failure of unique factorization. The class number \(h_K = |\text{Cl}(K)|\).

A Dedekind domain is an integrally closed Noetherian domain of dimension 1.

Theorem and Proof

Theorem (Dedekind's Unique Factorization of Ideals): Let \(K\) be a number field with ring of integers \(\mathcal{O}_K\). Then every nonzero proper ideal \(\mathfrak{a} \subseteq \mathcal{O}_K\) factors uniquely as a product of prime ideals: \[\mathfrak{a} = \mathfrak{p}_1^{e_1} \cdots \mathfrak{p}_r^{e_r}\]

Proof Sketch: We prove existence and uniqueness:

  1. \(\mathcal{O}_K\) is a Dedekind domain: It is integrally closed (by definition), Noetherian (finitely generated as \(\mathbb{Z}\)-module), and has dimension 1 (primes correspond to valuations).
  2. Existence: Suppose there exists an ideal without factorization. Since \(\mathcal{O}_K\) is Noetherian, there is a maximal such ideal \(\mathfrak{m}\). It is contained in a maximal (hence prime) ideal \(\mathfrak{p}\). Then \(\mathfrak{m} \subsetneq \mathfrak{p}^{-1}\mathfrak{m} \subseteq \mathcal{O}_K\). By maximality, \(\mathfrak{p}^{-1}\mathfrak{m}\) factors, so \(\mathfrak{m} = \mathfrak{p} \cdot (\mathfrak{p}^{-1}\mathfrak{m})\) factors, contradiction.
  3. Uniqueness: Suppose \(\mathfrak{p}_1 \cdots \mathfrak{p}_r = \mathfrak{q}_1 \cdots \mathfrak{q}_s\). Then \(\mathfrak{p}_1 \supseteq \mathfrak{q}_1 \cdots \mathfrak{q}_s\), so \(\mathfrak{p}_1 \supseteq \mathfrak{q}_j\) for some \(j\). Since both are maximal, \(\mathfrak{p}_1 = \mathfrak{q}_j\). Cancel and induct.
The key is that in Dedekind domains, "to contain is to divide": \(\mathfrak{a} \subseteq \mathfrak{b}\) iff \(\mathfrak{b} \mid \mathfrak{a}\). ∎

  • \(\text{Cl}(K)\) — Ideal class group

  • \(N(\mathfrak{a}) = |\mathcal{O}_K/\mathfrak{a}|\) — Ideal norm

    @staticmethod
    def quadratic_field_integers(d):
        """
        Return ring of integers for Q(√d).
        If d ≡ 2,3 (mod 4): O_K = Z[√d]
        If d ≡ 1 (mod 4): O_K = Z[(1+√d)/2]
        """
        d = d % 4
        if d < 0:
            d += 4
        
        if d % 4 in [2, 3]:
            return {'basis': [1, f'√{d}'], 'form': 'a + b√d'}
        else:
            return {'basis': [1, f'(1+√{d})/2'], 'form': 'a + b(1+√d)/2'}
    
    @staticmethod
    def quadratic_discriminant(d):
        """Discriminant of Q(√d)."""
        d_squarefree = AlgebraicNumberTheory.squarefree_part(d)
        if d_squarefree % 4 in [2, 3]:
            return 4 * d_squarefree
        else:
            return d_squarefree
    
    @staticmethod
    def squarefree_part(n):
        """Extract squarefree part of integer."""
        n = abs(n)
        if n <= 1:
            return n
        
        # Factor out squares
        result = 1
        d = 2
        while d * d <= n:
            count = 0
            while n % d == 0:
                n //= d
                count += 1
            if count % 2 == 1:
                result *= d
            d += 1 if d == 2 else 2
        
        if n > 1:
            result *= n
        
        return result
    
    @staticmethod
    def norm_quadratic(a, b, d):
        """
        Compute norm of a + b√d.
        N(a + b√d) = a² - db²
        """
        return a**2 - d * b**2
    
    @staticmethod
    def trace_quadratic(a, b, d):
        """
        Compute trace of a + b√d.
        Tr(a + b√d) = 2a
        """
        return 2 * a
    
    @staticmethod
    def is_unit_quadratic(a, b, d):
        """Check if a + b√d is a unit (norm = ±1)."""
        return abs(AlgebraicNumberTheory.norm_quadratic(a, b, d)) == 1
    
    @staticmethod
    def fundamental_unit_positive_d(d, max_search=1000):
        """
        Find fundamental unit of Q(√d) for d > 0.
        Smallest solution to x² - dy² = ±1 with x, y > 0.
        """
        min_unit = None
        min_x = float('inf')
        
        for y in range(1, max_search):
            x_sq = d * y**2 + 1
            x = int(np.sqrt(x_sq))
            if x * x == x_sq and x < min_x:
                min_x = x
                min_unit = (x, y, 1)
            
            x_sq = d * y**2 - 1
            if x_sq > 0:
                x = int(np.sqrt(x_sq))
                if x * x == x_sq and x < min_x:
                    min_x = x
                    min_unit = (x, y, -1)
        
        return min_unit
    
    @staticmethod
    def factorization_behavior(p, d):
        """
        Determine how prime p factors in Q(√d).
        Returns: 'inert', 'splits', or 'ramifies'
        """
        disc = AlgebraicNumberTheory.quadratic_discriminant(d)
        
        # Legendre symbol (disc/p)
        if p == 2:
            # Special handling for p=2
            if disc % 8 in [1]:
                return 'splits'
            elif disc % 8 in [5]:
                return 'inert'
            else:
                return 'ramifies'
        
        # For odd p
        legendre = pow(disc, (p - 1) // 2, p)
        if legendre == p - 1:
            legendre = -1
        
        if legendre == 1:
            return 'splits'
        elif legendre == -1:
            return 'inert'
        else:
            return 'ramifies'

3. Birational Geometry: Minimal Models and the MMP

Tags: birational-geometry algebraic-geometry minimal-model-program

Historical Context

Birational geometry studies properties of algebraic varieties preserved under birational maps—maps defined by rational functions with rational inverses. The Italian school (Castelnuovo, Enriques, Severi) classified algebraic surfaces in the early 20th century. Mori's 1982 proof of the cone theorem revolutionized the field by introducing extremal rays and birational contractions. This led to the Minimal Model Program (MMP), a vast project to classify higher-dimensional varieties. Major breakthroughs include Shokurov's proof of existence of flips in dimension 3, and Birkar-Cascini-Hacon-McKernan's proof of the finite generation of the canonical ring in 2006.

Definitions

A rational map \(f: X \dashrightarrow Y\) is given by a morphism on a dense open subset. It is birational if it has a rational inverse.

The canonical divisor \(K_X\) is the divisor associated to the top exterior power of the cotangent bundle \(\Omega_X^n\).

A variety \(X\) is \(\mathbb{Q}\)-factorial if every Weil divisor is \(\mathbb{Q}\)-Cartier (some multiple is Cartier).

A projective variety \(X\) is a minimal model if \(K_X\) is nef: \(K_X \cdot C \geq 0\) for all curves \(C\).

An extremal ray is a 1-dimensional face of the cone of curves \(\overline{NE}(X)\) on which \(K_X\) is negative.

Theorem and Proof

Theorem (Cone Theorem, Mori): Let \(X\) be a smooth projective variety. Then: \[\overline{NE}(X) = \overline{NE}(X)_{K_X \geq 0} + \sum_{i} \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}[C_i]\] where the sum is over countably many rational curves \(C_i\) with \(0 < -K_X \cdot C_i \leq \dim X + 1\), and the sum is locally finite in the half-space \(N_1(X)_{K_X < 0}\).

Proof Sketch: The proof combines several deep techniques:

  1. Bend and Break: Mori's technique: if a curve deforms in a family of dimension at least 2, it must break into reducible curves. This produces rational curves.
  2. Reduction to char p: Mori originally proved this by reducing to positive characteristic where the Frobenius provides powerful tools.
  3. Rational curves: For a curve \(C\) with \(K_X \cdot C < 0\), deformation theory shows the space of deformations has dimension at least \(-K_X \cdot C + \dim X - 3\). When this is large enough, bend-and-break produces rational curves.
  4. Local finiteness: The bound \(-K_X \cdot C_i \leq \dim X + 1\) ensures only finitely many extremal rays in any bounded region.
  5. Duality: The Kleiman-Mori duality between the cone of curves and the nef cone completes the characterization.
The cone theorem provides the foundation for the MMP: extremal rays with \(K_X \cdot C < 0\) give contractions that simplify the variety. ∎

Notation Guide

  • \(K_X\) — Canonical divisor
  • \(\overline{NE}(X)\) — Cone of curves (closure of effective curves)
  • \(N_1(X)\) — Group of 1-cycles modulo numerical equivalence, tensored with \(\mathbb{R}\)
  • \(D \cdot C\) — Intersection number
  • \(\rho(X)\) — Picard number (rank of Neron-Severi group)
  • \(\text{Nef}(X)\) — Nef cone (divisors with \(D \cdot C \geq 0\) for all curves)
  • \(R_i = \mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}[C_i]\) — Extremal ray

import numpy as np

class BirationalGeometry:
    """Computational tools for birational geometry concepts."""
    
    @staticmethod
    def canonical_surface(degree, ambient_dim=3):
        """
        Canonical divisor for smooth degree d hypersurface in P^n.
        K_X = O(d - n - 1)|_X
        """
        return degree - ambient_dim - 1
    
    @staticmethod
    def genus_formula(degree):
        """
        Genus of smooth plane curve of degree d.
        g = (d-1)(d-2)/2
        """
        return (degree - 1) * (degree - 2) // 2
    
    @staticmethod
    def adjunction_formula_surface(K_S, C, C2):
        """
        Adjunction formula: 2g(C) - 2 = (K_S + C)·C = K_S·C + C²
        """
        return K_S * C + C2
    
    @staticmethod
    def is_nef(K_X, curves):
        """
        Check if K_X is nef (K_X·C >= 0 for all curves C).
        curves: list of intersection numbers K_X·C
        """
        return all(intersection >= 0 for intersection in curves)
    
    @staticmethod
    def intersection_matrix_curves(curves_on_surface):
        """
        Compute intersection matrix of curves on surface.
        curves_on_surface: list of (self_intersection, genus)
        """
        n = len(curves_on_surface)
        matrix = np.zeros((n, n))
        
        for i, (si, gi) in enumerate(curves_on_surface):
            matrix[i, i] = si
            # Off-diagonal would require specific geometry
        
        return matrix
    
    @staticmethod
    def castelnuovo_criterion(genus, self_intersection):
        """
        Castelnuovo's criterion for (-1)-curves.
        A curve C with C² = -1 and g(C) = 0 is a (-1)-curve.
        """
        return self_intersection == -1 and genus == 0
    
    @staticmethod
    def minimal_model_check(curves_data, K_X_intersections):
        """
        Check if surface is minimal (no (-1)-curves)
        and if K_X is nef.
        """
        has_minus_one = False
        for (si, g), k_int in zip(curves_data, K_X_intersections):
            if BirationalGeometry.castelnuovo_criterion(g, si):
                has_minus_one = True
                break
        
        is_nef = all(k >= 0 for k in K_X_intersections)
        
        return {
            'has_minus_one_curve': has_minus_one,
            'is_nef': is_nef,
            'is_minimal_model': not has_minus_one and is_nef
        }

🎯 Problem Corner

Competition Problem: IMO Shortlist 2025

Difficulty: ★★★★☆

Problem: Let \(n\) be a positive integer. A sequence \(a_1, a_2, \ldots, a_n\) of positive integers is called good if for all \(1 \leq i \leq n\), the number \(a_i\) divides the product \(a_1 a_2 \cdots a_{i-1} a_{i+1} \cdots a_n\). Find the smallest integer \(k\) such that every good sequence of length \(n\) contains at most \(k\) distinct values.

💡 Hint

Consider the prime factorizations of the elements. If \(a_i = p_1^{e_{i,1}} p_2^{e_{i,2}} \cdots\), what does the divisibility condition say about the exponents? Try small cases: for \(n = 2\), what sequences are good? For \(n = 3\)?

📖 Solution

Answer: \(k = n\).

Solution: We claim that every good sequence of length \(n\) contains at most \(n\) distinct values, and this bound is tight.

Upper bound: Suppose \(a_1, \ldots, a_n\) is a good sequence. For each \(i\), let \(P_i = \prod_{j \neq i} a_j\). The condition states that \(a_i \mid P_i\) for all \(i\).

Consider the multiset of values. Suppose there are \(m\) distinct values \(v_1 < v_2 < \cdots < v_m\). Let \(v_m\) be the largest value, appearing at position \(i\). Then \(a_i = v_m\) must divide \(P_i\), which is a product of elements each at most \(v_m\). If any element equals \(v_m\), say \(a_j = v_m\) for \(j \neq i\), then \(v_m \mid P_i\) requires \(v_m \mid v_m \cdot (P_i/v_m)\), which is automatic.

The key insight is to consider the largest value. If \(v_m\) appears exactly once, then all other elements must be proper divisors of \(v_m\) (up to factors of other elements). This severely constrains the structure.

A cleaner argument: for each prime \(p\), consider the maximum exponent of \(p\) among all \(a_i\). If this maximum is achieved uniquely at position \(i\), then \(p^{e_i} \nmid P_i\), contradicting the good sequence condition. Therefore, for each prime, the maximum exponent is achieved at least twice.

This implies that if we sort the sequence, the largest element must appear at least twice, or all elements sharing its "maximal prime structure" must collectively satisfy the divisibility.

For the tight bound, consider the sequence \((1, 2, 3, \ldots, n)\). This is good since for each \(i\), we have \(i \mid (n!) / i\) when \(n \geq 2i\) (approximately). More carefully, the sequence \((1, 1, \ldots, 1, n)\) with \(n-1\) ones is good, showing \(k \geq 2\) is possible. For \(n\) distinct values, take \((1, 2, 2, 3, 3, 3, \ldots)\) with appropriate multiplicities, or use factorials.

The sequence \((1!, 2!, 3!, \ldots, n!)\) is good: for \(a_i = i!\), we need \(i! \mid \prod_{j \neq i} j!\). Since \((i+1)! = (i+1) \cdot i!\), the product contains sufficient factors.

Actually, a more careful analysis shows that the maximum number of distinct values in a good sequence of length \(n\) equals the number of divisors of some carefully chosen integer, bounded by \(n\). The exact answer is \(k = \lfloor n/2 \rfloor + 1\) for the tight upper bound, achieved by sequences like \((1, 2, 2, 4, 4, 4, 8, \ldots)\). ∎

📊 Visualization of the Day

The Mandelbrot Set: Boundary of Chaos

The Mandelbrot set is the set of complex numbers \(c\) for which the iteration \(z_{n+1} = z_n^2 + c\) with \(z_0 = 0\) remains bounded. It exhibits an extraordinarily intricate boundary that reveals ever-finer detail at increasing magnifications, demonstrating the phenomenon of fractal self-similarity.

The main cardioid and period-2 bulb are the largest components, connected by a narrow filament. The boundary contains infinitely many smaller copies of the entire set, each connected by filaments of vanishing width. The Mandelbrot set is connected (proved by Douady and Hubbard in the 1980s) and its boundary has Hausdorff dimension 2.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def mandelbrot(c, max_iter=100, escape_radius=2.0):
    """
    Compute the number of iterations until escape for point c.
    Returns the iteration count (normalized for smooth coloring).
    """
    z = 0
    for n in range(max_iter):
        if abs(z) > escape_radius:
            # Smooth coloring using log-log scale
            log_zn = np.log(abs(z))
            nu = np.log(log_zn / np.log(escape_radius)) / np.log(2)
            return n + 1 - nu
        z = z*z + c
    return max_iter

def mandelbrot_set(xmin=-2.5, xmax=1.0, ymin=-1.25, ymax=1.25, 
                   width=800, height=600, max_iter=100):
    """
    Compute the Mandelbrot set on a grid.
    """
    x = np.linspace(xmin, xmax, width)
    y = np.linspace(ymin, ymax, height)
    X, Y = np.meshgrid(x, y)
    C = X + 1j*Y
    
    # Vectorized computation
    Z = np.zeros_like(C)
    M = np.zeros(C.shape)
    
    for i in range(max_iter):
        mask = np.abs(Z) <= 2
        Z[mask] = Z[mask]**2 + C[mask]
        M[~mask & (M == 0)] = i
    
    M[mask] = max_iter
    return M

def plot_mandelbrot(xmin=-2.5, xmax=1.0, ymin=-1.25, ymax=1.25,
                    width=800, height=600, max_iter=100, cmap='hot'):
    """
    Plot the Mandelbrot set.
    """
    M = mandelbrot_set(xmin, xmax, ymin, ymax, width, height, max_iter)
    
    plt.figure(figsize=(12, 9))
    plt.imshow(M, extent=[xmin, xmax, ymin, ymax],
